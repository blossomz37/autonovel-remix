## Chapter Three: The Smell of Base Note

She woke at the fourth hour, which she had been doing for weeks.

The ceiling was the ceiling. The room was the room. Adaeze lay on her back and read herself the way she read anyone — starting at the edges, working in — and found what she always found now: the thinness. Not pain. Not absence. Just the sensation of paper held to light, the slight translucence of something that should be more solid. She pressed two fingers against the left side of her ribcage, the checking gesture, and held them there for a moment before letting her hand drop to the mat.

She got up. She made tea.

The water took time to boil and she stood at the window while it did, watching the platform district come apart from the dark — the oil lamps going out one by one as the sky behind them grayed, the sound of the first boats already moving on the water below, someone calling out to someone else in the flat carrying voice that the channels made possible. The city waking up the way it always woke up. Ordinary. She wrapped both hands around the clay cup when it was ready and the heat moved through her palms and up into her wrists and she stood there until the tea was cool enough to drink and then drank it standing.

She had been standing in front of the shelf for some minutes before she realized she had moved.

The east wall. The ledger on it, cloth-covered, sitting between the small wooden box where she kept her certification stamps and the oil lamp she never used because the window gave enough light. Eight months it had sat there. The cloth cover was undyed — standard House issue, the same cover on every ledger she had ever handled at work, and she had handled hundreds, and not one of them had ever looked like this one looked right now, which was: like something that had been waiting.

She stood in front of it and the smell reached her.

She had been not-naming it for eight months. Shea butter and woodsmoke — her mother's cooking, her mother's hair, the particular combination that meant *home* the way sounds mean things before you learn the words for them. And underneath. The base note. She knew it the way she knew all base notes, from the inside of her training, from the hundred assessments she had done where she had leaned slightly forward over a desk and read the biological signature of a stranger's remaining count. She had read chest-fever patients, she had read the elderly, she had read a child once and written the number down and handed it to the child's mother and driven the image of the mother's face from her mind by the time she reached the street. She had read every kind of person. She had never read her mother, because you did not assess your own family — it was in the certification rules, it was also just something you did not do — and so she had never smelled this particular note, this specific biological signature, and she recognized it now with the full force of something that had been true for years and simply unconfirmed.

Her mother's count. Her mother's remaining years, recorded in the smell of the cloth.

Adaeze sat down on the floor. This was not a decision she made.

---

The ledger opened the way they all opened — stiff at the spine, the cloth cover releasing a concentrated breath of the smell as the pages fanned. She held it in her lap and looked at the first contract and her eyes moved over it with professional habit before the rest of her understood what she was reading.

*Contract of Temporal Lending, House of Adim, District Registry.*

*Primary borrower: Chidinma Mwenye. Occupation: cloth merchant. Natural yield at time of signing: assessed at thirty-one years. Collateral offered: the natural yield of named heir Adaeze Mwenye, currently aged four years, assessed yield pending maturation. Amount borrowed: eleven years, immediate disbursement. Purpose of loan: treatment and recovery, chest fever, acute presentation. Interest rate: standard variable. Experiential interest: to be assessed at time of collection.*

*Date of signing: the fourteenth day of the dry season, twenty-seven years prior.*

She read it twice. On the third reading she noticed that her hands had gone very still in her lap, the ledger balanced across her knees, the morning light from the window lying flat across the page. Her mother had been twenty-three. Thirty-one years of natural yield — young, then, young enough that the count was still high, young enough to be worth lending against. Chest fever, acute presentation. That phrase was in the clinical register, the Assessor's register, and she understood it clinically: her mother had been dying. Not slowly, not maybe. Dying.

She had been four.

She had not known.

What she had known, at four, was that her mother made bitter-leaf soup. That her mother's hands were always slightly warm. That her mother hummed while she worked at the cloth frames, a low sound, not quite a song. She had not known about the chest fever. She had not known about the contract. She had not known that the word *collateral* could contain a person — could contain a four-year-old, a child who was at that moment presumably asleep somewhere, unaware that her future had just been offered as security against her mother's survival.

*Assessed yield pending maturation.* She knew what that meant. She had written that phrase herself, on contracts where the named heir was a minor — it meant the House would wait, would conduct a full assessment when the child reached majority, would determine the actual collateral value once the count could be properly read. It was standard language. She had written it four times in the past year alone.

She had been four years old when someone wrote it about her.

Adaeze closed her eyes. Opened them. The contract was still there.

She turned to the second contract.

*Date of signing: the third day of the wet season, twenty-three years prior.*

Her mother had been twenty-seven. Four years after the first contract, four years after the chest fever, four years of borrowed time already spent. The purpose of the loan was listed in the same clinical shorthand: *business expansion, cloth trade, primary income source for household.* Amount borrowed: five years. Collateral: the same clause, updated. *Named heir Adaeze Mwenye, currently aged eight years, assessed yield pending maturation.*

Eight years old. She had been eight. She tried to place herself in that year and found she could: she had been in school, the building with the leaking roof two streets over from their house, the teacher who smelled of palm oil and chalk dust. She had learned to read that year. She had had new sandals — the kind with the leather strap across the toe, slightly too large so she could grow into them, and she had been proud of them in the specific way of eight-year-olds, wearing them even on days when the other children went barefoot because the platforms were warm from the sun.

New sandals. Her mother had borrowed five years of her future to buy her new sandals, or to keep the cloth trade running, or to keep the household intact, or all of these things at once, and she had been eight years old and she had not known, and she had worn those sandals until they fell apart.

*Business expansion, cloth trade, primary income source for household.* She read the phrase again. Her mother had not been dying this time. Her mother had been trying to build something. She had been trying to make the household stable, to make the income reliable, to give her daughter — the daughter whose future was already listed as collateral — a good life. Adaeze understood this. She understood it the way she understood a contract clause: completely, technically, with full comprehension of every word, and no ability at all to feel what she was supposed to feel about it.

She closed the ledger.

She sat with it in her lap.

The smell came up through the cloth cover, steady and specific — shea butter, woodsmoke, and underneath, the base note. Her mother's count, reading out at her from the pages. She had smelled this smell every morning for eight months and not opened the ledger, and she had told herself she was grieving, and she had been right that she was grieving, but she had been wrong about what for. She had thought she was grieving her mother. She had been grieving, without knowing it, the thing she was now reading.

Outside, the city continued. Boats on the water. The drum from last night was gone — whoever had been practicing had finished, or given up, or gone somewhere else. A woman's voice called a name from a nearby platform and was answered from further away, the call and response carrying over the channels with the particular clarity of early morning. Ordinary sounds. The world going about itself.

Adaeze sat on the floor of her main room with the ledger in her lap and her mother's smell coming up through the cloth and she was not crying. This was not composure. It was something older than composure, something she had learned in her first year of assessments when she had understood that the information would keep arriving and that the only way to receive it was to be still. An Assessor who moved while reading moved through the data instead of through it. You held still. You let the information settle. You waited until you could see the shape of it.

She was waiting for the shape of it.

Two contracts. Twenty-seven years ago and twenty-three years ago. Eleven years and five years. Her mother at twenty-three, dying. Her mother at twenty-seven, building. Her name in both of them, listed as collateral at four years old and at eight years old, her yield pending assessment, her future offered as security before she had been old enough to know the word for what was being done.

There were seven contracts in the ledger. She had counted the tabs before she opened it, the professional habit of knowing what you were working with before you read. She had read two. The ledger sat in her lap and smelled of her mother and the remaining five contracts sat unread inside it, and she understood, sitting on the floor in the early light, that she was not going to read them today. Not because she couldn't. Because she had just learned something that required being learned slowly, and she was still in the first minutes of knowing it, and the knowing was not finished with her yet.

Her mother had been dying. She had been four. Her mother had chosen — had sat across a desk from an Assessor at the House of Adim and had signed a contract and had offered her daughter's future as the price of her own survival. And then she had lived. And then she had borrowed again. And she had come home and made bitter-leaf soup every birthday, the soup you were supposed to eat when you signed a contract, the soup that meant *taste what you are agreeing to* — she had made that soup every year on Adaeze's birthday and Adaeze had eaten it and thought it was tradition and it had been, in a way that Adaeze was only now beginning to read, an apology. A recurring one. An apology that had to be made every year because the debt was still accruing and her mother had known and could not say.

The ledger was warm from her hands.

*Assessed yield pending maturation.* She had been four. She had been eight. She was thirty-one now and her mother was dead and the yield was no longer pending — it had been assessed, it was in the ledger somewhere in the five contracts she had not yet read, her count was written down in the House of Adim's records and in the Grand Ledger's master copy and in the marginal notations of whatever Assessor had finally sat across a desk from her and read her thinness and written down a number.

She pressed two fingers against the left side of her ribcage.

She had been reading herself as thin for two years. She had told herself it was stress. She had been falsifying her annual self-assessments, writing in the acceptable range, because to report it accurately would have required explaining it and she had not been ready to explain it. She had told herself she was not ready. She understood now that she had been not-ready in the specific way of someone who already knows the answer and is not prepared to have it confirmed.

The light moved on the floor. The boats kept moving. Somewhere a child was being called in for morning meal, the name repeated three times with increasing volume, and then the sound of small feet on the platform boards, running.

Adaeze sat on the floor and held the ledger and smelled her mother and did not move for a long time.

She was not crying. She was doing what Assessors did when they read something they needed to understand. She was being very still. She was letting the information settle into its shape. She was waiting to see what the shape was, because she could feel that she did not yet have the full measure of it — two contracts out of seven, eleven years and five years, and her name in both of them, and five more contracts still inside the cloth cover sitting in her lap.

The shape was going to be worse than this.

She knew the shape was going to be worse than this the way she knew a borrower's count before she had finished reading them — not yet confirmed, but present, legible in the texture of things. She was thirty-one years old. Her mother had borrowed against her yield before she could walk. Whatever the remaining five contracts contained, the arithmetic was already beginning, and she was already inside it, and had been since she was four years old and asleep somewhere while her mother sat across a desk and signed her name.

The bitter-leaf was still on the kitchen counter. She had not made the soup.

She held the ledger and breathed the smell of it and outside the city kept going, ordinary and relentless, and the morning came fully in through the window and lay across the floor and across her hands and across the cloth cover of the ledger, and she sat in it and was still, and waited for the knowing to finish arriving.
