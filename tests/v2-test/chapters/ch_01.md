# Chapter One: The Texture of a Tuesday

The fisherman's hands gave him away before anything else.

He had set them flat on the desk when he sat down, the way people do when they want to appear calm, but the skin across his knuckles had that particular thinness — not age, exactly, more like paper that had been wet and dried too many times. Adaeze read the rest of him in the same order she always did: face, throat, the way he held his shoulders. Nineteen years. Maybe twenty if the rains were kind and he stayed out of the deep water and didn't do whatever it was that had put that old compression scar along his left forearm.

She wrote *nineteen* in the certification box. Then she picked up the stamp.

"You came in wanting to borrow three years," she said.

"Yes." He had a fisherman's voice, low and used to carrying over water. "For the boat. The hull needs full replacement, not just patching, and if I wait another season—"

"I understand." She did. She had heard this particular shape of reason four hundred times, or close enough. "Three years against nineteen is within our standard lending parameters. The repayment schedule will run over eight years at the variable rate, with experiential interest assessed at time of collection." She slid the form across the desk. "You'll want to read the third clause. It specifies what categories of experience are eligible for interest collection."

He read it. He moved his lips slightly. Then he looked up.

"My count," he said. "Is it — is it good?"

Adaeze looked at him. Forty, she had estimated when he walked in, maybe forty-two. He had the kind of face that had spent its whole life outside, which made reading the density harder — the sun aged the surface of a person without touching what was underneath, and you had to learn to look past the leather of it to the thing the skin was covering. She had learned. Nineteen years was solid. Nineteen years was a man who would see his children grown and still have time left after.

"Your count is good," she said. "Nineteen years, give or take a season. You can take that certification to any House in the city."

The relief that moved across his face was almost painful to watch. Not joy, exactly. More like the specific loosening of a man who has been holding his breath for weeks and has finally been told he is allowed to stop. He thanked her with both hands pressed together, a gesture she associated with her grandmother's generation, and he took the stamped form and he left.

Adaeze set the stamp back in its holder.

She pulled the next file.

---

She walked home the long way, which she told herself was habit and which was actually a small daily refusal to take the covered walkway the House had built for its Assessors two years ago. The walkway was faster. It also smelled powerfully of contract ink, and after eight hours of assessments she had had enough of that particular smell.

The Adim district in late afternoon had its own specific quality of light — the sun coming at a low angle across the tidal flat, hitting the water between the platform supports and bouncing up to catch the undersides of everything, so that the whole neighborhood seemed lit from below. The boats knocked against their pilings with a sound like someone working a loose tooth. Children were playing count-and-run in the narrow passage between the dyer's shop and the grain merchant's, the caller's voice carrying clearly across the water: *twelve, eleven, ten* — the tagged child running, the others counting down with the particular glee of children who understand a game is about something without knowing what. Somewhere to the east, someone was burning palm kernel. The smell of it moved through the cooking-fish and river-clay smell of the district like a thread.

Adaeze moved through all of it without stopping. She had been moving through this particular combination of sounds and smells for thirty-one years. The boats knocked. The children counted. The palm kernel burned. She knew it the way she knew the layout of her own rooms — not consciously, just as the shape of normal, the background condition of being here.

The old women were still outside the House when she passed it on her way back. There were always three of them in the morning, sometimes four in the afternoon. They sat on the low bench that had been there as long as Adaeze could remember, older than the current bench certainly, probably older than the House's current facade, which had been replastered twice in Adaeze's lifetime. They did not come to borrow. They came to sit near it, the way her grandmother had always said the old people sat near the river — not to cross it, just to be close to the thing that carried everything.

One of them looked up as Adaeze passed.

"How is your count?" The standard greeting, delivered in the standard warm tone, the opening of a conversation that never needed to go further than its own formula.

"My count is good," Adaeze said.

She had been saying this for two years.

Her left hand touched the side of her ribcage as she said it — a quick press, two fingers against the lower ribs, and gone. She was already three steps past the old women before she registered that she had done it.

---

The case files she had brought home were about a family in the northern section of the flat — third-generation account, grandmother had borrowed in the Dry Decade, mother had borrowed to service that interest, and now the daughter was thirty-two and the compound had grown to something that made Adaeze's eyes move to the top of the page twice before she believed the number. She stood at the kitchen table and ate pepper soup from the pot she had made on Sunday, reading. The soup had gone slightly sour. She ate it anyway, turning pages with her free hand, tracing the interest calculations with one finger the way she had learned to do in training, following the logic of the numbers the way you follow a river upstream to find where the water started.

The ledger was on the shelf above her desk.

She could see it from where she stood.

It was an ordinary ledger, to look at — cloth-bound, the cover faded from whatever color it had started as to a grey-brown that could have been anything. It sat between a volume of the Orokwe Accords commentary she had bought secondhand and a clay cup she used to hold pens. She had put it there eight months ago because she had needed to put it somewhere and the shelf was where things went when she didn't know what to do with them yet. She had been stepping around the fact of it the way you step around a piece of furniture you have decided to move — acknowledging it only in the peripheral way, never quite looking at it directly, because looking at it directly would require her to either open it or actively decide not to, and she had not been ready for either.

She turned a page in the case file.

The ledger sat on the shelf.

She finished the soup, washed the bowl, put the case files in her bag for the morning. She changed the lamp wick because it had been guttering for a week. She found a loose button on her work jacket and sewed it back on, sitting on the edge of the bed with the jacket across her knees and the needle moving in and out of the cloth in the small, precise stitches her mother had taught her. Her mother had been very particular about buttons. *A loose button is a question you haven't answered yet*, she used to say, which was the kind of thing her mother said, the kind of thing that had seemed like wisdom when Adaeze was a child and seemed, now, like a woman who had organized her entire inner life around the language of small repairs.

She put the jacket away.

She got into bed.

In the dark, with the sound of the water moving under the platforms and a boat somewhere knocking its slow knock, she read herself.

It was involuntary. She had tried, early in her training, to turn it off — to look at a person and simply see a person, without the density reading starting up beneath the looking, the way a musician cannot hear a song without also hearing whether it is in tune. Her instructor had told her not to bother trying. *The gift is not a switch*, the woman had said. *It is more like a heartbeat. You can ignore it. You cannot stop it.* So Adaeze had learned to ignore it, more or less, except in the dark, lying still, when the ignoring required more effort than she had left.

She read herself.

The same result she had been getting for two years. Thin. The Assessor's word for a count that was running low, that had the particular quality of light through paper rather than light through cloth — present, but with the sense of something behind it showing through. She had attributed this, for two years, to stress. To the accumulated weight of eight hours a day of other people's counts. To grief, after her mother. To the ordinary exhaustion of a person who ate standing up and slept six hours and had been carrying a sealed ledger on her conscience for the better part of a year.

Thin, she thought. Just stress.

Outside, the boat knocked against its piling. Once, twice.

She lay in the dark and did not open the ledger, which was on the shelf above her desk, which was in the next room, which she could not see from where she was lying but which she was aware of the way you are aware of a sound that has stopped — not hearing it, just conscious of the space where it was.

Nineteen years, she had told the fisherman. Your count is good.

She pressed two fingers against the left side of her ribcage.

She counted her own breath in the dark — one, two, three — and eventually she slept.

---

In the morning she woke before the lamp needed lighting, dressed in the grey that was her work color, and stood for a moment in the doorway between the bedroom and the main room.

The ledger was on the shelf.

It smelled, faintly, in the way that all ledgers smelled — the cloth cover, the particular ink the Houses used, and underneath both of those the base note, the biological signature that clung to a ledger the way a person's smell clung to their clothes. She had noticed this smell eight months ago when she had set it on the shelf. She had been noticing it every morning since without allowing herself to name what it was, exactly, that she was smelling.

She knew what she was smelling.

She put on her jacket — the button firm now, sewn back properly — and picked up her bag and went to work.

The fisherman's form would be in the processing queue by now. Three years, disbursed immediately, against a count of nineteen. A reasonable ask. A stamped approval. A man walking out with the particular relief of someone who has been told he is not yet running out.

She walked the long way to the House, past the old women's bench, which was empty this early, past the children's passage where the count-and-run game had left a chalk mark on the wall — *seven* — past the smell of the palm kernel burning somewhere east, which was already burning again or had never stopped.

*My count is good.*

Two fingers against the left side of her ribcage, quick and gone, the gesture completing itself before she noticed she had started it.

She went inside.
