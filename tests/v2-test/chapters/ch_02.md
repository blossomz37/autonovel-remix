## Chapter 2: What the Borrowers Know

The woman across the desk was named Tessie Ibori. She had come in wearing her work clothes — the indigo-stained fingers were still indigo-stained, which meant she had come straight from the loom, which meant she had not wanted to wait another day to do this. She sat with her hands in her lap and her back very straight, the posture of someone who had rehearsed being here.

Adaeze let the silence run for a moment. Beside her, Amara had her notebook open.

"You want to borrow two years," Adaeze said. "Against the business."

"The dry season's been bad three years running." Tessie Ibori's voice was steady. She had also rehearsed this. "My buyers are still there. I just need to hold through until the rains bring the market back. Two years, and I can pay it down in installments — I've worked out the schedule, I can show you—"

"I don't need the schedule yet." Adaeze looked at her. Not at her eyes — Assessors who looked at eyes were reading emotion, and emotion was not the work. She looked at the quality of the woman's presence, the density of her, the way she occupied the chair. Some people sat in chairs as if the chair were already slightly too large for them. Tessie Ibori sat in the chair as if she had sat in many chairs in her life and expected to sit in many more. Thirty-six years, maybe thirty-seven. Adaeze let the number settle. Thirty-six. Count of eleven, at least — probably eleven and a half, given the texture of her. Good collateral for a two-year ask. Very good.

She looked down at the form.

Beside her, she felt Amara writing something.

"Give me a moment," Adaeze said, and she went through the standard assessment — the questions about existing contracts, about named heirs, about the business's registration with the city trade office. Tessie Ibori answered each one promptly and honestly, which Adaeze could tell because Tessie Ibori was a bad liar and had not needed to lie once. The business was clean. The count was good. The ask was reasonable. Adaeze stamped the form.

"Your loan is approved. Two years, disbursed in installments against the repayment schedule you've prepared — bring that in when you come to sign the full contract, it'll be attached as an exhibit." She turned the form around. "You'll sign here, and your wit will sign here. Do you have someone to bring?"

"My sister." Tessie Ibori let out a breath that had been sitting in her chest for some time. "My sister can come. When — is tomorrow too soon?"

"Tomorrow is fine."

The woman stood, and there was a moment — Adaeze had seen it four hundred times — where the relief was so complete it looked almost like grief. The particular loosening of a person who had been braced for a different answer.

"Thank you," Tessie Ibori said. "Thank you, really."

"The contract will be ready at the second bell," Adaeze said, and she meant it kindly, and she was already looking at the next form in the queue.

---

Amara waited until the door had closed.

"Thirty-six," she said. It was not a question. She had written something in her notebook and was looking at it.

"Yes."

"I read her as forty." Amara pressed her pen against the page. "I was four years off. I know I was four years off — I could feel I was wrong when you started reading, something shifted — but I don't know what I was missing."

"You were reading her face."

"Isn't that—"

"It's part of it." Adaeze capped her stamp and set it back in its tray. "Her face reads older. She's been working in indigo since she was a girl — the dye does something to the skin around the eyes. You were reading the work, not the person." She paused. "Try the hands next time. And the way someone breathes when they're sitting still. The face lies. The body's harder to lie with."

Amara wrote this down. She wrote everything down, which Adaeze found either admirable or exhausting depending on the day.

"She didn't ask," Amara said.

Adaeze looked at her.

"Her count. You certified it — thirty-six, eleven years — but you didn't tell her. She didn't ask, and you didn't say."

"Borrowers are entitled to their certified count on request." Adaeze straightened the forms on her desk. "The House doesn't volunteer it unless asked. That's standard practice."

"Why?"

The question was simple and Amara had asked it the way a nineteen-year-old asked things — not as a challenge, just as a genuine gap in understanding that she expected to be filled. Adaeze had been asked this question before, by other trainees, and she had answered it the same way each time and the answer had always satisfied them.

"The count can be distressing information," she said. "Particularly for borrowers who haven't thought carefully about their remaining yield. The House's position is that an unsolicited count can cause harm — anxiety, poor financial decisions, family conflict. The Accords provide for disclosure on request. The borrower can ask at any point during the assessment or afterward, and we are required to answer honestly."

Amara was writing this down. She wrote *disclosure on request* and underlined it, and then she stopped writing and looked at the door where Tessie Ibori had gone.

"But she didn't know to ask," Amara said.

Adaeze picked up the next form in the queue.

The form was for a man in the Tidal Quarter, a fisherman — not the fisherman from yesterday, a different one, there were always more fishermen — wanting to borrow eighteen months against a count of fourteen years. Reasonable. She read the preliminary assessment notes and found them accurate.

"The policy is the policy," she said. Her voice came out the way she had trained it to come out: even, neither warm nor cold. "The Accords provide the right. The borrower exercises it or doesn't."

Amara did not argue. She wrote something else in her notebook, and Adaeze did not look to see what.

---

Obi Adim-Eze's office was at the back of the House, past the processing room and the small archive where the current-year ledgers were shelved. His door was always open during working hours — a policy he had maintained for twenty years, which Adaeze understood as both genuine accessibility and a way of making it impossible for anyone to feel they were having a private conversation with him. You always knew someone in the processing room could hear.

He was at his desk when she knocked on the frame. He looked up, and something in his face arranged itself carefully.

"Adaeze. Come in. Sit."

She sat. His desk had a brass paperweight, a small carved figure that might have been a bird, a cup of pens, and a stack of forms. Everything was very straight. She had worked in this House for nine years and his desk had always been very straight.

"How is Amara doing?" he asked.

"She reads faces. I'm working on it. She'll be good."

"Good." He moved the brass paperweight slightly, then moved it back. "I wanted to check in with you. About the Mwenye inheritance."

He always called it the Mwenye inheritance. Not *your mother's ledger*, not *the account*. The Mwenye inheritance, as if it were a case in the queue and she were the Assessor assigned to it.

"I haven't opened it yet," she said.

"I know." He said this without any particular emphasis, which meant he had known and had been waiting to say it. "The sixty-day review period is still available to you. You received the ledger eight months ago, but the formal review period begins at opening — you have sixty days from the day you read the first contract, not from delivery. You don't have to—" He stopped. He straightened the forms on his desk. "There's no requirement that you act immediately."

"I'm aware of the review period."

"Of course." He looked at the cup of pens. "Would you like tea? I was going to ask Emeka to—"

"No."

A pause. Outside, in the processing room, someone was reading a contract aloud — the flat, rapid delivery that the Houses had developed two hundred years ago, technically fulfilling the requirement, functionally impenetrable. Adaeze had learned to stop hearing it. She heard it now.

"Is there something I should know," she said, "before I open it?"

Obi looked at her. He was fifty-eight years old and she had been working for him since she was twenty-two and she knew his face the way she knew the faces of people she had assessed many times — not the count, she had never assessed him, she had made a private rule against it — but the expressions, the tells. He was about to not tell her something. His eyes moved to the open door.

"I don't have specific information about your mother's account," he said. "I wouldn't have access to the details of a personal inheritance."

"That's not what I asked."

He was quiet for a moment. He picked up the brass paperweight and set it down.

"In my experience," he said, "the ledgers that are hardest to read are the ones where the borrower loved someone very much."

Adaeze looked at him. He was looking at his desk.

She stood. "Thank you for the tea offer."

"Adaeze."

She stopped in the doorway.

"The review period," he said. "Sixty days. Use them."

She went back through the processing room, past the trainee still reading the contract aloud, past the archive with its smell of cloth covers and ink, and she picked up her bag from her desk and told Amara she was going to be out for the afternoon and that the Tidal Quarter fisherman's assessment was approved and the form just needed filing.

She went outside into the market.

---

The Adim market was loudest in the middle hours, when the morning buyers had gone and the afternoon buyers had not yet arrived and the vendors had time to talk to each other. Adaeze moved through it without stopping, past the cloth stalls and the pepper sellers and the woman who sold palm wine from clay jugs she sealed with banana leaves. Past the old women's bench, which had four women on it today, sitting in the particular companionable silence of people who had been sitting together long enough that they didn't need to fill it.

She stopped at a vegetable stall near the eastern platform.

The woman behind it was small and efficient and selling a dozen things at once, and Adaeze looked at the bundles of leaves arranged on the flat table and pointed at the bitter-leaf without knowing she was going to.

"How much."

"Two coppers for the bundle."

She paid. The woman wrapped it in banana leaf and tied it with a strip of the same, and when she held it out the smell came through the wrapping — green and specific and wrong in a way Adaeze could not name. Not bad. Just wrong. The smell of something in the wrong context, like hearing a song from childhood in a building you have never been in before.

She stood at the stall for a moment after the transaction was finished. The vendor had already moved on to the next customer. Adaeze was holding a bundle of bitter-leaf she had no plan to cook.

She walked home.

Her apartment was on the second floor of a building at the edge of the reclaimed silt, where the platform district met the older ground. The stairs creaked at the fourth step, which she had stopped noticing years ago and noticed now. She unlocked the door and went inside and set her bag down and stood in the main room with the bitter-leaf still in her hand.

The shelf was on the east wall. The ledger was on the shelf.

Eight months, and the cloth cover had not changed. The particular ink the Houses used had not changed. The smell — she was aware of it now, standing in the room, the smell she had been not-naming every morning for eight months — had not changed. It had never changed. It would not change regardless of how long she waited.

She went into the kitchen.

She set the bitter-leaf on the counter and looked at it. Two coppers. Bundle of leaves wrapped in banana leaf, tied with a strip of the same. She could unwrap it and put water on and start the broth. She had made this soup before — her mother had made it every year, on her birthday, and she had watched enough times to know the steps. The bitter-leaf went in last, after the stock, after the palm oil, just long enough to soften but not long enough to lose the bite. You were supposed to taste what you were agreeing to. That was the point.

She stood in the kitchen with her hands at her sides.

She did not unwrap the bitter-leaf. She did not put water on. She stood in the kitchen while the light moved on the counter and the smell of the leaves came through the banana wrapping and somewhere outside someone was playing a drum, a practice rhythm, the same six beats repeated and repeated and then stopping and then beginning again.

*But she didn't know to ask.*

Adaeze pressed two fingers against the left side of her ribcage, the checking gesture, quick and automatic and gone before she had finished making it.

The bitter-leaf sat on the counter. The ledger was on the shelf in the other room. The drum outside started its six beats again and this time did not stop.

She stood there for a long time.
