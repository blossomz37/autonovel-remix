  1. **Quiet chapter.** Adaeze walks to the school where her mother taught. It is early morning, before the children arrive. She stands outside the gate. Collected."* And then, at the bottom of the page, in Amina's handwriting, one word: *"Enough."* Adaeze does not know if her mother meant *I have borrowed enough* or *she has enough now* or *I am done.* She thinks it might be all three. She gave me the drawing of the river. She gave me enough.* She opens her notebook. She writes: *"What I have: the photographs. Kofi's notes. The ledger. The founding document. The names of the heirs who have not been notified. Approximately eighteen months. The complete record of what was done and to whom."* She underlines *the complete record.* She thinks: *the system requires silence to function. The question is what happens when the record is outside.*

- **Beats:**
  1. Adaeze goes to Kofi. He is at home, suspended, eating too much fish. The apartment is warm and slightly chaotic — papers on the floor, his sash hanging off the back of a chair. The fisherman's eleven years are gone. The weaving woman's cooperative failed anyway."* She pauses. *"I know what this doesn't fix."* Kofi says: *"I know you know."* He adjusts his sash — it is still off-center, as it has always been — and she reaches over and straightens it, as she has always done, and they both understand this is a farewell gesture and neither of them says so. She says: *"Let's start with the first name on the list."*

  - The plan's three parts as a structural map for Act III (Ch 21: notify the heirs; Ch 22: present the record to the tribunal; Ch 23: aftermath)

  - *"That's not the same as being free"* / *"No. But it's alive"* — the novel's revised stakes, stated plainly; survival without full justice is still survival

- **Payoffs:** Kofi's number-discrepancy observation from Ch 2 pays off fully — he has known about her rounding errors for eighteen months and chose to be her ally anyway; this is why. The sash-adjustment ritual from Ch 2 pays off as a farewell gesture. ### Ch 22: The Elders' Accord
- **POV:** Adaeze
- **Location:** The Elders' Accord tribunal chamber; the antechamber where the eleven heirs wait; the steps outside afterward
- **Save the Cat beat:** Finale (The High Tower Surprise / Climax)
- **% mark:** 81–88%
- **Emotional arc:** Controlled determination → the vertigo of the tribunal proceeding → the specific, strange calm of having done the thing and not yet knowing if it worked
- **Try-fail cycle:** Yes-but (the tribunal accepts the founding document and opens a formal review — but the review will take months, and Yewande immediately files a counter-motion that will delay proceedings; the sequencing is disrupted but not destroyed; Adaeze's debt is contested but not resolved)
- **Beats:**
  1. The tribunal chamber. The eleven heirs sit in the gallery — the fisherman's daughter, the retired schoolteacher, the dock clerk, Sena, and seven others. Kofi is not present; he cannot be, as a suspended employee. He is outside, on the steps, eating fish from a paper wrapper. Adaeze stands before the tribunal with the photographs, the founding document, the ledger, and the complete record. She has prepared her presentation with the same precision she brings to assessments. She is precise. She is thorough. She is also, for the first time in the novel, speaking in her own voice rather than the professional voice — she presents the evidence, and then she says: *"My mother was twenty-three years old and she had a four-year-old who could not yet read. She walked to the Ledger House the same afternoon she was told she had eighteen months. She signed. She loved me. These two things are both true. The system she signed with knew both things would be true, and it built its architecture on that knowledge, and it has been building on it for four generations. I am asking this tribunal to look at what was built."*
  2. The tribunal looks. The founding document is authenticated — the tribunal has its own archivists, independent of the Ledger Houses. The simultaneous petitions from eleven heirs with founding-sequence standing create the procedural backlog Adaeze calculated. The tribunal opens a formal review. This is not a verdict. It is a process. It will take months. But it is a process that the Ledger Houses cannot control, because the Elders' Accord tribunal has founding-era jurisdiction and the founding document is now in evidence.
  3. Yewande appears. She was not invited; she has the right to attend as Senior Ledger-Master. She sits in the observer's gallery and watches the proceedings with the same patient certainty she has brought to every interaction with Adaeze. When the tribunal opens the review, she stands and files a counter-motion: the founding document was accessed through unauthorized means and its chain of custody is compromised. The tribunal takes the motion under advisement. The review is not stopped, but it is complicated. Adaeze looks at Yewande across the chamber. Yewande looks back. Adaeze tries, one more time, to read her — to find the number, to see through the river water. And this time, for the first time, she gets something: not a number, but a quality. She thinks about her mother teaching her to swim, patient, laughing, holding her up in the water. She thinks about the fact that her mother was robbed of the memory of it, and that she has been carrying it for both of them, and that she will carry it for the rest of her life — however long that is. She thinks about the fisherman's daughter: *"Then we'll carry it."* She thinks about *"Enough."* She thinks: *yes. Enough. This is enough. I am here. I am at the river. I have the drawing. I have the record. She does not need a festival to stand at the river. She stands at the river. The water moves. The amber light comes through the mangroves. She is thirty-two years old. She does not know how long she has. She knows what she has done with what she was given. She thinks: *"Enough."* She means all three things at once.
