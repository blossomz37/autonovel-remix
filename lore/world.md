# THE ACCOUNTANCY OF GRIEF — WORLD BIBLE

---

## Cosmology & History

### The Founding Myth

Before Varanthe was a city it was a problem. The delta floods twice yearly, the dry season kills what the floods don't drown, and the people who settled the mangrove margins were the people no one else wanted — fishing communities, escaped bondspeople, traders too small for the northern caravan routes. The founding myth says that a woman named Nne Oru made a bargain with the river: she would give it her remaining years in exchange for one good harvest, enough to survive until the rains. The river, or something wearing the river's face, accepted. She lived three more months. The harvest lasted the settlement through two dry seasons. Her children built the first Ledger House in her name, or so the story goes. The Ledger Houses have been promoting this story for four hundred years. Its moral shifts depending on who's telling it: *sacrifice sustains community*, or *read the contract before you sign*.

### Timeline of Tensions

**~400 years ago — The Founding Compact:** The original five Ledger Houses are established under the Orokwe Accords, a document that frames temporal lending as a public utility. Each House is assigned a district and a clientele. The system is presented as mutual: the Houses need human time to function (the documents never explain why), and humans need more time than their bodies allow. The Accords include a Jubilee clause — every fifty years, all outstanding debts are to be audited and debts held by the poorest quarter of borrowers forgiven. This clause has never been invoked.

**~300 years ago — The Consolidation:** Three of the original five Houses merge into what is now the Grand Ledger, the dominant institution. The remaining two Houses — the House of Mwenye-Tala (now defunct) and the House of Adim, where Adaeze works — become "minor Houses," serving borrowers the Grand Ledger considers poor collateral risks. In practice: the desperate, the sick, the people borrowing against their children.

**~200 years ago — The Ikechi Reforms:** A reformist city-governor named Ikechi Eze mandates that all debt contracts be read aloud to borrowers before signing, in the borrower's primary language, with a waiting period of three days. The Grand Ledger complies by developing a reading style — rapid, flat, legally complete — that renders comprehension nearly impossible. The reforms remain on the books. They are technically honored. They change nothing.

**~80 years ago — The Dry Decade:** Ten consecutive failed rainy seasons. The worst food scarcity in recorded Varanthe history. The Ledger Houses' lending volume triples. An entire generation borrows against their children's time to survive. This is the generation of Adaeze's grandparents. The compound interest from the Dry Decade is still accruing. Many of the families in Adaeze's district are third-generation debtors without knowing it.

**~40 years ago — The Assessor Certification Act:** The city government, under pressure from the Grand Ledger, formalizes the Assessor profession. Assessors must be licensed, trained in a Grand Ledger-approved curriculum, and bonded to a House. The Act frames this as consumer protection. It also means that independent temporal assessors — people who could give borrowers an honest accounting of their collateral — are now illegal. The only people who can tell you how long you have left are employed by the institutions lending against that time.

**2 years ago — Adaeze's mother dies.** Her name was Chidinma Mwenye. She was fifty-four. The official cause of death is a chest illness that had been treated, managed, and survived for eleven years — years that, Adaeze will discover, were borrowed time. The ledger is delivered to Adaeze's door three weeks after the funeral, per standard inheritance law. Adaeze sets it on a shelf without opening it for eight months. This is not unusual. Most heirs wait.

**Present day:** Adaeze opens the ledger.

## Magic System

### Hard Rules: Temporal Lending

**What time is, here:** Time in Varanthe is not metaphysical. It is biological and measurable. Every person carries a finite quantity of living-time in their body — the Assessors call it *natual yield*, the borrowers call it *your count*, old women in the market call it *your portion*. It is not destiny. It is not fate. It is closer to what a doctor means by life expectancy, except in Varanthe it is precise, individual, and legible to trained eyes. Natural yield cannot be extended by medicine or will. It can be borrowed against.

**The lending mechanism:** A Ledger House can advance years from a borrower's future to their present. A woman with twenty natural years remaining can spend those years now — in health, in capacity, in full presence — and owe them back later. The debt is recorded in the ledger, a physical book bound in cloth that gradually takes on the specific smell of the borrower (sweat, cooking oil, the particular soap they use, and underneath, something the Assessors call *base note* — a biological smell that has no analogue and that Assessors learn to read like a second language). The ledger is the contract. Destroying it does not cancel the debt; the House holds a master copy. Stealing it accomplishes nothing. The debt is in the borrower's body.

**Experiential interest:** This is the mechanism that makes temporal debt different from financial debt. You do not repay in years alone. The Ledger House's Assessors identify specific future experiences as interest — a wedding, a particular conversation, a decade of good health in old age — and when the debt comes due, those experiences are *taken*. The borrower remembers having lived through them (the body was present, the time was spent) but the experience itself is gone: no emotional residue, no specific memory, just a gap shaped like something that should matter. Longtime debtors describe this as "the hollow feeling" — they know they were at their child's naming ceremony, they can describe the room, they cannot remember feeling anything. The experiences are not destroyed. They are held in the Ledger House's vaults, in secondary ledgers, for purposes that are not publicly explained.

**The texture of borrowed time:** Borrowed years feel different from natural time. Colors arrive slightly oversaturated — the amber of late afternoon is almost orange, the white of salt-bleached walls is almost blue. Sound comes a half-beat late, so that conversations feel subtly out of sync, like watching a mouth that doesn't quite match its words. Long-term borrowers develop a particular look: vivid, slightly unreal, present in the way that something preserved is present. Children of borrowers sometimes describe their parents as "too bright." Assessors are trained to read this texture as a diagnostic tool.

**Collateral limits:** You cannot borrow more than your remaining natural yield. An Assessor must certify your count before any contract is signed. This is the rule the system presents as a consumer protection. In practice, it means the Houses always know exactly how much you have left, and you do not.

**Inherited debt:** When a borrower dies with outstanding debt, the named heir inherits the full balance, including accrued interest. This is the cruelest mechanism and the most common source of catastrophic debt. The heir owes back years they never spent, experiences they never had, on a repayment schedule set by the House. The heir's own natural yield is assessed at the time of inheritance. If the inherited debt exceeds the heir's natural yield — if they are, mathematically, already in arrears — the House enters a period of "patient observation." They do not foreclose immediately. They wait. The contract specifies no reason for this. The senior Ledger-Masters know the reason.

**What the Houses cannot do (stated limitations):**
- They cannot lend time to a person whose natural yield is zero or negative. (This rule has an exception buried in the Orokwe Accords that no one in two centuries has had cause to invoke — until now.)
- They cannot take experiences that have not yet occurred. Interest is always drawn from the future.
- They cannot transfer debt to anyone not named in the original contract or its inheritance clause. (This is why the inheritance clauses are so carefully drafted. The Houses name heirs before the borrower does.)
- They cannot assess a person without physical proximity. The reading requires presence. This limits the Houses' reach to Varanthe and its immediate trade network — they have no mechanism for chasing debtors who leave the city. This is why leaving is illegal for anyone with outstanding debt. The law is framed as contract enforcement. It functions as a border.

**What the Houses can do that they don't advertise:**
- Ledgers can be read by anyone who knows how. Adaeze, as an Assessor, can read her mother's ledger in full — not just the contracts, but the marginal notations, the Assessor's shorthand, the base-note readings that track her mother's count year by year. This is how she discovers the sequencing.
- Experiences held in the vault ledgers retain some residue of the person they came from. Senior Assessors can read them. What they are reading toward is the novel's central mystery.

### The Assessor's Gift

Assessors are trained, not born, but training selects for a particular perceptual tendency — the ability to read biological time in a person's face, posture, and smell. It is not magic in the showy sense. It is closer to what a skilled diagnostician does, except the information is temporal rather than medical. An Assessor looks at a person and knows, within a margin of about eighteen months, how long they have left. They cannot always explain how they know. They describe it as reading *density* — some people seem very present, very solid; others seem thin, like paper held up to light. Adaeze is an unusually good Assessor. She can read within six months. She has never been wrong.

The cost: Assessors cannot easily turn this off. They read everyone. They read people they love. They read themselves, which is how Adaeze knew, two years before she opened the ledger, that something was wrong with her own count. She had been reading herself as thin for years. She had attributed it to stress.

### Societal Implications

**Class and debt:** Temporal debt in Varanthe follows the same logic as financial debt everywhere: the people who need it most can afford it least. The Grand Ledger serves merchants, guild masters, and city officials — people with enough natural yield to offer good collateral, whose borrowed time funds expansion and ambition. The minor Houses serve the rest. The compound effect over four generations is that the poor neighborhoods of Varanthe are populated by people who are, in aggregate, temporally exhausted — they have borrowed against their children, their children borrowed against their grandchildren, and the interest has been steadily stripping the texture from their lives. The hollow feeling is so common in the Adim district that people have a word for it: *oghere*, meaning roughly "the place where a thing was." Children in these neighborhoods grow up knowing their parents have oghere. It is ordinary. It is not discussed.

**Law and contract:** Varanthe's legal system has been shaped entirely around the Ledger Houses' needs over four centuries. Debt inheritance is constitutional. Leaving the city with outstanding debt is a criminal offense. Forging an Assessor's certification carries the death penalty — not because the city is unusually punitive, but because the Houses lobbied for it two hundred years ago and no one has successfully repealed it since. The city governor is technically independent of the Houses. In practice, the governor's family has held Grand Ledger accounts for three generations.

**Religion:** The dominant faith in Varanthe holds that the dead pass into a state called *the long count* — an afterlife measured not in years but in unlived time, the experiences that were never spent. The theological implication of temporal debt is quietly devastating: a person who dies in arrears enters the long count depleted, their afterlife already partially spent. The Ledger Houses do not engage with this theology publicly. They do not need to. The fear of dying in debt — of arriving in the afterlife hollow — is one of the most powerful forces driving people to borrow. To pay off debt before death is considered a form of spiritual hygiene. The irony is that paying off debt often requires borrowing more. The priests know this. Most of them have accounts.

**Childhood:** Children under twelve cannot sign contracts and cannot be named as primary borrowers. They can be named as heirs. This distinction is the legal mechanism that allows parents to borrow against their children's time — the child isn't borrowing, they're inheriting. The Orokwe Accords include language that was almost certainly written to enable this: "Inheritance of temporal obligation shall pass without restriction to named heirs upon the death of the primary holder, regardless of the heir's age at time of inheritance." Children in debtor families often develop the borrowed-time texture without having borrowed anything. Assessors call this *secondary saturation*. No one has studied why it happens.

## Geography

### Varanthe

The city sits where three distributaries of the Oru River meet the sea. It is built on a combination of solid ground (the older, higher districts), wooden platforms over tidal flats (the market districts), and stilted structures over open water (the poorest neighborhoods, which flood seasonally and are rebuilt seasonally, and have been for two hundred years). The city smells of dried fish and river clay and burning palm kernel and, in the Grand Ledger district, the particular acrid sweetness of the ink the Houses use for contracts — a smell Adaeze associates with her work and now cannot separate from dread.

**The Adim District:** Where Adaeze works and grew up. Built on the eastern tidal flat, half on platforms, half on reclaimed silt that floods every wet season. The buildings are painted in colors that have faded to near-pastels — ochre that was once orange, blue that was once indigo. The market here runs six days a week and sells everything the Grand Ledger district sells, at higher prices, in smaller quantities. The sound signature: constant water movement under the platforms, the knock of boats against pilings, voices carrying across water with unusual clarity. Children play a game called *count-and-run* that involves tagging each other and shouting numbers — everyone understands this is a game about debt, no one says so. The food: pepper soup with catfish, roasted plantain sold from flat baskets, palm wine from clay jugs sealed with banana leaves. Old women sit outside the Adim House every morning, not to borrow, just to sit near it, the way people sit near churches.

**The Grand Ledger Quarter:** Built on the highest ground in Varanthe, the only district that doesn't flood. The buildings are stone-faced, which is unusual in a city where stone must be imported. The streets are wide enough for two carts side by side. The Ledger Houses here are the size of government buildings, which is what they functionally are. The smell: that contract ink, and underneath it something older, something the building itself seems to exhale — the base-note smell of ten thousand ledgers stored in basement vaults. Assessors find this neighborhood difficult to spend time in. The accumulated temporal residue from the vaults interferes with their readings. Senior Assessors who work here long enough begin to have trouble reading themselves.

**The Mangrove Margin:** The outer edge of the delta, accessible by small boat, where the city frays into fishing settlements and then into pure mangrove. This is where people go when they are running from debt — it is technically illegal, but enforcement requires boats and the city guard prefers not to spend the money. The mangrove margin has its own economy, its own social structure, and its own relationship to time: the people who live there long-term report that the borrowed-time texture fades. No Assessor has been able to explain this. The Ledger Houses consider the margin a minor loss and do not pursue it aggressively. There may be a reason for this they have not disclosed.

**The Salt Towns:** Three small towns on barrier islands at the delta mouth, connected to Varanthe by ferry. They process fish, harvest salt, and serve as the city's link to coastal trade. The Ledger Houses have no formal presence in the Salt Towns — the Orokwe Accords grant them jurisdiction only within the city proper. This is why the Salt Towns have become, over two centuries, the place where Varanthe's wealthy send money they don't want the Houses to know about. The Salt Towns have their own informal temporal economy, running on handshake agreements and no written contracts. It is illegal. It is extremely common.

## Factions & Politics

**The Grand Ledger:** The dominant institution, controlling approximately 70% of all outstanding temporal debt in Varanthe. Publicly: a utility, a public service, a four-hundred-year tradition. Privately: an organization with a plan that spans generations and requires specific people to die at specific times. The senior Ledger-Masters know the shape of this plan. Most of them inherited their positions from parents who inherited from grandparents. They believe in the plan with something that functions like faith. The plan is called, in internal documents, *the solvency of history*. What this means is not yet clear to Adaeze. What is clear: it requires her death on schedule, and the deaths of approximately three hundred other people across the next thirty years, in a specific sequence.

**The House of Adim:** The minor House where Adaeze works. Nominally independent; in practice, financially dependent on the Grand Ledger for capital, which gives the Grand Ledger leverage over its operations. The House of Adim genuinely believes it is doing good work — its Assessors are more careful, its contracts are less predatory, its interest rates are lower. It is still a Ledger House. It still lends against children's time. Its director, a man named Obi Adim-Eze who is the fourth generation of his family to run the House, knows something is wrong with the Grand Ledger's long-term accounting. He does not know what. He has been not-knowing for fifteen years because knowing would require him to act.

**The Debtors' Assembly:** An informal political organization, technically a mutual aid society, that has been agitating for Jubilee invocation for sixty years. They have no formal power. They have significant popular support in the Adim district and the tidal flat neighborhoods. Their current leader is a woman named Sena Tawiah who borrowed against her own remaining years to fund the Assembly's operations and is now, by her own accounting, approximately four years from her natural end. She knows this. She has made her peace with it. She is the most dangerous person in Varanthe because she has nothing left to lose and has chosen to spend what remains on this.

**The Assessors' Guild:** The professional body for certified Assessors, controlled by the Grand Ledger through the Certification Act. Most Assessors are not aware of the sequencing plan — they are functionaries who do their work and go home. A small number of senior Assessors, the ones who have spent years in the Grand Ledger Quarter and absorbed enough vault-residue to read differently than they used to, have begun to notice the pattern in the debt calls. None of them have spoken about it. Two of them have died in the past three years under circumstances that were assessed, by other Assessors, as natural.

## Cultural Details

**Greetings and time:** The standard Varanthe greeting translates roughly as "how is your count?" — meaning, how are you, how is your time, are you well. The standard response is "my count is good" or, among close friends, an honest answer. Asking about someone's count directly is intimate. Asking about their debt is rude, the way asking about someone's salary is rude, except that everyone knows everyone is in debt and the rudeness is a social fiction maintained by mutual agreement.

**The ledger-opening:** When an heir receives an inherited ledger, there is a customary period of mourning before opening it — typically forty days. This custom has no legal standing but is nearly universal. Opening the ledger before forty days is considered disrespectful to the dead. Adaeze waited eight months. This is unusual but not unheard of. The longer you wait, the more interest accrues. The Houses do not remind you of this.

**Food and debt:** There is a dish called *ofe onugbu* — a bitter-leaf soup — that is traditionally eaten when signing a contract. The bitterness is the point. You are supposed to taste what you are agreeing to. The practice has become so associated with debt that some families refuse to serve it at any other occasion. Adaeze's mother made it every year on Adaeze's birthday. Adaeze did not know why until she read the ledger.

**Children's knowledge:** Children in debtor families are not told about inherited debt. This is considered protective. The result is that most people in Varanthe learn about temporal debt as a system in childhood but learn about their own family's debt as adults, often through inheritance. There is no word in the common language for the specific feeling of discovering that your childhood was purchased. There are many words for the feeling of discovering you owe something you cannot pay.

**Death customs:** The dead are buried within three days, before the wet-season heat. The body is wrapped in undyed cloth — color is for the living. The ledger of the deceased is placed on their chest for the burial night, then removed before interment. This is the moment the debt formally transfers. The heir does not have to be present. The transfer happens regardless.

## Voice Identity

### Tone
Quiet and precise, with grief underneath. The voice of someone doing paperwork on something that is breaking them. Not cold — Adaeze is not cold — but controlled, because control is the professional habit and the personal armor and, eventually, the thing that has to crack. The horror is never announced. It arrives in the language of accounting.

### Sentence Rhythm
Medium-length sentences as the baseline. Short sentences when something lands. Long sentences for memory and for the ledger passages, where Adaeze is reading and the prose enacts the experience of reading — clause after clause, the meaning accumulating, the implications arriving late. Dialogue is clipped. People in Varanthe do not say what they mean directly; they speak around things, and the thing being avoided is always audible in the gap.

### Vocabulary Register
Latinate for bureaucratic and institutional language (the language of the Houses, of contracts, of professional life). Anglo-Saxon for the body, for grief, for the physical world. The tension between these registers is the tension of the book: the clinical trying to contain the human.

### POV and Tense
Third limited, past tense, close to Adaeze. Occasional free indirect discourse — the narration slides into her assumptions and then pulls back. The reader should sometimes be unsure whether a judgment belongs to Adaeze or to the narrative, because Adaeze is sometimes unsure.

### Exemplar Passages

*The ledger smelled like her mother. That was the first thing — before she'd opened it, before she'd read a single line, just the smell of it sitting on her work table in the early morning, the particular combination of shea butter and woodsmoke and something underneath that Adaeze recognized from her training as base note, the biological signature of a person's count. She had assessed hundreds of people by their base note. She had never smelled her mother's.*

---

*The contract was dated the fourteenth day of the dry season, twenty-seven years ago. Adaeze had been four. The primary borrower was listed as Chidinma Mwenye, occupation: cloth merchant, natural yield at time of signing: assessed at thirty-one years. The collateral offered: the natural yield of her named heir, Adaeze Mwenye, currently aged four, assessed yield pending maturation. The amount borrowed: eleven years, immediate disbursement. Purpose of loan: treatment and recovery, chest fever, acute presentation. Interest rate: standard variable. Experiential interest: to be assessed at time of collection. Adaeze read this three times. Her mother had been dying. She had been four. She had not known.*

---

*"Your count is good," she told the woman across the desk, because it was true, because the woman was forty and had at least twenty-two years left and had come in wanting to borrow three, which was a reasonable ask, which Adaeze could approve without thinking about it. The woman smiled with relief so pure it was almost painful to look at. Adaeze stamped the form. She had stamped this form, or a form like it, four hundred times. She had stopped thinking about what it meant approximately three hundred and eighty stamps ago. She was thinking about it now.*

## Internal Consistency Rules

**Hard constraints:**

1. Temporal debt cannot be forgiven unilaterally by the House — it requires a formal Jubilee declaration under the Orokwe Accords, which requires a two-thirds vote of all active Ledger Houses. The Grand Ledger controls enough votes to prevent this. The Jubilee clause is not a dead letter; it is a locked door.

2. An Assessor's reading is the legal record. If an Assessor certifies a count, that number stands in court. This means Adaeze's assessment of her own situation — if she formally records it — is legally actionable. It also means that if she falsifies an assessment, she is committing a crime that carries the same penalty as forgery.

3. Experiences taken as interest cannot be returned. The vault ledgers hold them, but there is no mechanism for reinstatement. This is presented as a technical limitation. Whether it is actually a technical limitation or a policy choice is something Adaeze will have to determine.

4. The mangrove margin's effect on borrowed-time texture is real and consistent. It is not a rumor. The Houses know about it. Their decision not to explain it is a decision.

5. The sequencing plan — *the solvency of history* — requires that the debt calls happen in a specific order. Disrupting the order does not simply delay the outcome; it changes the outcome in ways the Ledger-Masters genuinely cannot predict. This is their actual vulnerability. They are not omniscient. They are executing a plan made by people who are dead, based on calculations that assumed compliance.

**What the writer must never do:**

- Give the Ledger Houses a moustache-twirling villain. The people running the Grand Ledger believe in what they are doing. They have inherited the belief the same way the debtors have inherited the debt.
- Resolve the question of Adaeze's mother. The ledger is evidence, not verdict. The mother was desperate and loving and she signed a contract that she understood imperfectly and that her daughter would pay for. All of this is true simultaneously.
- Make the magic legible through action sequences. The violence in this world is paperwork. The most devastating moments happen at desks.
