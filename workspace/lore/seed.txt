# THE ACCOUNTANCY OF GRIEF

**HOOK:** When a woman inherits her dead mother's debt-ledger — a book that literally contains the unlived years her mother borrowed against — she discovers her entire childhood was purchased on credit she now owes back to time itself.

---

**WORLD:**

The city of Varanthe is built along a river delta in a landscape inspired by coastal West Africa — mangrove labyrinths, salt-bleached market towns on stilts, the particular amber light of late dry season. The city smells of dried fish and river clay and the acrid sweetness of burning palm kernel. What makes it strange: *Varanthe runs on temporal credit.* Time here is a fungible substance. The Ledger Houses — great institutions older than the current dynasty — will lend you years against future payment. Want your dying child to live another decade? Sign the contract. Want ten more years of your prime for your weaving business, your music, your second chance at love? The Ledger Houses will provide. The interest accrues in *texture* — borrowed time feels different, slightly underwater, colors one shade too saturated, sounds arriving half a beat late. Longtime debtors develop a particular look: vivid and somehow *adjacent* to the moment they're inhabiting. The ledgers themselves are physical books that smell like the specific borrower — their sweat and perfume and something underneath, something biological and old. When the ledger-holder dies, the debt passes to their named heir. This is law. This is also catastrophe.

---

**MAGIC/COST:**

The speculative engine is **temporal debt and inheritance**. You can borrow years, but:

- You cannot borrow more than your natural remaining lifespan as collateral — the Ledger Houses have Assessors who *look at you* and know, the way a horse trader knows a horse
- Borrowed time accumulates *experiential interest* — you don't just owe years back, you owe *specific experiences* the Ledger House chooses, drawn from your remaining life. A wedding. A particular Tuesday. The birth of a grandchild. You don't know which until the Assessor arrives and takes them. You remember having lived them. They remember nothing
- Inherited debt is the cruelest mechanism: you owe back years you never spent, experiences you never had, *on a timeline the Ledger House sets*
- The magic is not dramatic or showy — it expresses as paperwork, clauses, compound interest. The horror is bureaucratic. The violence is contractual

Our protagonist, **Adaeze Mwenye**, is a *debt-assessor herself* — she works for a minor Ledger House, the kind that serves the poor, the desperate, the people who borrow against their children's first decade because the harvest failed. She is very good at looking at people and knowing how long they have left. She has always considered herself a fair functionary of a necessary system. Then she opens her dead mother's ledger and discovers her mother borrowed seventy-three years across Adaeze's entire childhood — which means Adaeze, who is thirty-one, *mathematically has no natural time left.* She is already living in arrears. She has, per the contract, been in default since her twenty-ninth birthday. The Ledger House has simply been... patient. Waiting to see what she'd do.

---

**TENSION:**

**Personal:** Adaeze must reckon with the fact that her mother — whom she loved, whom she mourned, whom she has spent years quietly eulogizing in the way of someone who lost a parent too soon — *spent Adaeze's life.* Every year of Adaeze's comfortable middle-class childhood, every year she had enough food and schooling and a mother who was *present and well and alive past the fever that should have taken her when Adaeze was four* — all of it was borrowed against Adaeze's future without her knowledge or consent. Her grief must now contain her mother's love AND her mother's theft. These cannot be separated. There is no version of this story where her mother was simply a villain. There is no version where she was simply a saint. To renegotiate her debt, Adaeze must understand the contracts her mother signed, which means reading the ledger — which means reading her mother's life in the clinical language of collateral and yield.

**Cosmic:** Adaeze, as an assessor, begins to notice irregularities in other ledgers. The Ledger Houses are not neutral institutions — they have been strategically lending to specific families, specific neighborhoods, specific lineages, for four generations. The compound interest of the poor has been *funding* something. Not profit. The Ledger Houses don't want money. They are assembling a particular *shape* of future — calling in debts in a specific sequence that will, in approximately thirty years, produce an event that the senior Ledger-Master refers to in one document as "the solvency of history." Adaeze doesn't know what that means. She knows it requires that she die on schedule. She knows it requires that a lot of people die on schedule. She knows that if she renegotiates her own debt — if she survives — the sequencing breaks.

**The tension between them:** To survive, Adaeze must become the thing she spent her career being: someone who decides whose time matters more. Her mother made that choice. She chose Adaeze. Adaeze now must choose whether to make it back.

---

**THEME:**

*When someone purchases your future without your consent in order to give you a good life — what do you owe them?*

And the darker second question underneath it: *Is love that costs someone else their autonomy still love, or is it just a prettier name for theft?*

The novel refuses to answer. Adaeze's mother was a real person with real desperation and real devotion. The Ledger Houses are a real system that real people built to solve real problems, and also a machine of intergenerational extraction. The personal question and the systemic question rhyme perfectly and resolve into each other and neither one provides absolution.

---

**WHY IT'S NOT GENERIC:**

A fantasy novel set in a West African-inspired coastal city where the magic system is *accounting* and the central act of heroism is a woman learning to read her dead mother's debt-ledger and understanding, line by line, what she was loved with and what she was robbed of — this is a book about inheritance, compound interest, colonial debt structures, grief, and the violence embedded in care, wearing the clothes of a quiet literary thriller.
