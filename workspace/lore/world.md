# WORLD.MD — HERON COVE: A COMPLETE WORLD BIBLE

---

## COSMOLOGY & HISTORY

### The Founding Myth

Heron Cove was settled twice. The first settlement, which locals call the Quiet Town, has no written record — only the evidence: foundations older than the oldest deed, a well that predates the county survey by two centuries, and a cemetery on the bluff where every headstone faces the water instead of away from it. The story told to children is that the first settlers came ashore in fog so thick they couldn't see the bow of their own boat, and when the fog lifted, half of them had already built houses and couldn't remember doing it.

The second settlement, the real one, arrived in 1743. Fishing families from the Azores, a handful of failed whalers from Nantucket, one documented Quaker minister named Elias Croft who never held a service. They found the Quiet Town's foundations and built on top of them because the stone was good. They found the cemetery and added to it. They found the well and drank from it. Within a generation, the new settlers stopped leaving.

This is not metaphor. The county records show no permanent emigration from Heron Cove between 1791 and 1887. People arrived. No one departed. The 1887 departure — a young schoolteacher named Maren Voss who left for Boston and sent back one postcard — is still considered locally significant enough to have a small plaque on the post office wall.

### Timeline of Tensions

**1743** — Second settlement established. The Croft family builds the first inn on the bluff road, above the fog line. It will change hands seven times but never leave the hospitality trade.

**1812** — The Drowning Season. Eleven fishing boats lost in six weeks. No bodies recovered. The widows of Heron Cove collectively refused to hold funerals, arguing that without bodies there was no proof of death. This was not denial. They were right about four of the eleven. The other seven have been waiting for their funerals ever since.

**1887** — Maren Voss leaves. The town does not understand why this is possible. The minister at the time writes in his private journal: *"Something in the Cove has loosened. I do not know if this is healing or rot."*

**1923** — The Olmstead Survey. A geologist named Frederick Olmstead arrives to map the coastal shelf and stays for three weeks. His published survey is unremarkable. His private notes, donated to the county historical society in 1971, describe a phenomenon he calls "temporal pooling" in the harbor basin — his instruments showed the same barometric reading for eleven consecutive days, which is physically impossible. He does not explain this. He returns to the Cove every summer until his death in 1949 and never publishes again.

**1961** — The Bellweather Inn changes hands for the seventh and final time. Agnes Murrow, age 34, purchases it from the Croft estate using money she does not discuss the origin of. She renovates the east wing, installs a commercial kitchen, and opens for business within the year. She is still there.

**1989** — The last living guest checks in. A traveling insurance adjuster named Harold Fitch stays three nights, eats Agnes's clam chowder, compliments the linens, and leaves a five-star review in the physical guestbook. After Harold, the living stop coming. Agnes does not advertise this fact.

**1991** — Agnes's husband, Douglas Murrow, dies of a coronary while repainting the garden fence. He is buried in the bluff cemetery. He is back in the kitchen by Thursday. Agnes sets a place for him at the staff table.

**2019** — The first living guest in thirty years arrives without a reservation. Her name is Carla Venn. She is dead by the third morning.

## THE RULES OF HERON COVE

### What the Town Is

Heron Cove is not haunted in the Hollywood sense. It is not a place where the dead linger because of unfinished business, though some of them have that too. It is a place where the membrane between the living and the dead has been thin for so long it has developed holes. The dead do not haunt the Cove. They *reside* there. They have routines. They have opinions about the weather.

### Hard Rules

**Rule One: The Fog Line.**
The fog that sits in Heron Cove harbor is not ordinary sea fog. It does not burn off. It thickens at dusk and thins at noon, but it never fully lifts, and it never rises above the bluff road. Everything below the fog line — the harbor, the cannery district, the old market street — exists in a state of softened time. The dead can move freely below the fog line. Above it, on the bluff, in the newer residential streets, in Agnes's inn, they must be *invited* or *anchored* to a specific object or place.

Cost of crossing the fog line uninvited: the dead who try it lose coherence. Not painfully — they describe it as becoming tired, then scattered. A spectral guest who wanders above the fog line without an anchor will spend the next several days more transparent than usual, struggling to hold a consistent shape or memory.

**Rule Two: Anchors.**
Every resident ghost is anchored to something physical. The anchor is almost never what you'd expect. It is not the murder weapon or the wedding ring. It is the coffee mug they used every morning, the particular chair, the door they always knocked on wrong. Destroy the anchor and the ghost does not die — they become untethered, which is worse. An untethered ghost in Heron Cove is called a *drifter*. Drifters cannot speak, cannot be spoken to, and move through walls and people leaving a cold streak and a smell of low tide. There are currently four known drifters in the Cove. Agnes keeps a list.

Cost of anchoring: the object slowly degrades. A wooden anchor rots faster than it should. A ceramic one develops cracks that spread from the center outward. Agnes has replaced Douglas's coffee mug four times. She buys them in bulk from a pottery supplier in Vermont who must wonder about her.

**Rule Three: The Appetite.**
The dead in Heron Cove are hungry. Not for food — they cannot eat — but for the *experience* of food. They want to smell it, to watch it being made, to sit at a table where it is present. A ghost who has not been in proximity to a prepared meal within a week becomes irritable, then agitated, then dangerous in the way a sleep-deprived person is dangerous: bad judgment, emotional volatility, a tendency to break things without meaning to. Agnes's inn functions primarily as a restaurant that does not serve the dead. She cooks. They gather. This is the arrangement.

Cost to Agnes: she cannot stop. She has not taken a vacation since 1991. She tried once, in 2003, left the inn in the care of her niece for a long weekend. She made it as far as the county line before turning back. She tells herself this was a choice.

**Rule Four: The Testimony Problem.**
The dead remember their lives with near-perfect clarity up until approximately five years before their death, at which point memory becomes unreliable and selective. They remember the emotional texture of recent years but not the facts. A ghost who died in 2010 can tell you exactly what their childhood kitchen smelled like and cannot reliably tell you what they had for breakfast the morning they died. This is relevant to the murder.

**Rule Five: The Living Visitor Problem.**
Living people who stay in Heron Cove for more than seventy-two hours begin to perceive the dead. Not dramatically — it starts as peripheral movement, voices in the next room, the sense that someone just left. By day five, most living visitors are holding full conversations with people they assume are eccentric locals. This is why the town's tourism industry collapsed in the 1970s. The Chamber of Commerce tried to rebrand this as a feature. It did not work.

Cost: living people who leave after the perception has begun carry a residue. They see things in other places. They hear voices in crowds. Most of them eventually stop talking about it. A few come back.

### Agnes's Specific Abilities

Agnes is not a medium in any formal sense. She has no training and no tradition. What she has is sixty years of proximity.

She can hear the dead clearly, at any time, in any weather. This is not a gift — it is an adaptation, the way a person who lives near train tracks stops hearing the train. The cost is that she cannot always tell, in a crowded room, whether the person speaking to her is living or dead. She has learned to check for the slight temperature drop, the way a ghost's voice comes from slightly the wrong direction, the fact that they never quite look at the door when they enter. She is right about ninety percent of the time. The ten percent causes social problems.

She can, with effort and physical contact with the anchor object, pull a ghost's recent memory into sharper focus. She holds the mug or the chair or the coat, and she asks, and sometimes the ghost can access what they normally cannot. This works about half the time and leaves Agnes with a migraine that lasts two days and a temporary inability to smell anything except ozone. Given that her entire livelihood depends on her sense of smell, she uses this ability sparingly.

She cannot compel the dead to do anything. She cannot banish them. She cannot protect the living from them. She is, functionally, a very attentive innkeeper.

### Societal Implications

**The Economy of the Dead.** Heron Cove has no tourism industry and a shrinking fishing industry. What it has is a small, quiet economy built around the needs of a town where the dead are permanent residents. The hardware store stocks unusual quantities of specific ceramic glazes and archival-quality wood sealant — anchor maintenance, though no one calls it that in the store. The grocery carries an outsized selection of ingredients with strong smells: good coffee, fresh bread, aged cheese, smoked fish. The dead cannot eat but the living cook for them, and the living have developed very strong opinions about quality. The best home cooks in Heron Cove are, without exception, people with a lot of dead family.

**The Legal Situation.** Heron Cove exists in a county that does not officially acknowledge the dead as residents, which creates persistent bureaucratic absurdities. Property owned by a dead person before 1980 is in legal limbo — the county wants to seize it for back taxes, the dead owners refuse to vacate, and the living relatives (where they exist) are caught in the middle. There are currently eleven properties in this limbo. Agnes is not in this situation because Douglas died after she owned the inn outright, but she knows all eleven households and has served on the informal committee that handles disputes. The committee has no legal standing and remarkable practical authority.

**Children and the Dead.** Children in Heron Cove grow up seeing the dead as a matter of course. This produces adults who are either very calm about death or very angry about it, with little middle ground. The local school does not address the dead in curriculum — this is a deliberate policy, not an oversight. The theory, held by a succession of principals, is that normalizing it in the classroom would require explaining it, and no one can explain it. Children learn what they learn at home. Some families tell their children the dead are just neighbors. Some families tell their children not to speak to them. The children of the second type of family tend to leave Heron Cove as soon as they can. They do not always succeed.

## GEOGRAPHY

### The Cove Itself

Heron Cove sits on a concave section of the northern New England coast where a granite headland breaks the prevailing wind from the northwest. The harbor is small — twelve slips, most of them occupied by boats that haven't moved in years. The water is very dark and very cold and the kelp beds come unusually close to shore. At low tide the exposed rocks smell of iron and something faintly sweet that no one has ever successfully identified. The fog pools in the harbor basin and moves in slow, deliberate patterns that don't match the wind direction.

### The Fog Line

The fog line runs approximately along the 40-foot elevation contour, which corresponds almost exactly to the old bluff road. Standing on the bluff road and looking down into the Cove on a typical evening: below you is gray-white fog, lit from within by the harbor lights, with the tops of the cannery buildings and the old market street visible as dark shapes. Above you is clear, cold air and stars. The line between them is not gradual. You can step across it.

### Key Locations

**The Bellweather Inn.** On the bluff road, above the fog line, facing the water. Three stories, white clapboard that Agnes repaints every four years, a porch that runs the full width of the front face. The kitchen is in the back, north-facing, and smells permanently of coffee and whatever Agnes baked most recently. The east wing has six guest rooms, all of them occupied by long-term spectral residents. The west wing is Agnes's private quarters. The dining room is the center of the inn's social life — a long table, eight chairs, a fireplace that Agnes keeps going from October through April. The inn smells like bread and old wood and, faintly, always, of the sea.

The inn sits on the Quiet Town foundations. The basement has walls that predate the 1743 settlement and Agnes does not go into the basement after dark. This is not a rule she has articulated. It is a rule her body enforces.

**The Harbor Market.** Below the fog line. A covered market that operates Tuesday and Saturday mornings, selling fish, produce from the inland farms, and a remarkable variety of canned goods that the vendors make themselves. The market is attended by both living and dead, and the vendors have developed a system: dead customers stand to the left of the stall, living to the right, and the vendor addresses them in alternation. This looks, to an outside observer, like a vendor talking to empty air every other sentence. Newcomers find this alarming. Regulars don't notice it.

**The Bluff Cemetery.** Above the fog line, which means the dead buried here cannot easily visit their own graves. This is considered, by the dead, to be extremely poor planning on the part of the living. Agnes has heard this complaint from Douglas so many times she has stopped responding to it.

**Croft's Point.** The northern headland. No structures. The granite here is a different color than the rest of the headland — darker, almost purple in certain light — and the wind off the point sounds different, lower in pitch. Children are told not to play there. Some of them do anyway. They come back quieter than they left.

**The Cannery.** Closed since 1978. Below the fog line. The building is intact because no one will purchase it and the owner — a dead man named Bertrand Luce — will not sell. Bertrand Luce is not a difficult ghost, but he is immovable on the subject of the cannery. Agnes has tried to negotiate. She has brought him good coffee and sat with him in the building for an entire afternoon. He is polite and firm and will not discuss his reasons. The building smells of rust and old salt and something Agnes has never been able to name.

### Neighboring Places

**Kettle Falls** (18 miles inland): The county seat. Bureaucratic, skeptical, and quietly aware that Heron Cove is unusual without having a framework for what to do about it. The county sheriff's department handles Heron Cove calls with a policy of minimal engagement. Deputies who are assigned to Heron Cove calls are volunteers. There are always volunteers because the deputies who go come back with stories, and the deputies who stay behind are curious, and curiosity turns over slowly.

**Wick Harbor** (11 miles south): A functioning fishing town, larger than Heron Cove, with a genuine tourism industry and a boardwalk and a seafood restaurant that's been written up in national magazines. Wick Harbor residents regard Heron Cove with a mixture of pity and unease. They call it the Quiet Cove, which they mean as an insult. Trade between the towns is limited and mostly flows one direction: Wick Harbor sells Heron Cove the things a town needs that it can't produce itself. Heron Cove sells Wick Harbor nothing, officially. Unofficially, there is a woman in Wick Harbor who pays Agnes for what she calls "consultations," and Agnes does not discuss what those consultations involve.

## FACTIONS & POLITICS

**The Permanent Guests.** The dead residents of the Bellweather Inn. Currently seven in number, not counting Douglas. They have been in residence between eleven and sixty-three years. They have strong opinions about room assignments, meal schedules, and each other. They are not a unified faction — they disagree constantly — but they share a common interest in Agnes's continued operation of the inn and will cooperate, reluctantly, when that interest is threatened. Key members: Douglas Murrow (Agnes's husband, anchor: his coffee mug, died 1991, primary concern: Agnes's stress levels and the quality of the morning coffee); Vera Ostrowski (anchor: a specific armchair in the east parlor, died 1974, primary concern: propriety and the correct way to set a table); and a man who will not give his name and is listed in Agnes's guestbook only as "Room 4" (anchor: unknown, died: unknown, primary concern: also unknown, behavior: watchful, quiet, never asks for anything, which Agnes finds more alarming than the demanding ones).

**The Drifters.** The four untethered dead. They are not organized and cannot communicate, but their presence affects the town in consistent ways: cold spots, inexplicable equipment failures, the feeling of being watched. One of the four drifters appears to be trying to communicate with Agnes — she finds arrangements of small objects, a kind of pattern she hasn't decoded. This has been happening for three years.

**The Holdouts.** Living families who have been in Heron Cove for multiple generations and have developed a proprietary attitude toward the dead. They do not welcome outside attention, official investigation, or Agnes's tendency to treat the dead as guests rather than residents. They believe the dead should be managed, not accommodated. Their spokesperson is a man named Gareth Croft, who is descended from the original Croft family and has opinions about everything. He and Agnes have been in a cold war since 1994, the origin of which neither will discuss in full.

**The County.** Kettle Falls sends a deputy when called, files reports that go nowhere, and is currently under pressure from a state-level historic preservation office that wants to survey the Quiet Town foundations under the Bellweather Inn. Agnes has refused access. The county is deciding how hard to push. The murder of Carla Venn has given the county a reason to push harder.

## BESTIARY / NATURAL WORLD

The herons that give the Cove its name are larger than the species should be. They stand in the harbor shallows at low tide and they do not startle. They watch. Locals do not eat them and do not discuss why.

The kelp beds in the harbor produce a variety that has not been successfully classified by the two marine biologists who have attempted it. It is edible — the Cove families have eaten it for generations — and it tastes of salt and something faintly metallic. Agnes uses it in her chowder. Visitors who eat the chowder sleep very deeply their first night and report vivid, specific dreams of places they have never been.

The fog itself is not alive, but it is not indifferent. It thickens around distress. Agnes has learned to read it the way a sailor reads the sky.

## CULTURAL DETAILS

**The Courtesy Rules.** Living residents of Heron Cove follow an unwritten but universal protocol for interacting with the dead: you do not ask how they died, you do not ask if they know they're dead, and you do not suggest they should move on. The first is considered rude. The second is considered cruel. The third is considered fighting words. Agnes enforces these rules at the inn with the same quiet authority she uses for everything else.

**The Guestbook.** Agnes keeps a physical guestbook at the inn's front desk. Living guests sign it with their names and dates. Dead guests sign it when they first arrive, and again on the anniversary of their arrival, and Agnes has thirty-one years of these signatures. The guestbook is the closest thing Heron Cove has to an official record of its dead population. The county does not know it exists. Gareth Croft has asked to see it twice. Agnes has declined twice.

**Food.** Agnes's cooking is the social center of the Cove. She makes chowder every Friday, bread every morning, and a rotating menu that she plans around what will smell best to the current guests. She has learned, over decades, what each of her dead guests most needs to smell: Douglas wants coffee and bacon. Vera wants something with vanilla. Room 4 has never responded to any food smell, which Agnes finds notable. She keeps trying different things.

**Coming of Age.** There is no formal ritual, but there is a recognized moment: the first time a Cove child has a full, unambiguous conversation with a dead person and doesn't run. Most children hit this around age nine or ten. Agnes had hers at seven, which is considered early and is part of why the Holdouts have always been suspicious of her.

**The Anniversary Week.** In the second week of November, which corresponds roughly to the anniversary of the Drowning Season, the dead in Heron Cove become more present. More visible, more audible, more insistent. The living treat this the way other towns treat a difficult holiday: with preparation, patience, and a lot of cooking. Agnes does not sleep during Anniversary Week. She has not slept through a full night in November in thirty years.

## INTERNAL CONSISTENCY RULES

**What Agnes cannot do:** She cannot compel, banish, or protect. She cannot communicate with drifters. She cannot access a ghost's memory without an anchor and a migraine. She cannot leave Heron Cove for more than approximately 36 hours — she has never tested exactly how long — without becoming unable to function. She does not know if this is the Cove holding her or her holding the Cove.

**What the dead cannot do:** They cannot physically move objects that weigh more than approximately two pounds. They cannot leave the Cove at all — the fog line is their outer boundary and the sea is their other. They cannot lie about their memories, but they can refuse to share them. They cannot be photographed, but they show up on audio recordings as a low-frequency hum that varies by individual.

**What the murder means:** Carla Venn is the first person to die violently in Heron Cove since 1923 (the Olmstead survey does not mention this). The dead are disturbed by this in a way Agnes has not seen before — not frightened, but *agitated*, the way a room full of people gets when someone breaks a social rule so fundamental no one knew it needed to be stated. Carla Venn herself has not yet appeared as a ghost. This is unusual. Agnes is waiting.

**The basement rule:** Agnes does not go into the basement after dark. The document does not explain why. Neither does Agnes.

**The fog's behavior around Carla's death:** The morning Agnes found Carla, the fog had risen above the bluff road for the first time in recorded memory. It receded by noon. Agnes noted this in the guestbook, in the margin, in small handwriting. She did not note what she thinks it means.

**The number seven:** Agnes has had exactly seven living guests in the history of her ownership. Harold Fitch was the sixth. Carla Venn was the seventh. Agnes has not consciously noticed this. The reader should.
