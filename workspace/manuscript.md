# Chapter One: What Agnes Cooks For

The yeast smelled right. Agnes held the bowl close and breathed it in — that particular living smell, faintly sour, faintly sweet, like something deciding to become something else — and set it back on the counter with the satisfaction of a thing confirmed.

Five in the morning. The kitchen was cold the way north-facing rooms are always cold, a cold that lives in the walls and doesn't care what the calendar says. October had come in hard this year, and the fog pressed against the window glass so thick Agnes could see nothing through it, not the garden, not the fence Douglas had been painting when he died. Just gray-white. The whole world reduced to the kitchen and whatever the kitchen contained.

She turned the dough out onto the floured board and began.

Her hands knew this. They had known it for sixty years, longer than most people she'd outlived had been alive, and they moved without instruction — heeling the dough forward, folding it back, turning the board a quarter inch by reflex. Agnes's mind was elsewhere, or nowhere, which was the same thing at five in the morning. The kitchen did what kitchens do when someone who loves them is working: it warmed. The oven ticked. The single light above the counter made everything amber and small and sufficient.

She shaped the loaves and set them to rise under the good linen cloth — not the cheap ones, she'd learned that in her second year, cheap linen left a texture on the crust — and washed her hands at the sink. The water ran cold before it ran warm. She stood and watched the fog on the glass while she waited for the temperature to change.

A draft moved through the kitchen from the direction of the east hallway. It smelled of salt and something older than salt, something that had been wet for a very long time. Agnes dried her hands on her apron without turning around.

"Breakfast at seven," she said, to the draft, or to whoever was making it. "Same as always."

The draft stilled. The kitchen settled back into its amber warmth, and Agnes put the coffee on.

---

She set the dining room table at half past five, before anyone came down.

Eight places. This was the number she'd laid for so long the count was in her hands before it was in her head — eight plates from the warming stack, eight cups, eight sets of silver from the velvet-lined drawer that stuck in damp weather and had been sticking for eleven years. She'd been meaning to sand it down. She kept meaning to.

Seven places for the permanent guests. The eighth for Douglas, at the near end of the table, closest to the kitchen door.

She placed his coffee mug last.

It was a plain mug, cream-colored, the kind sold in sets of four from a pottery supplier in Vermont who must have thought Agnes ran a very clumsy household. This was the fourth version. She turned it in her hands before she set it down, running her thumb along the side until she found it — a hairline crack, running from the rim down about two inches, thin as a thread. It had appeared last Tuesday. She pulled the small ledger from her apron pocket and noted it: *Oct. 8. Crack, rim, approx. 2 in. Stable.*

She closed the ledger and set the mug at Douglas's place, handle facing right.

The table looked the way she wanted it to look. She stood at the end and checked it the way a carpenter checks a level — not with instruments, with the eye that had done this long enough to know. The salt cellar was centered on its trivet. The cream was poured. The sugar bowl held the kind of sugar Vera required, not the coarser kind, and this had taken Agnes two full years to sort out in 1974, and she had not forgotten it since.

She moved the salt cellar a quarter inch to the left.

Vera would move it back. She always did. Agnes had stopped finding this irritating sometime in the 1980s and had arrived, somewhere in the 1990s, at a kind of appreciation for it — the salt cellar as a small, reliable argument, the argument as a kind of conversation, the conversation as the thing Vera actually wanted and would never say so. Agnes had learned to read Vera the way she read her dough: by texture, not by instruction.

She went back to the kitchen to check the bread.

---

Vera came down first, as she always did. Agnes heard her on the stairs — a particular rhythm, measured and deliberate, the step of someone who had decided how to descend a staircase and would not be revising the decision. She came through the dining room doorway in her good gray wool, hair set, and went directly to the table without greeting Agnes, which was also how she always did it.

Agnes watched through the pass-through as Vera's hand found the salt cellar and moved it back.

"Morning," Agnes said.

"The fog is very low." Vera settled into her chair. "Lower than last week."

"Mm."

"You'll want to check the porch boards before the others come down. The cold makes them contract. The third one from the door has been —"

"I'll check them."

Vera arranged her napkin in her lap with the precision of someone performing a task for posterity. Agnes turned back to the stove.

Douglas materialized near the sideboard.

She didn't see him arrive — she never did, he was simply not there and then he was, standing with his coffee mug already in hand, wearing the paint-stained work clothes and the boots she had been trying to get him out of for thirty-three years. He looked at the table, at his place, at the mug she'd set for him, and his expression did something complicated and quiet that Agnes had learned not to examine too closely.

"Morning, Ag," he said.

"Coffee's fresh."

He couldn't drink it. He stood near it, held his own mug, and the smell of it reached him, and this was the arrangement. Agnes had understood this for thirty years and had stopped needing to name it.

She cracked eggs into the pan — six, then two more, because Mr. Haverford from Room 6 had been more present than usual this week and presence correlated, in her experience, with appetite, or what passed for appetite among the dead. The eggs hissed and set at the edges. She added the smoked fish in strips, the good kind, from the harbor market, and the smell of it moved through the inn the way good smells do in old buildings, finding every room.

By the time she turned around, the table was full.

She had never seen Room 4 arrive. In eleven years, not once. He was simply there when she looked — at the far end of the table, in the chair he always occupied, wearing clothes from no decade she could confidently place. His hands were folded on the table. He was watching the window, or the fog beyond it, or something past both.

Agnes set a plate of toast at his end of the table. He never ate. She kept trying different things.

Mr. Haverford came down the stairs in a rush of cold air and took his seat with the particular eagerness of someone who has been waiting since before waking. The Okafor sisters drifted in from the east hallway, arguing in low voices about something Agnes couldn't hear and had learned not to try to. Old Mr. Pell came last, as always, and stood in the doorway for a moment as if remembering which room this was, and then sat down with the careful relief of someone who has been standing a long time.

Agnes cooked. They gathered. The fire in the dining room hearth took the edge off the October cold. Douglas stood near the sideboard with his mug and watched Agnes through the pass-through, and she felt his watching the way you feel a room's temperature — not intrusively, just as a fact of the air.

This was the arrangement. It had been the arrangement for thirty-three years, and it worked because she maintained it, and it would continue to work because she would continue to maintain it, and Agnes had not looked too closely at the circular logic of this in a very long time.

She sliced the bread and brought it out.

---

She took her own coffee to the front porch at half past seven, after the dishes were started and the dining room had quieted to the particular hush of the dead at rest.

The cold hit her at the door — a clean, honest cold, nothing like the draft from the east hallway, just October doing what October did. She stood on the porch with both hands around her mug and looked out.

Below the fog line, the harbor was gone. The Cove had become a bowl of white, lit gray from inside by the harbor lights, the tops of the cannery buildings dark shapes rising through it like the backs of things. Above the fog line, where she stood, the air was clear and sharp and the bluff road ran north and south in both directions, empty in the early light.

Agnes stood sideways to the front door. She always stood sideways to doors she'd recently come through — a habit so old she no longer knew she was doing it, only felt the wrongness in her body when she didn't. She checked the road north. Empty. She checked it south. A heron stood at the edge of the bluff, large and still, watching the fog below with an attention Agnes recognized as professional.

She checked the road north again.

Nothing moved. The fog held its line exactly at the elevation of the bluff road, as it always did, neither climbing nor retreating. The heron turned its head and looked at her, and Agnes looked back, and then she drank her coffee.

The third porch board from the door had contracted overnight; she felt it give slightly under her heel, not alarmingly, but enough. She made a note of it in the ledger. The crack in Douglas's mug. The porch board. The fog line holding.

Everything in its place.

She stood a moment longer, not because she needed to, but because the cold was clean and the coffee was good and the world above the fog line was, for now, simply itself — a road, a sky going pale, a very large bird who had opinions about the harbor. Agnes had learned, decades ago, to take the mornings for what they were and not ask them to be more.

She went back inside.

---

The guestbook lived on the front desk in the small entry hall, between the key hooks and the brass lamp Agnes polished every Sunday. It was a proper ledger, cloth-bound, the third one she'd kept since 1961 — the first two were in a box in the west wing, and she did not look at them often, but she knew where they were.

She opened it now, as she did most mornings, to make a notation about the porch board. The pen she kept in the desk drawer was the same style she'd bought in bulk in 1998, a box of twelve, and she was on the eleventh.

She wrote the date. She wrote: *Board 3, front porch. Contraction. Monitor.* She wrote the date of Douglas's mug crack, which she'd forgotten to enter the day it happened. She paused, and then wrote: *Fog line stable.*

She turned back a page to check her last entry against the date.

Harold Fitch, 1989. Three nights. Clam chowder. *Finest linens I've encountered outside of a proper hotel, and frankly finer than most of those.* His handwriting was small and careful, the handwriting of a man who wrote things down for professional reasons and had brought that habit to his personal life. Agnes had liked Harold Fitch. He'd asked sensible questions and tipped well and left on the third morning with a wave from the garden gate, and she had watched him drive up the bluff road and had thought: good. A good guest.

He'd been the last.

She knew this. She had always known this. The page with Harold Fitch's entry had been the last filled page for thirty years, and she turned past it every time she made a notation, and every time she registered this fact with the same mild, practiced acknowledgment she gave to the porch board and the hairline crack — *noted, stable, monitor.*

She turned the page now, as she always did, to find the next blank page for her entry.

She found it.

Agnes stood at the desk with the book open and the pen in her hand and looked at the blank page.

It was blank in the way pages are blank when they have not been written on. It was blank in the way the page after Harold Fitch had been blank every morning for thirty years, waiting for the next entry, which had not come.

She looked at it for a long moment.

Then she turned it.

The page underneath was blank too. She turned another. Blank. She fanned the remaining pages with her thumb — twenty, thirty, the whole back half of the book — and they were all blank, as they had always been, as they should be, as she had always known them to be.

She stood with the book open to the page after Harold Fitch.

The blank page looked back at her.

Agnes closed the book. She set it on the desk with both hands, squarely, the way she set Douglas's mug — handle right, position fixed. She smoothed the cover once with her palm.

The pen was still in her hand. She put it back in the drawer.

From the dining room came the sound of the fire settling, and the low murmur of the Okafor sisters' argument resuming, and the particular silence of Room 4 at the far end of the table, which was its own kind of sound once you had listened to it long enough.

Agnes straightened the brass lamp. She straightened it, and then she looked at her hand doing it, and she stopped.

---

# Chapter Two: The Courtesy Rules

The harbor market smelled of ice and iron and woodsmoke from the brazier that old Pell kept burning by the entrance, more for the dead than the living, since the dead felt cold in a way they could not fully articulate but circled toward heat regardless. Agnes came down the bluff road at half past seven with her canvas bag and her list, and the fog parted for her the way it did for everyone who had been walking through it long enough — not dramatically, not as if she commanded it, but with the mild, habitual courtesy of a door held open by someone who has seen you coming.

Tuesday market. She had been coming to Tuesday market since 1962, when she was still learning the names of the dead and writing them in a small notebook she no longer needed.

Pell's stall was first, as it had always been. He had the smoked fish — haddock and the darker, oilier cut he called harbor mackerel, which was not precisely mackerel but which Agnes had long ago stopped trying to classify. He was addressing a woman to his left when Agnes arrived, a dead woman in a housedress Agnes recognized as Marta Hjelm, who had drowned in 1958 and came to market every week to stand near the smoked fish and breathe.

"—same as last week, Marta, I know, I know you prefer the haddock but the mackerel's what came in fresh—" He turned to his right without pause. "Morning, Agnes. The usual?"

"And a half-pound of the mackerel."

"Marta, she wants the mackerel—" He was already wrapping. "—no, not your mackerel, she'll have her own. Agnes, she says good morning."

"Good morning, Marta," Agnes said, to the air to her left where Marta stood. The temperature there was four degrees colder than the rest of the stall, and Agnes could smell the low-tide iron that clung to the long-dead like a second skin. "How's the shoulder?"

Pell listened, then: "She says better. She says she thinks the cold is helping."

"Cold usually does." Agnes paid. Pell's change came back warm from his coat pocket, and she tucked it away and moved on.

This was the market's rhythm: vendor, then the dead customer, then Agnes, then the next vendor. She moved through it with the ease of someone who has done this so long the ease has become invisible even to herself — she no longer noticed the way she angled her body to leave room for the dead standing to her left, no longer registered the moment of temperature drop as anything other than information, the way she registered the quality of light or the smell of incoming weather. She knew the dead by name and preference and she knew their living relatives' faces and she knew, without ever having catalogued it formally, who had stopped coming to market and what that usually meant.

At the kelp stall — no name on the sign, no vendor name she had ever learned, just a woman of indeterminate age who arrived before the market opened and left before it closed — Agnes bought the unclassified kelp in its usual quantity. The woman wrapped it without being asked in the brown paper Agnes preferred. The kelp smelled of salt and something faintly metallic, and Agnes breathed it in before the paper closed around it, a habit she could not have explained except that the smell was clarifying in a way she had come to rely on.

She walked home up the bluff road with the bag on her arm and the fog thinning as she climbed.

---

Vera was in the east parlor when Agnes came through at eleven, which was not unusual. Vera was in the east parlor most mornings, in her chair — the specific wingback armchair with the green upholstery that Agnes had reupholstered four times, each time to the same specifications, because it was the chair and Vera had made this clear. She sat with the posture of someone who had decided, at some point in the mid-twentieth century, what correct posture was and had not renegotiated since.

"Agnes." Not a greeting, exactly. A summons.

Agnes set down her bag by the door. "Vera."

"The salt cellar." Vera paused. "The left side of the table."

"Mm."

"It has been on the right side of the table for three mornings running. I have moved it. Someone moves it back."

Agnes thought: Douglas. Douglas moved it back because he found the left placement fussy and had been conducting this particular campaign since 1994. She did not say this. "I'll speak to the kitchen."

"You are the kitchen."

"Then I'll speak to myself."

Vera's expression adjusted fractionally. She was not appeased, but she was cataloguing this as adequate acknowledgment, which was the best outcome available. Agnes came further into the parlor and sat in the chair across from her — the other wingback, the blue one, which was nobody's anchor but which Agnes had always found comfortable in the specific way of chairs worn to the shape of long use.

The fire was going. Agnes had set it before breakfast and it had settled into the steady, low burn she preferred — not the showy crackle of a fire trying to impress, but the quiet, working heat of one that knew its job.

"There's something else," Vera said.

"There usually is."

Vera's hands were folded in her lap with the precision of someone who had once been taught that a woman's hands should always be doing something useful, and who had resolved the impossibility of this requirement by making stillness itself look deliberate. She looked at Agnes with the expression she wore when she was deciding how much to give away.

"I heard something last night," she said. "From the east wing."

Agnes waited.

"A sound I don't recognize. I have been in this inn for fifty years and I know its sounds, Agnes. The pipes, the wind off the north corner, the particular way Room 2 settles after midnight. This was not one of those sounds."

"What did it sound like?"

"It sounded like someone moving furniture. In a room where furniture should not be moving." She paused. "I am not suggesting anything. I am reporting."

"I'll check the east wing."

"Yes." Vera looked at the fire. Something shifted in her, small and quick, and then was gone. "You can't keep everyone comfortable forever, Agnes." She said it in the same tone she'd used for the salt cellar — a management complaint, a notation. "Eventually something comes in that won't be managed."

Agnes looked at her.

Vera was still watching the fire.

"I'll check the east wing," Agnes said again.

"Yes," Vera said. "You said."

Agnes stayed another ten minutes, because Vera would not ask her to and needed her to, and then she picked up her bag from the door and went to start lunch.

She filed Vera's line in the same place she filed the porch board and the hairline crack. *Noted. Monitor.*

She did not examine why her hands were cold when she got to the kitchen, or why she kept them busy with the bread until the cold went away.

---

She ate alone at the small table in her quarters, as she always did. The dining room table was for the guests, and the staff table in the kitchen was where she took her morning coffee, but dinner was here, in the room at the west end of the inn where she had slept for sixty-three years. The room smelled of wool and old paper and the lavender sachets she replaced every spring. She had a single window that faced the water, and in winter the fog pressed against it from outside like a cat asking to come in.

The plate in front of her was the same chowder she'd made for the guests, because she had stopped making separate things for herself sometime in the 1980s when she noticed she wasn't tasting the distinction. She ate methodically. She was good at eating alone. She had long since stopped noticing the quality of the silence.

On the wall above the small bookcase, Douglas smiled at the camera with the particular squinting expression of someone who had been told to hold still and found this unreasonable. Paint on his forearm — the fence, she thought, or possibly the garden gate, the photo was from the late eighties and there had been a lot of painting that year. He looked, in the photograph, like a man who had things to do and was tolerating the interruption. She had always loved this about him.

On the shelf below the photograph, the mug. Number four. A plain cream stoneware with a slightly thicker handle than numbers one through three, which Douglas claimed he preferred, though she suspected he said this to spare her the guilt of the upgrade. The crack along the base was new — a week old, maybe ten days. She had not looked at it closely.

She did not look at the door in the east wall.

She had not looked at it in eleven years. The door was there — she was aware of it the way you are aware of a tooth that has been hurting long enough to become part of the landscape — and she had keys, and there was no reason she could not open it, and she had not opened it in eleven years, and she ate her chowder and looked at Douglas's photograph and was not thinking about the door.

A knock at the outer door — the hall door, the correct one.

"Come in," she said.

---

Douglas came in the way he always came in: slightly sideways, as if apologizing for his mass, which had been considerable in life and which his ghost retained in some structural sense even if the physics no longer entirely applied. He was carrying the mug. He always carried the mug. She had asked him once, years ago, if he could set it down, and he had looked at her with the expression of a man being asked to set down his own hand.

He sat in the chair by the window. The chair was his — not an anchor, not technically, but her body knew not to leave things on it.

"How was market?" he said.

"Pell's got good mackerel this week. Marta's shoulder is better."

"The cold helping?"

"So she says."

He looked at her plate. "You're eating late."

"I was in the kitchen until six."

"Bread day."

"Bread day." She pushed the last of the chowder around the bowl. "Vera came to me about the salt cellar again."

Douglas had the grace to look slightly caught. "The left side is fussy."

"I know."

"It crowds the bread plate."

"Douglas."

"I'm not moving it back."

"You are going to move it back," Agnes said, "and you are going to do it before Vera notices it's been moved again, and we are going to let her believe she's won this one because she needs to win something this week." She looked at him. "She heard something from the east wing last night."

Douglas's expression shifted. Not much. Enough.

"What kind of something?"

"Furniture moving."

He looked at the window. The fog outside had thickened with the evening, and it pressed against the glass with its usual patience. "Ag," he said, and stopped.

"I'll check it tomorrow."

"The roof's been on my mind," he said, which was not what he'd been about to say. She could hear the seam of it. "East wing. You know the corner above Room 5 — the flashing's been pulling loose since September. If we get a hard freeze before you get someone up there—"

"I'll call Hennessey."

"Hennessey moved to Wick Harbor."

"Then I'll call someone else."

Douglas was quiet for a moment. He turned the mug in his hands, which he did when he was working up to something. She watched him do it and kept her expression even and waited for him to either say the thing or not say it.

"You know what I think?" he said.

"Usually."

"I think the roof isn't what you're not getting to."

The fire had burned down to coals. The room was warm enough, the wool blanket on the chair arm was soft and familiar, and outside the fog pressed its blank face against the glass, and Agnes put her spoon down on the plate with a small, definitive sound.

"Hennessey's nephew," she said. "He does roofing. I think he's still in the county."

Douglas looked at her for a long moment.

"Right," he said. Not agreement. The sound a man makes when he has been redirected so many times in a particular direction that he has learned the shape of the wall. "Right, I'll — I think I have his number somewhere."

"Thank you," Agnes said.

He stayed another half hour, talking about the boat in slip seven that had started listing, about Pell's new assistant who might be living and might not, about whether the Okafor sisters' ongoing argument about the window in Room 6 was getting worse or had simply reached its natural equilibrium. Agnes listened and asked questions and was grateful for all of it, every mundane word of it, and when he left she sat for a while in the quiet room with the photograph on the wall and the mug on the shelf and the door in the east wall that she was not looking at.

Then she got up, because there was still the guestbook.

---

The book lived on the front desk in the small entry hall, under the brass lamp she kept on a timer. She turned the lamp up and opened it to the first page, which she never read anymore but touched anyway — the original entry in her own handwriting, June 1961, the ink brown with age. She turned through the years with the ease of long practice. The living guests were easy to find: their handwriting pressed harder, their ink darker, the signatures of people who had not yet learned to hold things lightly.

She ran her finger down the column of dead guests' signatures. Counted them, as she did every night.

One: Eleanor Marsh, arrived 1963.
Two: the Okafor sisters — Patience and her sister Constance, whose names she counted separately because they had arrived separately and had never, in Agnes's understanding, agreed on anything enough to be a single entry.
Three: Constance.
Four: Vera Ostrowski, arrived 1974.
Five: a man named Hollis Pratt who had died in 1981 and arrived the following spring and who occupied Room 2 with the quiet, undemanding quality of someone who had made peace with his situation.
Six: Douglas, 1991. His handwriting unchanged from the living signature two pages earlier, which she had always found, privately, a mercy.
Seven: Room 4. One line. No name. The date: March, 2012.

Seven.

She closed the book. She set it square on the desk, both hands, and smoothed the cover once.

Outside the window at the end of the hall, the fog was doing something. She became aware of it peripherally — a thickening at the glass, a whitening, the sense of pressure increasing without sound. She looked up.

The fog pressed against the window with a density she had not seen since — she did not finish the thought. It held for perhaps thirty seconds. Then, as if something had exhaled, it thinned back to its usual gray patience and the harbor lights came through again, small and steady.

Agnes stood at the desk with her hand still on the guestbook.

The lamp buzzed once, faintly, and then was quiet.

She turned off the light and went to bed, and she did not sleep for a long time, and in the morning she would tell herself it was the bread sitting heavy, and she would almost believe it.

---

# Chapter Three: A Guest Without a Reservation

The doorbell was a brass thing, original to the house, and it didn't ring so much as it spoke — a low, declarative *bong* that Agnes felt in the back of her teeth more than she heard with her ears. She stood at the kitchen counter with her hands in a bowl of bread dough and listened to the silence after it, trying to remember the last time she'd heard it from this direction.

Deliveries came to the back. Had come to the back since 1989, when she'd moved the welcome mat and stopped replacing the porch light bulb on the front side and generally made the back door the kind of door you used and the front door the kind of door you looked at. The dead didn't ring the bell. They came through the wall or the window or they were simply there, in the hallway, looking at her with the patient expression of people who had all the time there was.

The bell spoke again.

Agnes pulled her hands from the dough, leaving two deep prints in the pale mass, and wiped them on her apron. She took her time. She composed, as she crossed the dining room, a polite refusal: *I'm sorry, we're not taking guests at the moment. I can direct you to Wick Harbor — there's a fine inn on the waterfront there, very well reviewed.* She adjusted this twice before she reached the door. She had it ready.

She opened the door.

---

The woman on the porch was dark-haired and thin in the way that comes from forgetting to eat rather than trying to. She had a backpack on one shoulder and two notebooks tucked under her arm — not in the backpack, under her arm, held against her side like something she needed to reach quickly. She wore a jacket with four visible pockets and probably more that Agnes couldn't see. Her face was the color of old paper, and there were circles under her eyes deep enough to have their own weather.

She was looking at the inn with an expression Agnes recognized, though she hadn't seen it in thirty years. It was the expression of someone who had been looking for a specific thing for a long time and had found it in the wrong place at the wrong time and was going to ask for it anyway.

"I'd like a room," the woman said.

Agnes kept her hand on the doorframe. "We're closed to guests."

"I know." The woman shifted the notebooks to her other arm. "Your sign is gone, but the hook's still there. And your porch light's been out since at least — I don't know, I can't tell from the bulb. But I know you're closed."

"Then you know my answer."

The woman looked at her. Not past her, not at the inn over her shoulder — at her, with the direct, slightly unfocused attention of someone running on insufficient sleep and too much coffee. "I've been in the Cove for six hours," she said. "I keep seeing things at the edge of my vision. Not — I know what peripheral hallucinations look like. I've been tested. This isn't that." She paused. "I'd rather be somewhere with walls I can trust."

Agnes's hand tightened on the doorframe.

She looked at the woman on the porch. Dark hair, too many pockets, the pallor of someone who had driven a long way without stopping. Six hours. Agnes did the arithmetic without wanting to. The seventy-two-hour rule was not a rule anyone had written down or explained to her — it was something she had observed, over thirty-one years, the way a farmer observes weather patterns: empirically, unhappily, with the knowledge that observation doesn't change the outcome.

Six hours in the Cove and she was already seeing things.

Agnes looked past her, briefly, at the fog below the bluff road. It was doing its usual evening work, thickening at the edges, the harbor lights going soft and diffuse behind it. The temperature on the porch was dropping in the way it dropped when the light failed, the cold coming up from the water rather than down from the sky.

If she turned this woman away now, she would be alone in the Cove at night, in a rented room somewhere below the fog line, watching something move at the edge of her vision with no framework for what it was.

Agnes stepped back from the door.

"Room 3," she said. "Dinner's at seven. Don't touch the right towel hook — it pulls from the wall."

The woman let out a breath. "Thank you."

"Mm," Agnes said, and led her inside.

---

Room 3 was at the end of the east wing hallway, the last door before the window that looked out over the back garden. It was the only room without a permanent occupant, and Agnes kept it made up from habit, the bed tight and the curtains straight and a small lamp on the nightstand. It smelled of lavender and the cedar lining of the wardrobe and, very faintly, of the sea — which everything in the inn smelled of, after enough years.

Agnes opened the curtains while the woman set her backpack on the bed.

"Bathroom's across the hall," Agnes said. "The hot water takes a moment. The window sticks in the lower sash — push it left before you push it up." She turned from the curtains. "Left hook for the towels."

The woman had put both notebooks on the nightstand. She was standing in the middle of the room looking at the walls with the focused attention of someone taking measurements by eye.

"Thank you," she said again. "I'm Carla Venn."

"Agnes Murrow."

Carla Venn nodded, like this confirmed something. She unzipped the outer pocket of her backpack and took out a pen, uncapped it, and wrote something in the top notebook without opening it to a fresh page — she already had one ready, Agnes saw. She'd had one ready before she knocked.

Agnes watched this and kept her expression the same as it always was.

Carla looked up from the notebook. "Does your basement predate the 1743 survey?" she said. "That's — sorry, I should — I know that sounds like a strange question. But that's the question."

Agnes stopped moving.

She was by the door, one hand on the frame, and she stopped. The lamp on the nightstand put a warm circle on the wall. Somewhere down the hall, she could hear the particular silence of a house with guests in it — not empty, not full, occupied in the way the Bellweather was always occupied.

"How do you know about the survey?" Agnes said.

Carla had the pen poised over the notebook. "I've been researching the Cove. For about two years." She said it evenly, without apology. "The survey is — the 1743 survey is where the documented record starts, but the foundations here are older. I've been trying to establish how much older."

Agnes looked at her for a moment. The notebook. The pen. The two years.

"Dinner is at seven," Agnes said. "Don't be late — the chowder doesn't hold."

She left before Carla Venn could ask anything else.

---

The kitchen received her the way it always did: warm, low-lit, smelling of the morning's bread and the stock she'd had going since noon. Agnes went to the counter and put both hands flat on the edge of it, feeling the cold solidity of the tile, the slight roughness where the grout had worn. She stood there.

The stock needed skimming. The bread from this morning needed to come out of its cloth. She had onions to dice and potatoes to cube and the kelp to prepare, which required its own attention — the harbor kelp was particular, and she'd learned over decades that you had to treat it with a certain respect, a certain deliberateness, or the chowder came out wrong in ways she couldn't entirely explain.

She did not start any of this immediately.

She went to the small desk in the corner of the kitchen, the one with the guestbook on it. She opened the drawer and took out the pen she used for entries — not the good pen, not a casual pen, but the specific pen she'd used for this purpose since 1984 when the previous one had run dry mid-signature and she'd taken it as a sign to be more deliberate. She opened the guestbook to the current page.

She wrote: *Carla Venn.* And the date.

She looked at it.

The ink was dark. The handwriting pressed into the page — she could feel the indentation on the back of the sheet if she ran her thumb across it. Living guests always pressed harder. They held things with more weight, as if they knew, somewhere below conscious thought, that the marks they made needed to last.

Agnes looked at the name. She set the pen down parallel to the spine of the book. She turned back, not deliberately counting, just looking — the way you look at a shelf of familiar objects and register their arrangement without naming each one. The signatures went back through the decades. The dead guests' signatures were lighter, more even, the handwriting of people who'd had time to practice patience.

She closed the book.

She put the pen back in the drawer.

She went to the counter and began on the onions, because the onions needed doing and the chowder needed time and Carla Venn had been in the Cove for six hours and was already seeing things at the edges, and Agnes had made her choice at the front door and there was no useful purpose in standing in her kitchen second-guessing it. She diced with the efficiency of someone who had diced ten thousand onions in this same kitchen under this same light, her eyes dry, her hands steady.

The stock needed skimming. She skimmed it.

She went to the cold box for the kelp, which she kept on the lowest shelf in its damp cloth, and she unwrapped it on the board and looked at it for a moment before she began. It was dark, almost purple at the fronds, and it smelled of iron and salt and something faintly sweet she had never been able to name. She had been using it in her chowder since the mid-seventies, when old Bertrand Luce had brought her the first bundle from the harbor shallows and told her, in his slow, incomplete way, that it was what the families used. She had asked what families. He had paused for a long time and then said, *all of them,* and that had been the end of the conversation.

She chopped the kelp fine. She added it to the stock.

From the east parlor, she heard the particular sound of Vera settling into her chair — a small, precise sound, the sound of a woman arranging herself correctly. From the direction of the dining room came the low, almost subsonic presence of Douglas, who was probably standing near the sideboard with his coffee mug, waiting to talk to her. She could tell his mood by whether she could feel him from this distance. Tonight she could feel him clearly.

She did not go to the dining room. She would deal with Douglas at dinner.

She diced the potatoes. She put them in cold water to wait. She measured the cream by eye, as she always had, and set it near the stove to take the chill off. She worked through the preparations with the methodical attention she brought to everything in this kitchen, and she did not think about Carla Venn's question, and she did not think about the two notebooks on the nightstand in Room 3, and she did not think about the word *survey* spoken in a stranger's voice in a room that had been empty for thirty years.

She thought about the chowder. She thought about what time it was and whether the bread needed to come out now or could wait another ten minutes.

It could wait.

She stood at the stove and stirred the stock in a slow circle and listened to the inn around her — the settling of old wood, the faint sound of movement from Room 3 above her, the particular quality of silence from the east parlor where Vera sat, and from Room 2 where Hollis Pratt was probably reading, and from Room 4 where the guest who would not give his name was doing whatever it was he did in the long hours of the evening.

She added the potatoes to the stock.

She added the onions.

She waited for the smell to change, the raw sharp smell of the raw vegetables giving way to something softer, something that would reach every room in the inn and pull everyone to the table the way it had been pulling them for thirty years. The dead couldn't eat. But they could smell. And the smell of a proper chowder on a cold evening was, Agnes had found, the closest thing she had to a language everyone in this house understood.

The cream went in last. She watched it bloom white through the darker stock and stirred it once, gently, and turned the heat down.

Seven o'clock. She would need to call Carla Venn down.

She stood at the stove with the wooden spoon in her hand and looked at the kitchen she had cooked in for sixty-one years. The same counter. The same tiles, the grout worn in the same places. The same window over the sink showing the same dark garden and the same fog-softened lights of the harbor below the bluff.

She had cooked for a living nose before. Six times before. Harold Fitch, 1989, who had complimented the chowder and left a five-star review in the physical book and driven away in a rental car and never come back. Five others before him, each one a small, careful problem she had managed and resolved. She had managed them by feeding them well and keeping them comfortable and making sure they understood, without being told directly, that the Bellweather was a particular kind of place that asked for a particular kind of guest.

She had never managed one who arrived already asking about the 1743 survey.

The chowder was ready. Agnes tasted it from the wooden spoon, considered, added a pinch of salt. She put the spoon down on its rest and went to the bottom of the stairs.

"Miss Venn," she called up. "Seven o'clock."

A pause. Then footsteps, quick and purposeful, from the direction of Room 3.

Agnes went back to the kitchen and began ladling the chowder into the good bowls.

She set seven places at the dining room table, as she did every night. Eight chairs, seven places — the eighth chair at the end, the one with the slightly uneven leg, was hers, and she ate in the kitchen or not at all, depending on the night. She set the bread in the center of the table. She put the salt cellar on the left side, where it belonged, because Vera would count the seconds until she could say something about it if it was on the right.

She put a bowl at every place.

She stood back and looked at the table, which was set for seven and would hold six dead and one living, and she thought: *well.* And then she went to get the bread knife, because the bread needed slicing and the chowder was getting cold and dinner did not wait, not even for this.

# Chapter Four: Seven at Table

The kelp had been sitting in a bowl of cold water since noon, and Agnes had been pretending not to notice it.

She stood at the counter with her back to it, reducing the stock. The stock needed attention — needed the heat kept just so, needed the occasional pass of the skimmer — and this was a perfectly good reason to stand with her back to the bowl of kelp and not think about what she was doing.

She was making chowder. She made chowder every Friday. This was a Friday.

The kelp was because it was the good chowder. The kind she made when the chowder needed to be good for a reason.

Agnes skimmed the stock and did not examine her reasons.

The kitchen was warm, the kind of warmth that collected under the ceiling and pressed down gently on everything below it. The window over the sink showed the garden going dark, and below the bluff the harbor lights were coming on one by one, soft and diffuse through the fog. She could smell the sea from here, the cold salt of it, and the bread she'd left cooling on the rack, and the particular green-iron smell of the kelp in its bowl. She turned and added it to the pot.

She had cooked for a living nose six times. Each one had been a small, careful problem, and she had managed each one by the same method: feed them well, keep them warm, let the inn do what the inn did, which was to make people feel held. Harold Fitch, 1989, had eaten two bowls of chowder and slept ten hours and left her a review in the physical book that said *exceptional hospitality, highly recommend.* He had driven away in his rental car and she had watched his taillights disappear down the bluff road and felt the particular relief of a problem resolved without incident.

She had never cooked for one who arrived already asking about the 1743 survey.

Agnes added the potatoes. She added the onion she'd been sweating since four o'clock, translucent and soft, and the cream, and she stood over the pot and breathed it in. Salt and sweet and the deep mineral note of the kelp. She adjusted the heat by feel. She got out the good bowls, the ones she didn't use for herself, and began to think about the table.

---

She had set seven places. She stood in the doorway of the dining room and looked at them: seven bowls, seven spoons, seven folded napkins, the bread in the center of the table with the good bread knife beside it. The fire had been going since four, and the room held the heat of it, amber and close. It smelled of wood smoke and the faintest trace of the chowder drifting in from the kitchen.

Five of the seven chairs already had their occupants.

Agnes could see them clearly, had been able to see them clearly for so long it registered less as sight and more as the simple fact of the room: Vera in her usual chair, spine straight, hands folded in her lap with the patience of someone who has been waiting for propriety to be restored and has learned to wait indefinitely. Old Marsh two seats down, already reaching for the bread before Agnes had put the knife down, and Agnes moved the knife slightly out of reach without looking at him, a reflex. The Calloway sisters in the chairs nearest the window, who had arrived together in 1983 and conducted a continuous, inaudible argument Agnes had been watching for forty years. And at the far end of the table, in the chair with the straight back, Room 4.

He was watching the door.

Agnes noted this the way she noted most things about Room 4: filed it, set it aside, moved on. He was often watchful. It was not her business what he was watching for.

She went back to the kitchen for the pot.

Carla Venn came down the stairs at three minutes past seven, carrying a notebook. She paused in the doorway of the dining room the way people paused when they walked into a room that felt occupied and found it apparently empty, and Agnes, coming in behind her with the pot, watched the small recalibration: Carla's shoulders dropping a fraction, her chin lifting, her eyes moving around the table with the careful attention of someone who had decided to take notes in her head.

"Sit anywhere," Agnes said. "Not the end chair."

Carla sat in the middle of the table, which put her between Room 4 at the far end and the Calloway sisters at the near end, with Vera directly across. She put her notebook on the table beside her bowl, then appeared to reconsider and moved it to her lap.

Agnes ladled the chowder.

The temperature in the room dropped two degrees when she moved to serve the far end of the table. She knew this the way she knew the state of the fire, the way she knew whether the bread was done before the timer — not from instruments but from sixty years of this particular kitchen, this particular room, these particular guests. Carla felt it too. Agnes saw her set down her spoon and pick it up again without eating, her eyes moving to the window and back.

"It's a cold house," Agnes said. "Old houses run cold."

"Right," Carla said. She picked up her spoon.

Vera, across the table, watched Carla the way she watched everyone who sat at Agnes's table for the first time: with the expression of a woman who has already identified three social infractions and is deciding which one to address first. The notebook on Carla's lap had registered. Agnes could tell.

Marsh was eating with enthusiasm, which was normal. The Calloway sisters were ignoring the food and continuing their argument, which was also normal. Room 4 had not moved.

Agnes took her place at the end of the kitchen pass and watched.

Carla ate the chowder. She ate it carefully, the way a person eats when they're paying attention to what they're eating — not performatively, not suspiciously, but with the focused consideration of someone whose nose was telling them something and whose brain was trying to catch up. She ate half the bowl before she said anything.

"The other guests," she said. "Are they always this quiet?"

"Some of them," Agnes said.

"Long-term residents, you said."

"I did."

Carla looked at the chair across from her, the one Agnes had not served, and then at the one beside it. Her gaze went around the table slowly. The fire popped. From the direction of the Calloway sisters — from slightly the wrong direction, Agnes noted, the direction of the wall rather than the chairs — came a low murmur of conversation.

Carla's head turned toward the wall, then back to Agnes.

"The acoustics in here are interesting," she said.

"Old houses," Agnes said again.

A small silence. Carla ate another spoonful of chowder. Agnes watched her, and Douglas watched Agnes watching Carla from the doorway to the kitchen, where he stood with his mug and said nothing, which was its own kind of commentary.

Room 4 was still watching Carla.

Agnes refilled the bread plate. She did not look at Room 4. She was aware of him the way she was aware of a cold draft, something at the edge of the room that her attention kept sliding away from, but she noted his attention on Carla and she filed it and she moved on.

"Do they eat?" Carla asked.

Agnes set the bread plate down. "Now."

"I'm asking because I've served six bowls of chowder and none of them have—"

"They're not hungry," Agnes said. "They come for the company."

It was not entirely a lie. Agnes straightened the bread knife.

Carla looked at her for a moment, then down at her bowl. "The chowder is extraordinary," she said. "I don't say that to change the subject."

"I know," Agnes said. "Finish it."

---

After dinner, Agnes cleared the table and the dining room emptied in the way it always emptied: the guests dispersing by degrees, Vera moving toward the east parlor with the measured pace of a woman who has somewhere to be and considers haste beneath her, Marsh going wherever Marsh went after dinner, which Agnes had stopped tracking in 2004. The Calloway sisters went upstairs. Room 4 stayed at the table for three minutes after everyone else had gone, which he sometimes did, and then he was not there anymore, and Agnes was alone with the dishes.

She heard Carla in the east parlor. The particular sound of a notebook being opened.

Agnes washed the bowls. She dried them. She put them back in the cabinet and stood at the sink looking at the dark garden and the fog-lit harbor below, and she thought about the seven places she'd set and the one living person who'd sat at them, and she picked up the pot and washed that too.

She made tea. She put it on the small tray with the good cups, the ones that matched, and she carried it to the east parlor.

Carla was in the armchair by the window — not Vera's armchair, Agnes noted, the one on the other side of the hearth, which was fine — with a notebook open on her knee. The fire had burned down to coals and Agnes added two pieces of wood before she set down the tray. The room brightened, and in the brightening Agnes saw the notebook page.

It was a map.

Hand-drawn, careful, with the coastline rendered in the particular shorthand of someone who had looked at a lot of maps and had a good eye for scale. She had marked the harbor, the bluff road, the point. And along the edge of the harbor, following the contour she had apparently estimated from the topography, a line in red ink.

The fog line.

Agnes had been drawing that line in her own head for sixty years. She had never seen anyone else draw it.

She set down the teapot. "What are you looking for?"

Carla looked up. She had the expression of someone who had been waiting for this question and had prepared three different answers and was now deciding which one was true. She chose, Agnes thought, the true one.

"My brother came here in 2016," she said. "He never left." A pause. "Not died — I don't mean died. I mean he came here and he stopped leaving. He stopped calling, stopped responding, stopped — existing in any way I could reach. And everyone I talked to said he chose that, that he was troubled, that I was—" She stopped. "I've been looking at this place for two years from a distance. I needed to come see it."

Agnes poured the tea. She handed Carla a cup. She looked at the red line on the map, the 40-foot contour marked with the precision of someone who had done the math.

"Six hours," Agnes said.

Carla looked at her.

"You've been here six hours and you've mapped the fog line."

"I mapped it from the road before I checked in," Carla said, with a faint note of something approaching apology, or possibly pride, it was hard to tell. "I've been marking it in my notes for two years. Satellite imagery, mostly, but the resolution isn't—" She stopped herself. "I'm a researcher. It's a reflex."

"Your brother," Agnes said. "His name."

"Marcus Venn." Carla's hands were still around the tea cup. "He was thirty-one when he came here. He'd been — he'd had a difficult few years, which is why no one took me seriously. But he wasn't the kind of person who just disappears. He was the kind of person who called every Sunday. He called every Sunday for twenty-three years and then he stopped."

Agnes stood by the fire and let the warmth of it press against her back. The room smelled of tea and old wood and, faintly, the particular cold-salt draft that came from the direction of Vera's armchair, where Vera was now sitting and listening with the fixed attention she reserved for conversations she had decided were her business.

Agnes did not acknowledge Vera.

"I'll look into it," Agnes said. "In the morning."

Carla looked at her for a long moment. "You said that like you know something."

"I said it like I need to sleep on it," Agnes said. "Drink your tea. The bed in Room 3 is good."

She picked up the tray and left the east parlor before Carla could ask another question in the right direction.

---

The kitchen was quiet. Agnes stood at the counter and listened to the sound of the inn at night: the fire settling, the old wood of the walls contracting in the cold, the wind off the water pressing at the north-facing window over the sink. From upstairs, after a while, the sound of a door closing. Then nothing.

She stood there for a long time.

The chowder had been good. She knew it had been good because she'd tasted it and because Carla had eaten every drop and because the kelp did what the kelp did to living guests, which was to pull them down into sleep like a warm hand on the shoulder, and Carla would be asleep within the hour and would sleep until eight and would wake up feeling, as Harold Fitch had once written, *remarkably rested.* Agnes told herself she had made the good chowder because it was Friday and because she had a living guest and because she was a professional.

She straightened the wooden spoon on its rest.

"Ag."

Douglas was in the doorway. He had his mug and his paint-stained boots and the expression he wore when he'd been working up to something for several hours and had decided the time was now.

"I know," Agnes said.

"You don't, though." He came into the kitchen. He moved through the room the way he always had, with the unhurried certainty of a man who had built most of the shelves in it. "The thing is—"

"In the morning," Agnes said. "I'll look into the brother in the morning."

"Agnes."

She turned to the counter and began wiping it down. It was already clean.

"There are things I can look up," she said. "The guestbook goes back to '61. If he was here, he signed in. I'll find the entry and I'll—"

"Agnes." His voice had changed register. Not Ag. Agnes. "I need you to sit down."

"I'm not tired."

"That's not why I'm asking."

She kept wiping the counter. The cloth moved in the same small circle, the same patch of tile she had been cleaning for thirty years, the grout worn pale in the same places. She was aware of him watching her. She was aware of the weight of what he wasn't saying yet, how it filled the kitchen the way heat filled it, pressing down from the ceiling.

She reached out and straightened the fork at the edge of the counter. It had been straight already.

Douglas watched her do it.

The mug in his hand made a small, sharp sound, like something giving way, and Agnes did not look at it, but she heard it: the new crack, finding its line.

# Chapter Five: What the Fog Does

The mug in Douglas's hand had cracked, and Agnes had gone to bed anyway.

She lay in the dark of her quarters with her eyes open and the ceiling above her doing nothing helpful, and at two she gave up and made tea, and at three she was sitting at the small table by the west window with Douglas's current mug — number four, the one from the Vermont potter, the one with the thumb-groove worn into the handle from thirty-three years of a dead man's grip — and she was trying to think clearly.

The mug was hers now, for the hour. Douglas was somewhere in the east wing, or in the dining room, or standing in the garden the way he sometimes did when he was troubled, looking at the fence he'd been painting when he died. He would not go far. He never did.

She wrapped both hands around the mug and tried to think about Carla Venn the way she thought about any management problem: what did she want, what did she need, how long would she stay, what was the risk. This was a framework Agnes had developed over sixty years of running a house full of people who had complicated relationships with their own desires, and it worked. It had always worked.

It was not working now.

Because Carla Venn was not a management problem. She was a woman of thirty-four who had driven to the end of a peninsula without a reservation and asked for a room and eaten the chowder and gone to bed, and what she wanted was her brother, and what she needed was her brother, and how long she would stay depended entirely on whether Agnes helped her find him, and the risk — Agnes set the mug down.

The risk.

She knew something about brothers who disappeared. Not her own — she had no brothers — but the shape of it, the specific texture of a person who has been looking for so long they've stopped being able to tell the difference between looking and grieving. She had seen it in Carla's face at dinner, in the way the woman had eaten the chowder with the focused attention of someone who had forgotten to be hungry and was now catching up. Two years, Agnes calculated. Two years of driving and asking and being told there was nothing to find.

The tea went cold. Agnes did not drink it.

She was still sitting there at four when the quality of the dark outside the window changed — not lighter, not yet, but different, the way a room changes when someone opens a door in the next house over. Agnes stood, her knees registering the hour, and went to the window.

The fog was wrong.

She could see it from here, below the bluff road, pooled in the harbor basin the way it always was, but it was moving against the wind. The wind off the headland came from the northwest at this hour, had come from the northwest at this hour for as long as Agnes had been watching, and the fog was moving southeast, thickening as it went, piling up against the eastern shore of the harbor in slow deliberate rolls. Agnes had learned to read the fog over sixty years of watching it from this window and from the porch and from the bluff road, had learned its moods and its patterns and its particular vocabulary of warning, and what she read now made her put on her coat.

The porch was cold. October cold, the kind with teeth. Agnes stood at the railing and watched the fog for a long time.

It was denser than it had any right to be, and the color was wrong — not the soft gray-white of a normal night but something darker at its center, the color of old pewter, the color of the harbor water when the kelp beds were disturbed. And it was moving with a kind of intention Agnes did not have a word for. Not toward the inn. Not toward anything she could identify. More like the movement of a person pacing a room: back and forth, back and forth, with the energy of something that could not be still.

*Distress,* Agnes thought, and the word landed in her chest with a weight she hadn't expected.

She was still watching when she heard footsteps on the bluff road.

She turned. Gareth Croft was walking east along the road, hands in the pockets of his canvas jacket, at four in the morning, which should have surprised her and did not. Gareth walked when something was wrong in the Cove. She had watched him do it for thirty years, had seen him out here in November storms and February freezes and on the night in 2009 when three of the harbor boats had broken their moorings for no reason and drifted in slow circles until dawn. He walked the way a man walks when he is checking the perimeter of something he considers his.

He stopped when he saw her on the porch. He did not wave.

Agnes came down the porch steps and stood at the edge of the road. They were perhaps ten feet apart, and neither of them said anything for a moment. They both looked at the fog.

"You have a living guest," Gareth said.

Not a question.

"Yes," Agnes said.

The fog moved in its slow wrong direction below them. Gareth's face in the pre-dawn dark was unreadable, which was usually the case. He had a face built for conveying authority and not much else.

"Historically speaking," he said, "that's not something the Cove accommodates well."

"I'm aware."

He nodded, once, as though she'd confirmed something he'd already filed. His eyes stayed on the fog. "Young woman?"

Agnes said nothing.

"She'll have come about something," Gareth said. "They always come about something, the ones who find their way here. They're not tourists, the ones who find this place. You know that." He paused. "She'll have come about someone."

Agnes kept her face still. Sixty years of not letting Gareth Croft read her face.

"Some debts transfer with the deed, Agnes." He said it conversationally, the way he said everything, as though he were discussing the weather or the state of the road. "You knew that when you bought it."

The cold off the fog had found its way up the bluff. Agnes felt it at her wrists, at the back of her neck.

"I knew it," she said.

Gareth looked at her then, directly, and she held his gaze because she had been holding his gaze since 1994 and she was not going to stop now. Something moved in his expression — not softness, nothing so simple as that, but an acknowledgment. He had said what he came to say. The visit was complete.

He turned and walked back the way he'd come, east along the bluff road, and Agnes watched him until the dark took him.

She stood on the road for another minute. The fog rolled against the eastern shore of the harbor and rolled back. The wind came from the northwest and the fog moved southeast and the two facts sat next to each other like a sum that wouldn't balance.

She went back inside.

---

The east wing was quiet. Agnes walked the hall with the particular care of a woman who has spent decades learning which floorboards announce themselves, stepping over the third board from the staircase and the one outside Vera's room, her hand trailing along the wall. The sconces were off. The hall had the compressed stillness of a place where several people are asleep and she moved through it like water finding the gaps.

She stopped outside Room 4.

The door was closed. It was always closed. Agnes stood before it and listened.

Nothing. No sound from inside, no movement, no sign of anything. The hallway smelled of old wood and beeswax and the faint salt that lived in the bones of the building, and from Room 4 there was the additional smell Agnes had learned to identify over eleven years: ozone, faint but constant, as though something in the room was always about to happen.

She had her hand raised. Not quite to the door. Close enough to feel the air between her knuckles and the wood.

She had a living guest in Room 3. A woman who was looking for her brother. And in Room 4, behind this door, was — Agnes lowered her hand.

She stood there for a full minute. She counted it, because she had learned that the things she didn't do were worth marking, worth knowing the exact length of.

Sixty seconds.

Then she went to bed.

---

She slept. This surprised her. She had expected to lie awake until dawn running the same calculations, but her body made its own decision and she was under before she'd finished the thought, and when she woke, the room was gray with early light and something was wrong.

Agnes sat up.

Not smoke. Not gas. She checked both of these in the first two seconds because sixty years of running a house had made the check automatic, and it was neither. She was out of bed and standing before she'd decided to stand, her feet on the cold floor, her nose working.

Ozone.

Strong, unmistakable, the particular metallic sharpness she associated with her own ability — the smell that came after she held an anchor and asked, the smell that meant she'd pushed through to something and paid for it. She knew this smell the way she knew her own hands. She had not used her ability. She had not touched any anchor last night, had sat with Douglas's mug but not in the way that counted, had not asked and had not pushed.

The smell was coming from the east wing.

Agnes put on her cardigan and her slippers and went into the hall. The smell was stronger here. Not from Vera's room, not from any of the middle rooms. From the far end of the hall.

From Room 4.

She stood in the hallway and the ozone smell sat in her nose and she could feel, at the back of her skull, the ghost of a migraine that wasn't hers. As though whatever had happened in that room had cost something, and the cost was bleeding through the walls.

Room 4's door was closed. It was always closed.

Agnes stood in the hallway for a long moment. The inn was quiet around her, the particular pre-dawn quiet before the building woke itself up, before Douglas came into the kitchen and the other guests began their slow emergence into another day. The light through the east-wing window was the pale nothing-color of six a.m., and the smell of ozone was as clear as a bell.

She turned and went downstairs to start the coffee.

Her hands were steady. She made note of this. Her hands were steady and she was moving with purpose toward the kitchen and the ordinary morning, and the ozone smell faded as she descended, replaced by the cold-wood smell of the staircase and then the bread smell of the kitchen, the ghost of yesterday's baking still in the walls.

She filled the kettle. She set it on the burner. She stood with her hands flat on the counter and looked at the window above the sink, which showed her the garden and the fence and the pale sky beginning, very slowly, to consider the possibility of light.

Room 4 smelled of ozone without her. It had always smelled of ozone without her. She had noticed this eleven years ago and had filed it and had not examined the filing, because the filing led somewhere she had decided, eleven years ago, not to go.

Some debts transfer with the deed.

The kettle began its low moan toward boiling, and Agnes kept her hands flat on the counter and her eyes on the garden, and from upstairs — from the east wing, from the direction of Room 3 — there was no sound at all.

Carla Venn, sleeping the deep sleep of the kelp, would not be down until eight. Agnes had until eight. She had two hours and she would use them to think, to plan, to find some approach to the problem of a living woman looking for a lost brother that did not require Agnes to open a door she had been keeping closed for eleven years.

She would use the two hours.

She lifted her hands from the counter and reached for the coffee.

---

# Chapter Six: The Third Morning

The coffee finished brewing at 6:14 and Agnes poured a cup and stood at the kitchen window and watched the garden go gray and then pale and then the specific washed-out green of early October morning.

Carla Venn had not come down.

Agnes drank her coffee. She refilled it. She started the bread because starting the bread was what she did at six-fifteen regardless of anything else, and the rhythm of it — flour, salt, the particular give of the dough under her palms — was something she could hold onto while she waited. Carla had been down at five-fifty the first morning and five forty-three the second. Agnes had not taken note of this consciously, but she knew it the way she knew when the tide was wrong, the way she knew when the bread was two minutes from ready without looking at the clock.

Six-thirty came.

Agnes shaped the loaf and set it to rise and washed her hands at the sink and looked at the garden again. The fog was thick this morning, pressing against the garden fence. Something in her sternum had gone tight. She breathed around it, wiped her hands on her apron, and went upstairs.

---

The east wing in the early morning had its own particular silence — not peaceful, never peaceful, but the silence of a place holding its breath. Her footsteps on the runner were soft and she kept her hand near the wall without touching it, an old habit from decades of moving through the inn before the light came.

Room 1 was quiet. Room 2 had a faint sound through the door, a low murmur she recognized as Vera talking to herself, which Vera did every morning and would deny. Room 4's door was closed. It was always closed.

Room 3's door was open.

Not ajar. Open, fully, swung back against the interior wall, and from the hallway Agnes could see the gray rectangle of the window, and the window was open too, the curtains moving in the cold air coming off the water.

She put her hand on the doorframe.

The room smelled of sea salt and something older underneath it, something she had no name for because she had only smelled it once before, in 1991, in the garden, and she had not let herself name it then either. It was not rot. It was prior to rot. It was what the body knew before the mind caught up.

Carla Venn was in the bed.

The angle of her arm was wrong. Not fallen-asleep wrong — the wrongness went deeper than that, into the geometry of a body that had finished with the work of holding itself together. Her face was turned toward the window, toward the cold air and the moving curtain, and the color of her skin in the gray morning light was the color of the fog below the road. Agnes's hand tightened on the doorframe until she could feel the grain of the wood against her palm, each ridge of it, the small roughness where the paint had begun to lift.

There was no ambiguity here.

Agnes let herself look at the whole of it. The stillness of the chest. The particular way the bedclothes had settled. Thirty-four years old, she guessed — she had not asked, had not needed to, and now would not get to. The cold air moved the curtain again and Agnes felt it reach her across the room, that air, carrying iron and kelp and the faint sweetness that had no name.

The two notebooks were on the desk. The first was closed. The second — the one Carla had described on her first night as "the other category," in a tone that suggested she found the category both embarrassing and necessary — was open. Agnes could see, from the doorway, the ragged edge where a page had been torn out. Not carefully. The paper still fringed with small fibers along the spiral. Beneath the torn edge, the top of the remaining page held the faint impression of words — pressure from a pen, the ghost of what had been written on the page above. Agnes was not close enough to read it.

She was also aware, in the cold clear way she was aware of most things, that she needed to read it before the county arrived. Once the county was in the room, the room would belong to the county, and the county would not know what it was looking at.

She released the doorframe. She crossed to the desk in four steps, keeping her eyes from the bed — not from fear, but from respect, the same instinct that made her lower her voice in certain rooms. She bent over the notebook.

The impression was faint but legible in the gray window light. Four words, pressed hard into the page below, underlined twice.

*Room 4 knows everything.*

Agnes straightened. She looked at the window, at the moving curtain, at the cold morning beyond it. Her jaw had gone tight. She breathed through her nose until it loosened, then turned and left the room, pulling the door to behind her — not closed, not latched, but no longer open to the hall.

She went downstairs.

---

The front porch hit her with cold she hadn't prepared for. She hadn't put on her coat and she didn't go back for it. The air was sharp against her face and she walked to the railing and gripped it with both hands and looked out at the Cove.

The fog had risen above the bluff road.

She had watched the fog every morning for sixty-three years the way other people watched the news, looking for information, looking for what the day intended to be. It sat in the harbor basin, it pressed against the lower town, it swallowed the cannery and the market street and the slips. It did not climb the bluff.

It was here now, gray and thick and silent, pressing against the garden fence, above the road. The tops of the fence posts disappeared into it. Agnes looked at this for a long time — not stunned, not frozen, but the way you look at something you need to memorize because you will be asked about it later and the asking will matter.

Then she went inside.

At the front desk she opened the guestbook to the back pages and wrote in the small careful handwriting she used for things she needed to remember exactly: *Oct. 11. Fog above bluff road. First time. 6:42 a.m. Receded by —* and left the time blank.

She did not write what she thought it meant.

Her fingers found the spine of the guestbook instead, running along it once. She thought about the torn edge of Carla's notebook, the fibers of paper still caught in the spiral. Someone had wanted something removed. Someone had known exactly which page to take — and had not known, or not thought to check, what the pen had pressed through to the page below.

She closed the guestbook and went to the kitchen and picked up the telephone.

---

The county dispatcher had a voice Agnes recognized — a woman named Sandra who had been on the morning shift for fifteen years and who had taken Agnes's calls twice before, once for a chimney fire and once for a burst pipe, and who had both times been brisk and asked nothing unnecessary.

"Bellweather Inn, this is Agnes Murrow. I need to report a death."

A pause. The particular pause of someone shifting from one kind of attention to another.

"Can you tell me the nature of the situation, Ms. Murrow?"

"I have a guest who has passed overnight. It isn't natural causes." Agnes stood at the kitchen counter with her free hand flat on the cold tile. "I need someone to come."

She answered the questions. Name of the deceased, as far as she knew it. Room number. Whether she had touched anything. Whether there was anyone else in the building. This last one she paused on for a fraction of a second — long enough to be honest with herself about what she was doing — and then said, "No other guests currently."

Which was true, depending on your definitions.

Her free hand moved to the notepad on the counter and aligned it with the edge of the tile.

Sandra told her someone would be out within the hour, possibly longer given the road conditions. Agnes thanked her and hung up.

The bread was still rising on the counter, covered with a cloth, doing what bread did regardless of everything else. Agnes looked at it. She thought about putting it in the oven and decided against it, and set the thought down with the same deliberateness she'd set down the phone.

She was still standing there when Douglas came in.

He was wearing his paint-stained work clothes, which he always wore, and he was holding his mug. She could see a new crack along the handle from where she stood — a thin dark line that hadn't been there yesterday. He came to the counter and his hand came to rest perhaps three inches from hers on the tile, close as he could manage.

"Ag."

"I know."

"I heard you on the phone." He looked at her with his face doing the thing it did when he had already decided something and was waiting for her to catch up. "I've been trying to figure out how to —"

"Douglas."

He stopped.

"I need you to tell me," she said, "what you've been not telling me."

The fog pressed at the kitchen window. Not a sound — a pressure, the way the air changed when it was thick and close. The bread rose under its cloth. Douglas's mug had another hairline crack now, running parallel to the first, and she watched it spread another millimeter as she looked at it.

"I know," he said. "The thing is — I don't know where to start."

Agnes looked at her hand on the counter. She looked at the space between her hand and his.

"Start with Room 4," she said.

---

# Chapter Seven: Deputy Vale Arrives

The knock came at eight-fourteen.

Agnes knew because she had been watching the clock above the dining room mantel, had watched the minute hand crawl from the twelve toward the three in the particular way it crawled when she wanted it to move faster, which was the same way it moved when she wanted it to stop. She had made coffee — not for herself, out of institutional reflex, the same reflex that had been running her hands through tasks all morning while her mind sat somewhere else entirely. The coffee was hot and smelled right and she had not drunk any of it.

She went to the front door.

The woman on the porch was younger than Agnes expected, though Agnes had stopped being surprised by how young official people were getting. Thirty-eight, perhaps. Brown hair braided back with the kind of efficiency that suggested she'd done it in a car mirror. The uniform fit her like a decision. She was looking at the inn the way Agnes had seen surveyors look at property lines — not admiring it, not judging it, reading it. Her gaze went to the porch boards, the window trim, the fog-dampened wood of the eaves, and came back to Agnes with something filed away behind it.

"Ms. Murrow." Not a question. "Deputy Nora Vale, Kettle Falls. You called in a death this morning."

"I did." Agnes stepped back from the door. "Come in."

Deputy Vale came in. She paused inside the threshold in a way that was not quite hesitation — she was taking the room, the entry, the smell of old wood and coffee and the faint persistent salt of the sea through every wall. Her hand went briefly to the notepad at her hip. Not opening it. Touching it.

Agnes gave her the abbreviated version in the entry hall, standing, because sitting would have suggested they had time. Guest arrived two nights ago, no reservation, paid in cash. Found her this morning at six-forty. No sign of forced entry. No other living guests in the inn.

Nora Vale listened without writing anything down. When Agnes finished, she said: "Walk me through the morning you found her. Not the summary — the actual morning."

Agnes looked at her.

"The whole morning," Nora said. "Start where you started."

So Agnes walked her through it. She was precise — the time she rose, the order of the kitchen tasks, the bread she had not put in the oven, the reason she had gone upstairs at all. She was truthful about the facts. She was silent about Vera at the end of the hall, about the smell that had been wrong since before she reached the door, about the way the fog had been sitting above the bluff road for the first time in her living memory and what she thought it meant. She described the open window, the chill in the room, the position of the body, the two notebooks on the desk.

Nora took notes without looking at her notepad.

Agnes noticed this the way she noticed when a dough was wrong before she could say why — a small wrongness, a competence she hadn't accounted for.

"The window," Nora said. "Second floor, north-facing. You said it was open."

"It was."

"You said the latch was intact."

"It was."

"So it opened from inside."

"Or it was never latched." Agnes kept her voice even, the way she kept a flame even under something delicate. "I don't know how it opened. I noticed it was open."

Nora wrote something. "The torn page. You mentioned a torn page from the second notebook."

"I noticed a page had been removed. The edge was still in the binding."

"Did you touch either notebook?"

"No."

Nora looked at her. Not suspiciously — Agnes had been looked at suspiciously before, and this wasn't it. This was assessment, the same reading she'd given the porch boards, the same filing-away. Then she wrote something.

Agnes had cooked for sixty years and could read a room the way most people read a face. She understood, standing in her own entry hall, that she was doing less well at this than she had expected to do.

---

Carla's room smelled of cold air and the particular staleness of a space where something had happened and the air hadn't been exchanged since. Agnes stood in the doorway. Nora moved through the room with a systematic quiet, touching nothing she hadn't photographed first, her phone out and doing the work of a camera with the brisk efficiency of someone who had done this enough times to stop thinking about the doing.

The two notebooks were still on the desk. Agnes watched Nora find them.

The first notebook — the facts one, Carla's documented observations, the handwriting Agnes had seen over Carla's shoulder at dinner two nights ago — Nora paged through it with gloved hands, her face doing nothing, and then bagged it. She looked at the second notebook. She opened it.

Agnes watched her look for the torn page.

The page was gone. Agnes had known it would be gone — had known it since she stood in the kitchen this morning with her hand flat on the counter. The torn edge was still there, a ragged inch of paper in the binding, but the page itself had not been in this room when Agnes found Carla, and it was not in this room now.

"You said you saw the torn page," Nora said.

"I saw the edge. I saw a page had been removed."

"Before or after you found the body."

"I noticed the notebook after. The edge was visible from where I was standing." Agnes kept her hands at her sides. Her fingers wanted the doorframe. She didn't let them have it. "I didn't go through the notebooks."

Nora bagged the second notebook. She stood for a moment looking at the north wall, the wall shared with the room next door. Her eyes moved along it in a way that had nothing to do with the wallpaper.

"You looked at the wall when I asked about your other guests," she said. "Downstairs. When you said there were no other living guests. You looked at that wall."

Agnes had not known she had done this.

"What's on the other side of that wall?" Nora asked.

The room was very cold. The window was closed now — Agnes had closed it herself before calling the county, because she was not going to leave a dead woman in a cold room for the hours it took a deputy to drive up from Kettle Falls — but the cold had stayed. It had settled into the plaster and the floorboards and the particular pocket of air near the desk, and it was not going anywhere.

"Room 4," Agnes said.

Nora wrote it down.

The fog pressed at the window glass, gray-white and close, and Agnes stood in the doorway of a dead woman's room and understood with the clarity of something long-postponed that she was not, in fact, managing this.

Nora looked up from her notepad. "I'll need to speak with your other guests."

"They're not available at the moment."

"Ms. Murrow." Nora's voice was not unkind. It had the particular patience of someone who had said a version of this many times. "I understand this is your home. I understand you have your own ways of handling things here. I'm not interested in making this harder than it needs to be. But a woman is dead in your inn and someone killed her, and that person is still somewhere." A pause. "I'll need to speak with everyone in the inn."

"I understand," Agnes said.

She did. She understood completely. She also understood that everyone in the inn was a category with specific complications that Nora Vale's notepad did not have a column for, and she stood with that knowledge the way she stood with many things — squarely, without visible distress, while it pressed against the backs of her eyes.

She went downstairs to make more coffee.

---

Nora was at the dining room table when Agnes came back with the pot. She had spread her initial notes across the table's end — not messy, organized in the specific way of someone accustomed to working in temporary spaces, making order out of available surface. The fireplace was going; Agnes had lit it before the deputy arrived, and the oak log she'd put on had caught properly and was putting out a steady heat. The room smelled of coffee and wood smoke and, faintly, the rosemary that had burned off the pan she'd left on the stove yesterday and never quite cleared.

Agnes set the mug down at Nora's right. She moved to take the pot back to the kitchen.

"She came here looking for her brother." Nora said it almost to herself, the way people said things they were still deciding what to do with. Her eyes were on her notes, not on Agnes. "That's in the notebook."

Agnes went very still.

The fire cracked. The fog moved against the dining room windows, slow and purposeful, and the room was warm and smelled of good coffee and Agnes stood with the pot in her hand and did not move for the length of a long breath.

"What was his name?" she said.

Nora looked up.

There was a moment — brief, specific — where the deputy's face did the thing faces did when something landed differently than expected. She had not anticipated the question. Agnes noted this, filed it, kept her own face doing nothing.

"Venn," Nora said. "Marcus Venn. You know him?"

Agnes looked at her. The fire put orange light across the table, across the notepad, across Nora's grandmother's ring on her right hand — Agnes noticed the ring, had noticed it at the door, had not known what to make of it and had set it aside. She looked at the deputy's face, which was waiting, which was patient, which was the face of someone who had learned to use silence as a tool.

"That's a question," Agnes said.

She went back to the kitchen.

The bread was still on the counter under its cloth, still rising, still doing what bread did. Agnes set the coffeepot on the stove and stood with her back to the door and listened to the sounds of the inn around her — the fire in the dining room, the creak of the east wing settling against the morning wind, the faint persistent sound of the fog against the north windows, which was not a sound exactly but a pressure, the way the air changed.

Marcus Venn.

She knew the name. She had known it for eleven years, in the way she knew many things she had filed in the drawer she did not open — she knew it the way she knew the shape of a crack in a wall she passed every day without looking at it directly. She had heard it once, a long time ago, in a context she had not examined since. She had heard it and she had put it away and she had made breakfast.

Marcus Venn.

She put her hand flat on the counter.

In the dining room, Nora Vale was writing up her initial notes, and the coffee Agnes had brought her was steaming, and the fire was going well, and a woman named Carla Venn had come to this inn looking for her brother and was dead in an upstairs room, and the torn page from her second notebook was not in the notebook and not in the room, and Room 4 was on the other side of a wall, and Agnes stood in her kitchen with her hand on the cold tile and understood, with the specific clarity of something she had been not-understanding for a very long time, that she needed to find Vera.

---

# Chapter Eight: What Vera Saw

Agnes found her in the east parlor at half past eight, in the chair by the window, with the particular stillness of someone who had been waiting long enough to stop fidgeting about it.

Vera's anchor was the chair itself — a wingback in faded green wool that Agnes had reupholstered twice, each time to the same specifications, because the specifications mattered. Vera's hands were folded in her lap. The morning light came through the curtains at a low angle and passed through her at the wrist, which happened more in winter.

Agnes sat down across from her.

The east parlor smelled of woodsmoke and the faint vanilla Agnes had put into yesterday's cake, which Vera had spent the better part of the afternoon near without acknowledging. The fire had burned down to coals. Agnes could hear the fog against the north windows — not a sound, a pressure, the way the air thickened and then thinned again — and she could hear, from upstairs, the particular quality of silence that meant Deputy Vale was awake and moving carefully, the way people moved in houses they didn't yet understand.

Agnes looked at the table between them. A fork, displaced from some previous arrangement. She put her hands in her lap.

"You are frightened, Agnes." Vera's voice was the same as it had been in 1974, which was to say precise, with the cadence of someone who had once been told she spoke too quickly and had overcorrected. "I can tell because you've straightened that fork four times. Sit down. I'll tell you what I saw the night the living woman arrived, and you will not interrupt me."

Agnes had not moved to touch the fork. She looked at it. She looked at Vera.

She sat.

---

"I was in the corridor near Room Three," Vera said. "This was around midnight, perhaps a little past. I don't sleep, as you know, and I find the late hours — manageable. There is less noise. I prefer less noise." A small pause. "The living woman had arrived that evening. I had observed her at dinner. She ate quickly and looked at the walls more than her food, which I find unnerving in a guest, but I understand some people are like that."

Agnes waited.

"I was in the corridor. And the door to Room Four opened."

The fire shifted, one coal dropping against another with a small sound like a word cut short. Agnes kept her hands in her lap.

"I want to be clear," Vera said, "because I understand this is relevant. It opened. Not ajar, not the latch misbehaving. Opened. He stood in the doorway for some time — I would estimate three minutes, though I acknowledge my sense of duration is not what it was — and he looked toward Room Three. He did not come out into the corridor. He stood in the doorway and he looked, and then he went back inside and the door closed."

Vera's hands remained folded. She looked at the middle distance, which was the window, which was the fog.

"I have been in this inn for fifty years," she said. "He has never opened that door. Not once."

Agnes thought of the door. She had passed it ten thousand times. She had knocked on it when she needed to, and it had opened for her, but she had never seen it open on its own initiative — and there was a word for the distinction, she thought, between a door that opened when asked and a door that opened because it chose to. She didn't have the word. She felt its shape.

"Did he see you?" Agnes asked.

Vera considered this with the seriousness it deserved. "I don't believe so. I was near the linen cupboard. I was not — I was not expecting to be a witness to anything, and I may have made myself less present than usual." A fractional pause. "I am capable of that."

"I know," Agnes said.

This was true. Vera had a talent for it — a kind of controlled dimming — that Agnes had always assumed was temperament and now suspected was something else. A practice developed over fifty years. A way of being in a hallway without being seen.

Vera unfolded her hands and refolded them, which was the most agitation Agnes had seen from her in a decade.

"There is something else," Vera said.

Agnes waited.

"You will want to know who he is. Who is in that room." Vera's eyes moved to Agnes's face and then away. "I assumed you knew. I assumed you had always known."

"I know what room he's in," Agnes said.

A long pause. Outside, a gull called once and went quiet. The fog pressed against the glass.

"Yes," Vera said. "Well." She was quiet for a moment. "I died in that room, Agnes."

Agnes heard the fire. She heard the fog. She heard, distantly, floorboards on the second floor as Deputy Vale moved from the bathroom to the corridor — she knew the inn's sounds the way she knew her own breathing, could map the deputy's path without looking up.

"I have never told you," Vera continued, "because it seemed impolite. There is a correct way to discuss these things and I was not — I had not found the correct way, and then fifty years passed, and I had maintained, for all of that time, that I have always been in my current room. I moved myself. After. I thought it was better." A pause. "I thought it was tidier."

Agnes looked at her. Vera's face was what it always was — composed, slightly severe, the face of a woman who had decided, somewhere in her fifties, that composure was a form of armor and had never taken it off. Agnes looked at the small tension around Vera's mouth and understood it had cost something to say this. Fifty years of maintained fiction, offered up on a Tuesday morning in November.

"Why are you telling me now?" Agnes asked.

Vera met her eyes.

"Because something has changed," she said, "and I think you need to know what the room is before you find out who's in it."

Agnes sat with this for a moment. The coal in the fireplace had gone gray at the edges. She looked at it.

"All right," she said.

Vera unfolded her hands and smoothed them against her skirt — a gesture Agnes recognized as the end of something. The morning light shifted, the fog thinning slightly outside, and for a moment Vera was more present than usual, her outline sharp against the chair, her green wool cardigan almost solid.

"The fork," Vera said, "is on the wrong side."

Agnes looked at the table. The fork was where it had been.

She left it there.

---

The kitchen was cold when she came back to it — she'd left the north window cracked and the morning air off the water had come in and settled. She closed the window. She washed her hands. She got the bread dough from under its cloth, where it had been rising since six, and she turned it onto the floured board and she began to work it.

The physical fact of dough was something she had relied on for sixty years and she relied on it now. The resistance of it, the way it pushed back against her palms and then gave, then pushed back again. She worked the heel of her hand into it and folded it and turned it. Worked and folded and turned. The kitchen smelled of flour and yeast and the faint iron of the sea coming through the seal on the north window, which she needed to replace before December.

Vera had died in Room 4.

Agnes pressed her palms flat against the dough and held them there.

The room had been there before Vera. The room had been there before Agnes. The room was part of the original east wing, which sat on the Quiet Town foundations, which were older than the county survey by two hundred years, which Agnes had known when she bought the inn and had not examined because the stone was good and the price was right and she had needed somewhere to put herself.

What the room was: a room.

What the room was: the place Vera had died, and then moved away from, and then a man had arrived and settled into it and had never once opened the door in response to anything Agnes had seen — not the Anniversary Week, not the bad November of 2009 when two of the other guests had a disagreement that shook the plaster off the hallway ceiling, not any of it — until a living woman arrived and checked into the room next door.

She folded the dough.

*She was looking for her brother.*

He had said it to her four days ago, in the corridor, unprompted, and she had filed it in the drawer she did not open. She had filed it and made breakfast.

Agnes pressed her hands into the dough and thought of the name Nora Vale had said across the dining room table. Marcus Venn. The orange firelight. The notepad. The ring on the deputy's right hand, which Agnes had noticed and set aside.

She had known the name for eleven years. She had heard it once, in a context she had not examined since. She had heard it and she had —

"You talked to Vera."

Douglas materialized near the pantry door. He was more solid than usual in the mornings, when the kitchen was warm and smelled of things he'd loved, and he had his coffee mug and his paint-stained trousers and his boots, which she had stopped arguing about.

"Yes," Agnes said.

He was quiet for a moment. She could feel him watching her hands on the dough.

"Ag —"

"Not yet," she said. "Let me think."

He stayed. He didn't push — Douglas had never pushed, which was one of the things she had loved about him and one of the things she had, in her more honest moments, used. He stood near the pantry and held his mug and watched her work the dough, and the kitchen was warm, and the fire she'd built up before going to find Vera was crackling steadily in the small hearth, and the fog pressed against the north window with its low, persistent patience.

She worked the dough. She folded and turned and pressed. Outside, the fog thinned another degree and the light in the kitchen shifted from gray to the particular pale gold of a November morning doing its best. She could hear the east wing settling into the day around her — the creak of the staircase, the distant sound of a door, the specific rhythm of the house breathing.

Room 4 had opened his door.

He had stood in the doorway and looked toward Room 3 and then gone back inside. Vera, who had fifty years of careful observation and no reason to exaggerate, had said: *not once, not ever.* And Agnes believed her, not because Vera was incapable of exaggeration but because Vera was incapable of this particular kind. She had no investment in Room 4 being unusual. She had, if anything, an investment in not thinking about him at all.

Agnes shaped the dough into its loaf and set it in the pan and covered it again. She washed her hands. She stood at the sink and looked at the fog and let herself hold the two things she now knew, side by side, without yet looking at what they made together.

Vera had died in Room 4.

Room 4 had opened his door for the first time in fifty years when Carla Venn arrived.

The drawer she did not open had two things in it now, and they were beginning to press against the wood.

Douglas was still near the pantry. She could feel him the way she always could — a slight drop in temperature, a presence in her peripheral vision, the specific quality of being watched by someone who was worried about her and trying not to show it.

"It's going to be a good loaf," he said finally.

Agnes looked at the covered pan. The dough was doing what it did — rising in the warm kitchen, slow and inevitable, the yeast working in the dark.

"Mm," she said.

She put the kettle on. She got down two mugs — one for herself, one habit she had never broken — and she stood with her back to Douglas and listened to the inn around her. Upstairs, Deputy Vale's footsteps moved toward the staircase. The deputy was coming down for breakfast. She would have her notepad. She would have her questions.

Agnes looked at the bread.

She looked at the drawer she did not open.

She looked at the bread again.

*Before you find out who's in it,* Vera had said, with the particular emphasis of someone delivering a warning they were not sure would be heeded. Agnes had sat with that emphasis in the east parlor and she sat with it now. Vera had given her something and Vera would not acknowledge having given it, and Agnes would not ask her to, and the fifty years of maintained fiction would go on being maintained because some things deserved that courtesy.

The kettle began to sound. Agnes poured it.

She set Douglas's mug on the staff table, where his place had been set since a Thursday in 1991 when she had done it by reflex and then stood very still for a long time and then gone to sit on the garden fence and look at the fog until she understood what she was going to do about the rest of her life.

The bread rose in its pan. The fire cracked. The fog thinned another small degree.

Agnes heard the deputy reach the bottom of the stairs.

She picked up her tea.

# Chapter Nine: The Researcher's Notes

The dining room at the Bellweather smelled of last night's fire and this morning's bread, and Nora had commandeered the far end of the long table before seven, spreading Carla Venn's first notebook open beside her own legal pad and her third cup of coffee, which had gone cold while she was reading.

The notebook was meticulous. Not the meticulous of someone performing thoroughness for an audience, but the meticulous of someone who had taught herself a system and trusted it — color-coded tabs at the page edges, a key on the inside front cover, marginal notations in two different pen colors with a legend explaining which color meant confirmed and which meant *consistent with but unverified*. Nora had worked with professional researchers who weren't this organized. She had worked with detectives who weren't this organized.

Carla Venn had spent two years on Heron Cove from a distance. The first forty pages of the notebook established this in careful layers: county deed records going back to 1791, cross-referenced with census data, cross-referenced again with the historical society's catalog of primary documents. Three FOIA requests, their response dates noted, the responsive documents summarized and flagged. A map of the fog line, hand-drawn, with the 40-foot elevation contour marked in red pen and a note in the margin: *corresponds almost exactly — not coincidence, topographic feature or topographic consequence?*

Nora wrote: *C.V. — systematic. Two-year research history. Not amateur. Not random.*

She underlined *not random* twice and looked at it.

A serious researcher with a documented methodology and two years of groundwork did not end up dead by coincidence. Nora had believed this when she drove into Heron Cove three days ago and she believed it more now, with the notebook open in front of her. The question was not whether Carla Venn had found something worth killing over. The question was what she had found and who had known she found it.

She turned to page forty-three, where the notebook's register shifted. Less historical society, more personal. A section headed *Marcus — timeline* in Carla's quick handwriting, the letters slightly compressed, like someone writing faster than they meant to.

Nora's pen hovered.

She wrote: *Background check. Marcus Venn. Last known location Heron Cove, 2016.*

She drew a box around it.

---

Agnes came in at half past nine with a plate of toast Nora hadn't asked for, set it at Nora's elbow without comment, and began straightening the sideboard with the particular efficiency of someone who had been straightening sideboards since before Nora was born.

"I have some questions about Ms. Venn's time here," Nora said. "The two days before she died."

"I expected you would."

Agnes turned from the sideboard. She didn't sit. She stood with her hands folded in front of her apron and looked at Nora with those gray eyes, and Nora had the brief, specific sensation of being assessed by someone whose assessment would be accurate and not necessarily comfortable.

"Walk me through her first evening. What time she arrived, what she ate, who she spoke to."

Agnes walked her through it. She was precise — times, observations, the specific words Carla had used when she asked about the town's history, the order in which she had eaten her dinner. She answered every question Nora asked. She volunteered nothing.

Nora noticed this. She wrote down the answers and she noticed the shape of what surrounded them, the way you notice a room by its walls and not only its furniture.

"Did she seem distressed? Nervous?"

"She seemed tired. She'd driven a long way."

"Did she interact with any of the other guests?"

A pause. Not long — a beat, maybe two. "She sat at the table with them at dinner."

"And?"

"She asked Mr. —" Agnes stopped. "She asked one of the long-term residents about the fog. He told her it came in from the northwest. She wrote it down."

"I'd like to speak with the long-term residents."

"They're not available."

Nora set her pen down. She looked at Agnes, who looked back at her with the same level attention, not defensive, not apologetic, simply present in the way of someone who has held a position for a long time and does not find holding it uncomfortable.

"Mrs. Murrow," Nora said. "I understand the town has its own ways of handling things. I'm not here to change that. I'm here because a woman is dead and someone killed her, and that person is still somewhere."

Agnes was quiet for a moment.

"I know," she said.

Something in it made Nora look up from her notepad. Not the words. The weight under them — the particular weight of a statement made by someone who has been living with a knowledge for days and has not yet decided what to do with it.

Agnes picked up Nora's cold coffee cup, replaced it with the fresh one she'd brought on the tray, and left the dining room without another word.

Nora looked at the door for a moment. Then she picked up her pen.

She wrote: *Agnes M. — answers all questions. Volunteers nothing. Knows more than she's saying. Not obstructing — something else. Protecting?*

She looked at the word. Then she crossed out the question mark and left the word standing on its own.

---

By afternoon the fire had settled into a steady low burn and the fog outside the dining room windows had thickened to the point where the garden was a suggestion of shapes rather than a garden. Nora had moved through the first notebook and halfway into the second. She had eaten the toast at some point without noticing. Her legal pad had two and a half pages of notes and a growing column of questions she couldn't yet answer.

She was aware, in the peripheral and comfortable way of someone absorbed in work, that she wasn't alone in the room. She had been aware of this for some time — the sense of a presence at the other end of the table, the occasional sound of a chair shifting, the way the air in the room felt occupied rather than empty. She had assumed, without examining the assumption, that it was one of the other guests Agnes had mentioned. Long-term residents. She had assumed a local who had come in from the cold.

The man had asked her, at some point — she couldn't pin down exactly when — what she was working on. She had told him, in the abbreviated way she talked about cases to civilians: a woman found on the shore, a research trip gone wrong, trying to build a timeline. He had listened carefully. He had asked thoughtful questions. He had the manner of someone who had spent a long time paying attention to the world and found it mostly interesting rather than threatening.

He had mentioned, unprompted, that the fog had been unusual the morning the living woman was found. *Came up above the road,* he had said. *Haven't seen that in my time here.* She had written it down.

She looked at her legal pad now.

*Fog — rose above bluff road morning body discovered. Unusual per local resident (Douglas, long-term). Not in any report. Weather service? Check.*

She looked at the name.

She looked at the chair at the other end of the table.

It was empty. The chair was pushed in slightly at an angle, the way a chair looks when someone has recently left it, except the fire had been burning steadily and the room had been warm and she had not heard the door open or close. She had not heard footsteps. She had not heard the particular sound of a chair being pushed back from a table.

Nora set her pen down.

She looked at the empty chair for what was probably ten seconds and felt like longer.

She looked at her notepad. She had written down everything the man said. Four separate entries, distributed through her afternoon's notes, interspersed with observations about Carla's notebook as if the conversation had been continuous, woven through her work. She read them back. The observations were specific and useful. The fog detail was the kind of thing a witness says and doesn't know is significant.

She looked at the chair again.

The fire cracked. Outside, the fog pressed against the glass.

Nora turned back to her legal pad and wrote, beneath the fog entry: *Douglas — confirm identity. Long-term resident per Agnes.*

She stared at the name. She had written it down because he had told her. *Douglas,* he had said, when she asked, and she had written it without looking up from the page.

She wrote it again on a fresh line, deliberately: *Douglas.*

She did not yet understand what she had written. She capped her pen, picked up the cold coffee Agnes had replaced an hour ago, and found it had gone cold again.

---

The call to Kettle Falls went through on the third try. The signal in the inn was unreliable in the afternoons — something about the fog, Agnes had told her, with the brisk tone of someone reporting a plumbing quirk rather than a meteorological impossibility.

"Vale." The dispatcher's voice came through with the particular flatness of a connection that was technically working.

"It's Nora. I need a background check run. Marcus Venn, male, last known location Heron Cove, 2016. He's the victim's brother — went off-grid around that time, no missing persons filed, I want to know why." She paused. "Also pull anything on Carla Venn's research history. Publications, affiliations, anything she filed officially in the last two years."

"Copy. Anything else?"

Nora looked at her notepad. At the window, at the fog.

"Yeah. One more thing." She kept her voice level. Procedural. "Has anyone at the office worked a Heron Cove call before? Prior to this one."

Silence on the line. Not the silence of a bad connection — the silence of someone deciding something.

"Deputy Vale." The dispatcher's voice had shifted, slightly. Something careful in it. "Everyone who volunteers for Heron Cove calls comes back with stories."

Nora's pen was in her hand. She hadn't picked it up consciously. "What kind of stories?"

Another silence. Longer.

"The kind," the dispatcher said, "where you're not sure if you're the one who's confused."

The connection held for another moment, and then it didn't. Nora pulled the phone from her ear and looked at the screen, which showed the call as ended. She set it face-down on the table.

Outside, the fog moved against the glass in slow patterns that bore no relationship to the wind direction. She watched it for a moment. She had been watching the fog do this for three days and she had been filing it, each time, under *weather patterns, coastal, unfamiliar.* The file was getting full.

She picked up her pen. She turned to a fresh page of her legal pad.

At the top she wrote: *What I know.*

Below it: *Carla Venn. Researcher. Systematic. Two-year investigation into Heron Cove, motivated by brother's disappearance 2016. Arrived without reservation. Dead by morning three. Cause of death: exposure, possible secondary factors pending full report. No weapon. No obvious suspect. No witnesses.*

She looked at the list.

She wrote: *What I don't know.*

The column below this one was longer. She wrote quickly, not pausing to consider — the way she'd been trained to do in the early stages, before the evidence started narrowing, when the job was to get everything onto the page and trust that the shape of it would emerge.

*Who knew she was coming. Whether her research led directly to threat. Marcus Venn — location, status, connection to death. The long-term residents — who they are, why Agnes is protective of them, why they're not available for interview. The fog on the morning of the death. Douglas.*

She stopped.

She crossed out *Douglas* and wrote it again. Then she underlined it.

She had been a deputy for eleven years. She had a record of closed cases she was proud of and a methodology she had refined over a decade of work, and the methodology had a specific shape: you gathered evidence, you built a timeline, you found where the timeline broke and you pressed on the break until it opened. It was not complicated. It was not glamorous. It worked.

She looked at the underlined name.

She looked at the empty chair.

The fire settled. A log shifted, and a brief shower of sparks rose and died against the back of the grate, and the room was warm and smelled of old wood and good bread and, very faintly, beneath both of those things, something she couldn't name — something cold and low, like the smell of the harbor at low tide carried up the hill on a wind that wasn't currently blowing.

Nora closed her legal pad.

She sat for a moment with her hands flat on the cover of it, feeling the texture of the cardboard, the slight ridge of the spiral binding. Grounding herself, the way her training said to do when a scene wasn't making sense — come back to the physical, come back to what you can verify, work outward from there.

She had a notebook full of a dead woman's research. She had an innkeeper who answered every question and volunteered nothing. She had a name she had written down four times and a chair that was empty in a way she could not account for.

She had a dispatcher in Kettle Falls who had gone quiet at the mention of this town, and the quiet had said more than the words around it.

Nora opened her legal pad again to the page with Douglas's name on it. She stared at it for a long time. Then, underneath it, she wrote: *72 hours.*

She had been in Heron Cove for seventy-one.

Upstairs, in the east wing, a door opened and closed softly. Footsteps moved down the hall — deliberate, unhurried — and then stopped. The inn settled around her, the old wood doing what old wood did in the cooling afternoon, contracting slightly, ticking in the walls. Nora knew the sound of a building. She had spent eleven years learning to distinguish the sounds buildings made from the sounds people made inside them.

These footsteps had stopped at the top of the stairs.

They had not come down.

She picked up Carla Venn's second notebook and opened it to where she'd left off, and she read, and the fire burned low, and she did not look at the staircase, and the name Douglas sat on her legal pad in her own handwriting and waited for her to understand it.

# Chapter Ten: Below the Fog Line

The fog was moving wrong again.

Agnes stood at the edge of the bluff road with her coat buttoned to the chin and watched it. Below her, the harbor basin held its usual gray-white mass, thick enough to lose your hand in if you reached down far enough — she had tested this once, in 1974, purely out of curiosity, and had not repeated the experiment. But the fog this morning was not pooling the way it should. It was drifting uphill in slow, deliberate tongues, pulling back before it reached the road's edge, then pressing forward again. Patient. Like something testing a fence.

She had noted this in the margin of the guestbook the morning after Carla died, when it had risen above the bluff road entirely. Now it was behaving like something between its usual self and that. In-between, she thought, and did not like the word.

She shifted her bag to her other shoulder — two thermoses of coffee, one for her, one for Bertrand — and started down the hill.

The fog took her at the forty-foot mark, right where it always did. The change was immediate and particular: the light softened, arriving from no specific direction, and the sounds of the upper town — a gull, the distant clank of her own garden gate in the wind — came back to her half a beat late, as if the air here processed things on a slight delay. She had always noticed this below the fog line. She had never entirely stopped noticing it, which meant it still unsettled her, which was probably healthy.

The dead moved freely through the market street at this hour. She passed the fish seller — a woman named Hedda who had died in 1987 and still kept her usual Tuesday pitch near the covered stalls — and nodded, and Hedda nodded back and said something to the living man on her right side in the same breath, the easy bilingual fluency of someone who had been doing it for decades. Two children ran across the cobblestones ahead of Agnes. One of them was wearing clothes from the wrong century; Agnes registered this without breaking stride. The harbor market had always been this way, below the fog line. The softened time made it easier. The dead were less effortful here, less translucent, more themselves. She had always appreciated the market for that, even when she found it difficult to look at.

She bought bread from the inland stall without stopping to talk. The baker, who was living and knew her, handed it over with a look at the thermoses and said, "Bertrand?" and she said yes, and he said, "Tell him the mackerel's been running," and she said she would.

The cannery district was quieter. It was always quieter — the living had little business here, and the dead who anchored to the old buildings tended toward solitude. Agnes had not come down here often in the thirty years since Bertrand had stopped accepting visitors from most people. He accepted her. She had never taken this for granted.

The cannery itself sat at the end of the district road, a long low building of corrugated iron and old timber, its paint so weathered it had become a color with no name. The smell reached her before the building did: rust, old salt, and underneath both of those something she had never successfully identified in sixty years of trying. Not rot. Something older than rot. Something preserved.

The side door was unlocked, as it always was.

Inside, the light came through the high industrial windows in slanted bars, catching the dust that lived permanently in the air of the place. The floor was original — wide boards, dark with decades of fish oil and salt water — and Agnes's footsteps on it were the only sound. She walked past the old processing lines, the equipment cold and still and furred with rust, and into the back room where Bertrand sat.

He was in his chair. He was always in his chair. It was a plain wooden thing, a foreman's chair, positioned at the exact angle it had occupied for forty-six years, facing the window that looked out over the harbor slips. He did not look up when she entered. He rarely did.

Agnes pulled the second stool from against the wall, set it across from him, and sat. She opened the first thermos and poured two cups, even though Bertrand couldn't drink his, because the ritual mattered. She put his cup on the crate beside his chair. He looked at it.

"Good coffee," he said.

"Same as always."

"Mm." He looked back at the window. The fog pressed against the glass outside, pale and close. "You came a long way for a Tuesday."

"I wanted to ask you about 1923."

A pause. Not surprise — Bertrand didn't do surprise — but a settling, the way a building settles when the temperature drops. He picked up his coffee cup and held it without drinking from it, and Agnes waited, and the cannery held its particular quiet around them.

"The survey man," he said, finally. "Yes. He came back every summer." Another pause. "Some things you see once and can't stop seeing."

"What did he see?"

Bertrand turned the cup in his hands. Outside, something moved in the fog — a shadow, boat-shaped, gone before Agnes could fix on it.

"There was a woman," he said. "1923. She came looking for someone, same as your living woman."

Agnes kept her hands around her thermos and her face still. "What happened to her?"

"She found what she was looking for."

The cannery ticked around them. Agnes could hear the harbor, muffled by fog, the low knock of a hull against a piling somewhere out in the gray.

"And then?"

"She didn't leave."

"She's still in the Cove?"

Bertrand was quiet for a moment. "Not the way you mean."

Agnes looked at her coffee. The surface of it was perfectly still, which meant her hands were steady, which was good, because the rest of her was not. "Is she a drifter?"

Bertrand set his cup down on the crate. He looked at the window. He said nothing.

Which was yes.

Agnes thought about the four names on her list. She had maintained that list for thirty years. She had been wrong, then, about at least one of them — wrong about how long the list had been necessary, wrong about the shape of the problem. A woman from 1923, untethered for a hundred years. A hundred years of cold drafts and low-tide smells and silence.

She drank her coffee. It was good. She had always made good coffee, which was a small thing and not nothing.

"I want to ask you about the floor," she said.

Bertrand looked at her then. His face was the face of a man who had been 71 for 46 years, which meant it held the particular stillness of someone who had stopped being surprised by questions and started simply deciding which ones to answer.

"What's under it," Agnes said.

He looked back at the window. "Things that need a roof."

"Is it the Croft arrangement?"

The pause this time was longer. Agnes counted it by the harbor sounds — three knocks of hull against piling, a gull somewhere above the fog line, the distant tick of the cannery's iron walls contracting in the morning cold.

"The Crofts," Bertrand said, slowly, "think they made a contract." He stopped. Started again. "What they made was a record. The contract was already there."

Agnes set down her thermos. "Who made it?"

"The Quiet Town dead." He said it the way you say the name of weather, not the name of people. "Before the 1743 settlement. They agreed to stay below the fog line."

She waited. He had more. She could tell by the way he was holding the arm of his chair, the slight forward tilt of his weight.

"In exchange for—" He stopped.

Agnes waited.

The fog pressed against the window. The harbor knocked. Somewhere in the floor beneath them, something old held its shape in the dark.

"In exchange for the inn staying open," Bertrand said.

Agnes heard it. She heard it the way you hear a step on a stair you thought was empty — with your whole body, before your mind has assembled the thought. She sat with it for a moment. Then she picked up her thermos, screwed the cap back on, and stood.

"I'll tell the baker about the mackerel," she said.

Bertrand nodded. He had already returned to the window.

She walked back through the cannery the way she had come, past the rusted processing lines, past the wide dark floor with its decades of salt and fish oil. She did not look down. She had never particularly looked down in here, and she understood now, with the new weight of what Bertrand had said settling into her, that this had been its own kind of knowledge — her body knowing to look elsewhere while her mind kept the question at a comfortable distance.

She came out the side door into the fog.

---

The walk back up the bluff road was longer than the walk down. It always was — the grade was the same in both directions, but going up required something going down didn't. She stopped once, halfway, to shift her bag again. The bread from the market was still warm through the cloth.

She came out of the fog at the forty-foot mark, and the light changed: harder, clearer, the morning sun doing what it could against the November sky. The sounds of the upper town arrived on time again, present-tense, no delay.

Agnes stopped walking.

She stood on the bluff road with the fog line at her back and looked at the inn.

The Bellweather sat at the top of the road as it always had, white clapboard, the porch running the full width of the front face, the east wing windows catching the morning light. She had repainted it four years ago. She would repaint it again in two years, if her knees cooperated, or she would hire someone, which she had been saying for twenty years. The garden gate was open — the latch needed fixing, had needed fixing since September, she kept meaning to ask Douglas to look at it and then not asking because Douglas had been dead for thirty-three years and she could fix a latch herself and the real reason she hadn't was that she liked having something small left undone.

She looked at the inn.

The inn staying open.

Sixty years she had run it. Sixty years of bread every morning and chowder every Friday and the east wing guest rooms kept fresh and the dining room fire lit from October through April. Sixty years of knowing, without examining why she knew, that she could not stop. Could not leave for more than a day. Could not take the vacation Douglas had suggested in 2003 when she'd driven to the county line and turned back, telling herself it was choice.

She had been telling herself it was choice.

She had been running the Bellweather for sixty years and she had never — not once, not in any of the careful, deliberate, non-digging way she had built her life — understood what she was doing. She had thought she was the innkeeper. She had thought she was keeping people fed and comfortable because she was good at it and because they needed it and because, underneath all of that, because it kept her from having to look at anything else.

She was not the innkeeper.

She was the terms of the contract.

Agnes stood at the fog line with the cold at her back and the inn in front of her, and she did not move for a long time. The morning went on around her — a gull crossed the sky, the garden gate swung in the wind, the fog pressed its slow patient weight against the back of her legs. The bread in her bag had gone cool.

She thought about the woman from 1923, who had come looking for someone and stayed a hundred years, untethered, unable to speak, leaving cold drafts in empty rooms. A drifter. A name on no list Agnes had kept.

She thought about Carla Venn, who had also come looking for someone.

She started walking toward the inn. The gate swung open as she reached it, which was the wind, and she went through it and up the porch steps and in through the front door, and the smell of the place closed around her — old wood, good bread from yesterday still in the air, the fire Douglas had kept going in the parlor — and she stood in the front hall and took off her coat and hung it on the hook she had hung it on for sixty years.

Douglas appeared in the kitchen doorway. He was holding his mug. The crack along its handle had spread another quarter inch overnight; she could see it from here.

"Ag," he said. "How was Bertrand?"

"Informative," Agnes said. She set her bag down on the hall table. She looked at her husband — his paint-stained work clothes, his boots she had never convinced him to change, the mug with its spreading crack. She looked at him for a moment longer than she usually allowed herself.

"I need to know," she said, "how much you know."

Douglas was quiet. He looked at his mug. Then he looked at her, and his face had the particular quality it got when he had been deciding something for a long time and had finally decided.

"Agnes," he said. "I need you to come sit down. I need you to not straighten anything while I talk."

She followed him into the kitchen. She sat at the table. She put her hands flat on the surface of it and felt the grain of the old wood under her palms and did not straighten anything.

The morning light came through the north-facing window and lay across the floor in a pale bar, and the fire in the parlor crackled through the open door, and somewhere in the east wing above them a door opened and closed softly, and Agnes waited for her husband to tell her what he knew.

# Chapter Eleven

The bell above the front door rang at half past nine, and Agnes knew before she turned around.

Gareth Croft stood in the parlor doorway with his hands in the pockets of his good canvas jacket and his eyes already moving around the room — cataloguing, she had always thought, the way a man does when he wants to know what's changed. He had the posture of someone who had decided, before arriving, to appear unhurried. The cold came in with him. It smelled of the harbor, which was four hundred feet below and should not have been detectable from the inn's front parlor, but Gareth always carried it with him, that low-tide iron edge, and Agnes had spent thirty years deciding whether this meant something.

"Agnes," he said.

"Gareth." She set down the bread knife. She did not wipe her hands on her apron. "You're a long way from your side of the bluff road."

He smiled, which he did rarely and badly. "I was in the area."

He had not been in the area. He had not been inside the Bellweather since 1994, when they had their first and most consequential argument about the guestbook, and he had left through the front door with the specific deliberateness of a man who wanted you to know he was choosing to leave. She had watched him go and felt, for the first time, the particular cold of an enemy who was also a neighbor — someone you'd have to see at the post office, someone who knew which window you left unlocked in summer.

He was still in the doorway.

She moved to the fireplace and added a log, not because it needed one but because she wanted something to do with her hands that was not gripping the bread knife. The fire took the wood and crackled and threw out a gust of warmth that smelled of good oak. The parlor was comfortable this morning — the curtains drawn against the gray, the lamp on the side table casting its amber circle, Douglas's chair empty but present, the way it always was.

Gareth watched her tend the fire.

"I've come to offer my assistance," he said. "With the county investigation."

"I don't need it."

"That's not quite the point." He shifted in the doorway, and she caught the movement: checking the front hall behind him, the porch beyond the door. His exit route. "You'll understand that the county's interest in the foundation survey is not something I can hold off indefinitely. Historically speaking, the Bellweather has always been a matter of shared concern."

She turned from the fire and looked at him directly. He held the look with the practiced ease of a man who had been in rooms where holding the look was what mattered.

"The county's interest in the foundation survey," she said, "is something you've been cultivating. Don't present it to me as weather."

The smile again, brief and asymmetrical. "You've always been sharp in the morning."

"I'm sharp at all hours, Gareth. The bread's in the kitchen and you're not invited to it. Now. What do you actually want?"

He let a moment pass. Outside, the wind off the water moved through the bare branches of the elm at the corner of the porch, and the sound of it was low and complicated. Agnes waited. She had learned, over decades, that Gareth Croft's silences were not hesitation — they were construction. He was building the next sentence the way he'd build a wall: one stone at a time, nothing left to chance.

"The guestbook," he said.

"It's not available."

"I'm not asking for the book." He took one hand from his pocket and gestured, a short, precise movement. "I'm asking you to acknowledge that some records belong to the town, not to the person who happens to be holding them at the moment."

"The book is mine."

"Some debts transfer with the deed." He said it evenly, without emphasis, which was worse than if he'd pressed it. "You knew that when you bought it."

"You've said that twice now." Agnes crossed her arms over her apron. "Say what you mean."

Something shifted in his face. Not softening — Gareth Croft did not soften — but a kind of settling, as if he had been carrying a weight at an angle and had finally decided to set it down properly. He took two steps into the parlor, which was the first time he had done so, and stopped near the wing chair by the window. He did not sit.

"My father," he said, "told me, when he was dying, that the Croft family had an arrangement with the Cove's dead. Not a metaphor. Not a family story. An arrangement — specific terms, specific obligations. He said it had been in place since 1743 and that it required maintenance." He looked at the chair he was standing near, then back at her. "He died before he told me what the terms were. I have spent forty years trying to reconstruct them. I believe the guestbook contains evidence of the original arrangement. The record of who agreed to what."

The fire crackled. Agnes stood with her arms crossed and her weight on her back foot and listened to Gareth Croft tell her, in the parlor of her inn, the thing she had been waiting for him to say for thirty years. She had not expected to feel anything in particular when he finally said it. She had expected to feel the cold satisfaction of confirmed suspicion. What she felt instead was something more like exhaustion — the particular tiredness of a long winter that has gone on past the point of being interesting.

"Your father was right," she said, "that there's an arrangement." She held his gaze. "He was wrong about who made it."

Gareth went very still.

It was not the stillness of a man who had nothing to say. It was the stillness of a man counting what he had just been given. Agnes watched him do it — watched the quick calculation behind his eyes, the assessment of what her words confirmed and what they implied. She had given him something. She felt it leave her hands the way you feel a document pass across a table. She could not take it back.

He pushed. She had known he would.

"Then tell me," he said.

"No."

The word sat in the warm room and did not apologize for itself. Gareth looked at her for a long moment. The elm branch moved against the porch rail outside. Somewhere in the east wing above them, a floorboard settled with a sound like a breath held and released, and Agnes did not look up at the ceiling, because she had learned long ago not to look at things she didn't want Gareth Croft to notice her looking at.

He put his hand back in his pocket. He took one step toward the doorway, then stopped.

"The county is going to want access to the east wing," he said. "The murder investigation gives them standing they didn't have before."

"Let them apply for it in writing."

"Agnes—"

"In writing, Gareth. To my solicitor. Who is in Kettle Falls and answers correspondence on Tuesdays."

He looked at her for another moment, and she held it, and then he turned and walked through the parlor doorway and through the front hall and out the door, and the bell rang, and the cold came in briefly and was swallowed by the warmth of the room.

Agnes stood in the parlor and listened to his footsteps cross the porch and descend the steps and move down the front path until she couldn't hear them anymore.

Then she went to the kitchen.

---

The kitchen smelled of the morning's bread, already baked and cooling on the rack, and of the coffee she had put on at six and not touched since. She poured a cup now and stood at the counter with both hands around it and looked at the north-facing window, which showed her the gray sky and the bare upper branches of the garden hedge and, beyond the hedge, the fog line — the place where the clear air stopped and the harbor mist began, soft and permanent and moving in slow circles that had nothing to do with the wind.

She had confirmed it. She had told Gareth Croft, in plain language, that an arrangement existed and that she knew what it was.

She had not meant to do the second part.

The first part she had intended, or something like it — she had decided, standing in the parlor with the bread knife on the counter behind her, that denying the existence of the arrangement was no longer useful. Gareth already knew it existed. His father had told him. Denying it would only make her look like someone with something to hide, and she had enough things to hide without adding unnecessary ones.

But she had said *he was wrong about who made it*, and those eight words were not a denial. They were a door. A door she had opened and could not now close, and Gareth Croft was on the other side of it with forty years of questions and the specific patience of a man who believed he was owed an answer.

She set down the coffee cup.

The bread on the rack was a good loaf — she could see the color of the crust from here, deep gold, the score marks spread exactly right. She had made it on reflex this morning, her hands doing the measuring and folding while her mind was somewhere else entirely, in the bluff cemetery with Bertrand's words from the day before still rearranging themselves into new shapes. The drifter from 1923. The woman who had come looking for someone. Carla Venn, who had also come looking for someone, and who had found something else.

She was thinking about the guestbook — the signatures, the dates, the thirty-one years of arrivals and anniversaries — when she heard the footstep in the kitchen doorway and turned.

Nora Vale stood with her notepad in her hand and her jacket still on, which meant she had come in through the front and had not stopped at the hall hook, which meant she had come quickly. Her braid was damp at the end. She had been outside this morning, then. Her grandmother's ring was on her right hand, and she was not twisting it yet, which meant she had not yet asked the question she had come to ask.

"I heard raised voices," Nora said. "From the front parlor."

"A local matter."

"Gareth Croft." Nora said it without inflection, not a question. "I know who he is." She moved into the kitchen and stopped at the far end of the counter, leaving the full width of the room between them. She had good instincts about space. Agnes had noticed this before. "He's been calling the county office every day since the body was found."

Agnes looked at her. "What does he want?"

"The same thing everyone who calls the county about Heron Cove wants." Nora set her notepad on the counter. She did not open it. "He wants us to make something official."

The coffee had gone lukewarm. Agnes picked it up anyway, because she needed something to do with her hands and the bread was not ready to be touched.

"Official," she said.

"A formal investigation. County jurisdiction, not—" Nora paused, choosing the word. "Not local management." She looked at Agnes steadily. "He's been very specific about the foundation survey. He wants it attached to the murder investigation as a concurrent inquiry. Same team, same access, same timeline."

Agnes set the cup down.

"He's been building this," she said, mostly to herself.

"For at least two weeks. Before that, I don't know — the calls I've seen go back to the morning the body was found, but the office said he had a file already prepared. Printed, tabbed, referenced." Nora's eyes moved to the bread rack, then back to Agnes. "He's been waiting for a reason."

Carla Venn. Dead in the garden. The fog rising above the bluff road for the first time in recorded memory. A reason.

"What would it cost you?" Nora asked.

Agnes looked at her.

"If the county made it official. Full investigation, foundation survey, access to the property." Nora's voice was even, not unkind. She was not threatening. She was, Agnes understood, doing the thing she did in every conversation: gathering. "What would that cost you specifically?"

The kitchen was warm. The bread smelled of good flour and the particular sweetness of a crust done right. Somewhere in the east wing, on the other side of the wall behind Agnes's left shoulder, a door opened and closed softly — one of the guests, moving through their morning, the sound of a house that was lived in by people who had nowhere else to go.

Agnes thought about the guestbook. Thirty-one years of names and dates, the signatures that would mean nothing to a county surveyor and everything to Gareth Croft. She thought about Room 4, whose name she had not written in the book in eleven years of knowing it. She thought about the arrangement — the real one, not the version Gareth's father had told him, not the version Gareth had spent forty years trying to reconstruct from the wrong materials.

She thought about what she had said in the parlor twenty minutes ago, and the door she had opened, and who was now standing on the other side of it.

"Sit down," Agnes said. "I'll pour you a cup of coffee."

Nora sat. She did not open her notepad. She waited, which was the right instinct, and Agnes poured two cups and set one in front of the deputy and kept one for herself, and she stood at the counter rather than sitting because standing was how she thought, and she looked at the north-facing window and the fog beyond the hedge and did not answer the question.

Not yet.

Nora wrapped both hands around the coffee cup — her grandmother's ring catching the kitchen light — and waited with the particular patience of someone who had learned that silence was a tool, and Agnes recognized the technique because she used it herself, and she almost said so.

She didn't.

"It would cost me," Agnes said, "more than you have a category for."

Nora looked at her across the kitchen. The coffee steamed between them. Outside, the wind moved through the hedge, and the fog sat at the line where it always sat, and somewhere below the bluff the harbor moved in its slow, cold circles, and Agnes stood in her kitchen in the inn she had owned for sixty years and felt, for the first time in a very long while, the specific sensation of a situation that was no longer fully in her hands.

Nora reached for her notepad. She opened it to a clean page.

"Walk me through," she said, "what Gareth Croft said to you this morning. Not the summary."

Agnes looked at her.

"The actual conversation," Nora said.

# Chapter Twelve

Agnes set the mug on the kitchen table and looked at it for a long time.

It was the fourth one. She knew it by the small asymmetry in the handle, a slight thickening on the left side where the Vermont potter had pressed a thumb and not pressed evenly. The glaze was honey-brown, and the cracks had started six months after she'd bought it, radiating from the base in the patient way of things that were being slowly used up. Douglas had not noticed. Douglas never looked at the mug the way Agnes looked at the mug.

Outside, the fog sat at the hedge line, thick and still in the gray November afternoon. The kitchen smelled of bread she'd baked at four in the morning, the loaves cooling now on the rack by the window, and of the coffee she'd made and not drunk. Her cup was on the counter, untouched and cold.

She wiped her hands on her apron. The apron was clean. She wiped them anyway.

"All right," she said to the room. "I'm going to do it."

Douglas did not answer. She'd sent him out of the kitchen an hour ago, which had required asking twice and then saying his full name in the particular register that meant she was not discussing it. He was somewhere in the east wing now, worrying at the problem the way he worried at loose doorknobs — persistent, circular, not quite effective. She could feel the quality of his absence the way you felt a draft from a door left open in another room.

She pulled out the chair and sat down.

The mug was warm, which meant Douglas had been holding it recently. She wrapped both hands around it the way he always did, thumbs up, and the ceramic settled against her palms and she felt the first edge of something — not a memory, not yet, but the shape of a door. She had done this four times in eleven years. She knew what came next.

"Douglas," she said. "I need you to let me in."

The kitchen was quiet. The bread cooled. A coal shifted in the firebox of the old range and the warmth pulsed once against her left side.

"I know it costs," she said. "I know what it costs me. I'm asking anyway."

The mug in her hands went very slightly heavier, and then the door opened, and Agnes went through it.

---

She was standing in the front hallway of the inn, but the light was wrong — thinner, colder, the particular light of three years after Douglas had died, when she'd still been repainting the parlor trim every autumn because Douglas had always repainted it and she didn't know how to stop. The wallpaper was the old pattern, the dark blue one she'd replaced in 1997. She could smell the oil paint and the salt air and Douglas's coffee, which meant she was inside his memory and not her own.

Douglas stood at the front desk. He was solid here, more solid than he'd been in years, and he was looking at the door.

A man had checked in.

Agnes watched from the edge of the memory the way you watched from the back of a theater — she could see but not touch, hear but not change. The man at the desk had his back to her. Medium height. Dark coat. He was signing the guestbook in the unhurried way of someone who had nowhere else to be, and Douglas was watching him with an expression Agnes had not seen on Douglas's face in thirty-three years of marriage and three years of haunting. Douglas's face, when he was troubled, went very still. This was the stillness.

The man finished signing. He took his key. He turned toward the east wing stairs, and Agnes saw his face, and the memory locked around her like a room with no windows.

She knew him.

Not from life. From a story — no, from the shape of a story she had told herself for sixty years without examining too closely, the way you told yourself the foundation was sound without wanting to go down and look at it. She knew the line of his jaw and the particular set of his shoulders, because she had seen a photograph once, in a Portland lawyer's office, a photograph attached to a file she had no business reading, a file she had read anyway on a cold morning in 1961 when she was twenty-four years old and desperate and certain she was doing the right thing.

The man from the file. The man from the Portland estate. The man whose money she had taken because he had taken it first, from her mother, from eleven other women, and she had told herself it was justice and she had bought an inn with it and she had never looked back.

He had not died naturally. She had known this, distantly, the way you knew things you had decided not to examine. A coronary, the lawyer had said when she'd called the number on the file. A coronary, alone, in the house on the hill above the harbor, six months after the estate had been quietly, illegally redistributed. She had said how terrible and she had hung up the telephone and she had not thought about it again.

She had thought about it every day for sixty years. She had thought about it in the specific way of not-thinking, which was the only kind she'd allowed herself.

Douglas had been watching him go up the stairs. And Douglas, who had been dead for three years at this point, who had sixty years of marriage behind him and a ghost's clarity about the things the living couldn't see clearly — Douglas had known. Not everything. Agnes understood this even inside the memory, felt the shape of what Douglas knew and didn't know. He'd recognized the face from a photograph she'd shown him once, early in their marriage, when she'd almost told him the whole story and had stopped at the photograph. He'd remembered. He'd put it together. And he had stood at the front desk of their inn and watched the man go up to Room 4 and he had not said a word.

He had been deciding ever since. Thirty years of deciding.

The memory released her.

Agnes was in the kitchen. Her hands were shaking — not badly, not dramatically, but the mug rattled once against the table when she set it down. The light through the north-facing window was the same gray afternoon light it had been. The bread was on the rack. The coffee was cold on the counter.

The migraine arrived like a door slamming in a room directly behind her eyes.

She pressed both palms flat against the table and breathed. The ozone smell was already displacing everything else — the bread, the coffee, the old wood smell of the kitchen — and she knew from experience she had perhaps ten minutes before the pain became something she'd need to lie down with. She stood up carefully, steadied herself on the counter, and stood at the north-facing window looking out at the fog.

Room 4.

She had known. She had known for eleven years, since the morning she'd found him in the east wing hallway reading the tide chart on the wall, and she'd looked at his face and the floor had gone soft under her feet and she'd said *good morning* and walked past him and gone into the kitchen and stood at this same counter and not moved for twenty minutes. She had known and she had not written his name in the guestbook and she had not asked him to leave and she had cooked for him every day since, trying different smells — vanilla, smoked fish, good coffee, fresh bread — because she did not know what else to do with what she had done, and he had never responded to any of it, and she had kept trying.

The migraine tightened. Agnes pressed two fingers to her right temple and thought: *He's been waiting for me to be ready.*

She had not been ready.

The question was whether she was ready now.

---

She was in the east wing hallway before she'd consciously decided to go there. This happened sometimes — her body making decisions while her mind was still arguing. The hallway was dim, the old wall sconces throwing a warm light that didn't reach the corners, and the floorboards here were the original ones, older than the 1743 settlement, and they made no sound under her feet because she had learned their particular language over sixty years.

Room 4's door was closed.

Agnes stood in front of it. The migraine pulsed behind her right eye. The ozone smell was complete now, blotting out everything else, and she registered its absence the way you registered the absence of a sound you'd been hearing so long you'd stopped hearing it.

She knocked.

"Come in," he said. One word. Like he'd been expecting her.

The room was tidy in the way his room was always tidy — not arranged, just undisturbed, as if objects came to rest naturally in their right places when he was present. The window faced east, toward the harbor, and the fog was at the glass, thick and white and still. He was sitting in the chair by the window. He was wearing the dark coat, the one from Douglas's memory, the one from the photograph in the Portland file. His face was the face she'd been not-looking-at for eleven years.

Agnes came in. She left the door open.

"Sit down," he said.

"I'll stand." She stood slightly sideways, the old habit. "I accessed Douglas's memory this morning."

Room 4 looked at her. His face was the particular kind of still she associated with people who had been waiting for something long enough to stop needing it urgently.

"I know what you are," Agnes said. "I know why you're here."

He didn't answer.

"I want you to understand," she said, and then she stopped, because she had been about to say *I want you to understand that I thought I was doing the right thing,* which was true and also the most useless sentence in the English language. She straightened the cuff of her cardigan instead. Her hands had mostly stopped shaking.

"You've been here eleven years," she said. "You signed the guestbook once. You've never asked for anything."

"No."

"Why not?"

He considered this. Outside, the fog moved against the glass, slow and purposeless. "You were going to have to arrive here on your own," he said. "I couldn't — it doesn't work if I push it."

Agnes looked at him. "Douglas knew."

"Douglas has known for thirty years."

The migraine pressed hard behind her eye. She breathed through it. "He was deciding how to tell me."

"Yes."

"He's not very quick about things," Agnes said, and something happened to her voice on the last word, a slight catch she hadn't planned, and she pressed her lips together and looked at the fog.

Room 4 watched her without expression, which was more bearable than sympathy would have been.

"The man you stole from," Agnes said, because she was going to say it plainly or she wasn't going to say it at all, and she was sixty years past the point of not saying it. "He was a bad man."

"Yes."

"He took money from women who had no way to get it back. My mother. Eleven others."

"Yes."

"I took it back." She paused. "I kept it."

Room 4 said nothing.

"I bought this inn with it," Agnes said. "I've run it for sixty years. I've —" she stopped. She had been about to list her justifications, and she recognized the list, she had maintained it for six decades, and she was so tired of the list. "I've been not looking at you for eleven years," she said instead. "And I owe you — I don't know what I owe you. I don't have a category for it."

"No," he said. "You don't." He said it without rancor, which was almost worse.

Agnes stood in the doorway of Room 4 with a two-day migraine assembling itself behind her right eye and the smell of ozone where the bread smell should have been, and she looked at the man she had displaced from his life and his death and his ordinary passage out of the world, and she said: "What do you want from me?"

He was quiet for long enough she thought he wasn't going to answer.

"I want to know my name is in the book," he said.

Agnes held very still.

"Not the story," he said. "Not the justification. Just the name. In the book, with the dates, like everyone else."

The fog shifted against the glass. Somewhere below the bluff, the harbor moved in its slow circles, and the herons stood in the shallows and did not startle, and the kelp beds came close to shore, and Agnes Murrow stood in the east wing hallway of the inn she had bought with stolen money and felt sixty years of careful maintenance crack straight down the middle like a mug that had been replaced four times and was going to need replacing again.

"I'll need your name," she said.

He told her.

She said it back to make sure she had it right. He nodded once.

Agnes turned and walked back down the hallway toward the kitchen, toward the guestbook on the front desk, toward Deputy Sheriff Nora Vale who was still sitting at the kitchen table with her notepad open to a clean page, waiting with the particular patience of someone who had learned that silence was a tool.

She had a name to write down. She had other things to say after that.

The migraine would last two days. She had two days' worth of things to say.
