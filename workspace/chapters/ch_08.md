# Chapter Eight: What Vera Saw

Agnes found her in the east parlor at half past eight, in the chair by the window, with the particular stillness of someone who had been waiting long enough to stop fidgeting about it.

Vera's anchor was the chair itself — a wingback in faded green wool that Agnes had reupholstered twice, each time to the same specifications, because the specifications mattered. Vera's hands were folded in her lap. The morning light came through the curtains at a low angle and passed through her at the wrist, which happened more in winter.

Agnes sat down across from her.

The east parlor smelled of woodsmoke and the faint vanilla Agnes had put into yesterday's cake, which Vera had spent the better part of the afternoon near without acknowledging. The fire had burned down to coals. Agnes could hear the fog against the north windows — not a sound, a pressure, the way the air thickened and then thinned again — and she could hear, from upstairs, the particular quality of silence that meant Deputy Vale was awake and moving carefully, the way people moved in houses they didn't yet understand.

Agnes looked at the table between them. A fork, displaced from some previous arrangement. She put her hands in her lap.

"You are frightened, Agnes." Vera's voice was the same as it had been in 1974, which was to say precise, with the cadence of someone who had once been told she spoke too quickly and had overcorrected. "I can tell because you've straightened that fork four times. Sit down. I'll tell you what I saw the night the living woman arrived, and you will not interrupt me."

Agnes had not moved to touch the fork. She looked at it. She looked at Vera.

She sat.

---

"I was in the corridor near Room Three," Vera said. "This was around midnight, perhaps a little past. I don't sleep, as you know, and I find the late hours — manageable. There is less noise. I prefer less noise." A small pause. "The living woman had arrived that evening. I had observed her at dinner. She ate quickly and looked at the walls more than her food, which I find unnerving in a guest, but I understand some people are like that."

Agnes waited.

"I was in the corridor. And the door to Room Four opened."

The fire shifted, one coal dropping against another with a small sound like a word cut short. Agnes kept her hands in her lap.

"I want to be clear," Vera said, "because I understand this is relevant. It opened. Not ajar, not the latch misbehaving. Opened. He stood in the doorway for some time — I would estimate three minutes, though I acknowledge my sense of duration is not what it was — and he looked toward Room Three. He did not come out into the corridor. He stood in the doorway and he looked, and then he went back inside and the door closed."

Vera's hands remained folded. She looked at the middle distance, which was the window, which was the fog.

"I have been in this inn for fifty years," she said. "He has never opened that door. Not once."

Agnes thought of the door. She had passed it ten thousand times. She had knocked on it when she needed to, and it had opened for her, but she had never seen it open on its own initiative — and there was a word for the distinction, she thought, between a door that opened when asked and a door that opened because it chose to. She didn't have the word. She felt its shape.

"Did he see you?" Agnes asked.

Vera considered this with the seriousness it deserved. "I don't believe so. I was near the linen cupboard. I was not — I was not expecting to be a witness to anything, and I may have made myself less present than usual." A fractional pause. "I am capable of that."

"I know," Agnes said.

This was true. Vera had a talent for it — a kind of controlled dimming — that Agnes had always assumed was temperament and now suspected was something else. A practice developed over fifty years. A way of being in a hallway without being seen.

Vera unfolded her hands and refolded them, which was the most agitation Agnes had seen from her in a decade.

"There is something else," Vera said.

Agnes waited.

"You will want to know who he is. Who is in that room." Vera's eyes moved to Agnes's face and then away. "I assumed you knew. I assumed you had always known."

"I know what room he's in," Agnes said.

A long pause. Outside, a gull called once and went quiet. The fog pressed against the glass.

"Yes," Vera said. "Well." She was quiet for a moment. "I died in that room, Agnes."

Agnes heard the fire. She heard the fog. She heard, distantly, floorboards on the second floor as Deputy Vale moved from the bathroom to the corridor — she knew the inn's sounds the way she knew her own breathing, could map the deputy's path without looking up.

"I have never told you," Vera continued, "because it seemed impolite. There is a correct way to discuss these things and I was not — I had not found the correct way, and then fifty years passed, and I had maintained, for all of that time, that I have always been in my current room. I moved myself. After. I thought it was better." A pause. "I thought it was tidier."

Agnes looked at her. Vera's face was what it always was — composed, slightly severe, the face of a woman who had decided, somewhere in her fifties, that composure was a form of armor and had never taken it off. Agnes looked at the small tension around Vera's mouth and understood it had cost something to say this. Fifty years of maintained fiction, offered up on a Tuesday morning in November.

"Why are you telling me now?" Agnes asked.

Vera met her eyes.

"Because something has changed," she said, "and I think you need to know what the room is before you find out who's in it."

Agnes sat with this for a moment. The coal in the fireplace had gone gray at the edges. She looked at it.

"All right," she said.

Vera unfolded her hands and smoothed them against her skirt — a gesture Agnes recognized as the end of something. The morning light shifted, the fog thinning slightly outside, and for a moment Vera was more present than usual, her outline sharp against the chair, her green wool cardigan almost solid.

"The fork," Vera said, "is on the wrong side."

Agnes looked at the table. The fork was where it had been.

She left it there.

---

The kitchen was cold when she came back to it — she'd left the north window cracked and the morning air off the water had come in and settled. She closed the window. She washed her hands. She got the bread dough from under its cloth, where it had been rising since six, and she turned it onto the floured board and she began to work it.

The physical fact of dough was something she had relied on for sixty years and she relied on it now. The resistance of it, the way it pushed back against her palms and then gave, then pushed back again. She worked the heel of her hand into it and folded it and turned it. Worked and folded and turned. The kitchen smelled of flour and yeast and the faint iron of the sea coming through the seal on the north window, which she needed to replace before December.

Vera had died in Room 4.

Agnes pressed her palms flat against the dough and held them there.

The room had been there before Vera. The room had been there before Agnes. The room was part of the original east wing, which sat on the Quiet Town foundations, which were older than the county survey by two hundred years, which Agnes had known when she bought the inn and had not examined because the stone was good and the price was right and she had needed somewhere to put herself.

What the room was: a room.

What the room was: the place Vera had died, and then moved away from, and then a man had arrived and settled into it and had never once opened the door in response to anything Agnes had seen — not the Anniversary Week, not the bad November of 2009 when two of the other guests had a disagreement that shook the plaster off the hallway ceiling, not any of it — until a living woman arrived and checked into the room next door.

She folded the dough.

*She was looking for her brother.*

He had said it to her four days ago, in the corridor, unprompted, and she had filed it in the drawer she did not open. She had filed it and made breakfast.

Agnes pressed her hands into the dough and thought of the name Nora Vale had said across the dining room table. Marcus Venn. The orange firelight. The notepad. The ring on the deputy's right hand, which Agnes had noticed and set aside.

She had known the name for eleven years. She had heard it once, in a context she had not examined since. She had heard it and she had —

"You talked to Vera."

Douglas materialized near the pantry door. He was more solid than usual in the mornings, when the kitchen was warm and smelled of things he'd loved, and he had his coffee mug and his paint-stained trousers and his boots, which she had stopped arguing about.

"Yes," Agnes said.

He was quiet for a moment. She could feel him watching her hands on the dough.

"Ag —"

"Not yet," she said. "Let me think."

He stayed. He didn't push — Douglas had never pushed, which was one of the things she had loved about him and one of the things she had, in her more honest moments, used. He stood near the pantry and held his mug and watched her work the dough, and the kitchen was warm, and the fire she'd built up before going to find Vera was crackling steadily in the small hearth, and the fog pressed against the north window with its low, persistent patience.

She worked the dough. She folded and turned and pressed. Outside, the fog thinned another degree and the light in the kitchen shifted from gray to the particular pale gold of a November morning doing its best. She could hear the east wing settling into the day around her — the creak of the staircase, the distant sound of a door, the specific rhythm of the house breathing.

Room 4 had opened his door.

He had stood in the doorway and looked toward Room 3 and then gone back inside. Vera, who had fifty years of careful observation and no reason to exaggerate, had said: *not once, not ever.* And Agnes believed her, not because Vera was incapable of exaggeration but because Vera was incapable of this particular kind. She had no investment in Room 4 being unusual. She had, if anything, an investment in not thinking about him at all.

Agnes shaped the dough into its loaf and set it in the pan and covered it again. She washed her hands. She stood at the sink and looked at the fog and let herself hold the two things she now knew, side by side, without yet looking at what they made together.

Vera had died in Room 4.

Room 4 had opened his door for the first time in fifty years when Carla Venn arrived.

The drawer she did not open had two things in it now, and they were beginning to press against the wood.

Douglas was still near the pantry. She could feel him the way she always could — a slight drop in temperature, a presence in her peripheral vision, the specific quality of being watched by someone who was worried about her and trying not to show it.

"It's going to be a good loaf," he said finally.

Agnes looked at the covered pan. The dough was doing what it did — rising in the warm kitchen, slow and inevitable, the yeast working in the dark.

"Mm," she said.

She put the kettle on. She got down two mugs — one for herself, one habit she had never broken — and she stood with her back to Douglas and listened to the inn around her. Upstairs, Deputy Vale's footsteps moved toward the staircase. The deputy was coming down for breakfast. She would have her notepad. She would have her questions.

Agnes looked at the bread.

She looked at the drawer she did not open.

She looked at the bread again.

*Before you find out who's in it,* Vera had said, with the particular emphasis of someone delivering a warning they were not sure would be heeded. Agnes had sat with that emphasis in the east parlor and she sat with it now. Vera had given her something and Vera would not acknowledge having given it, and Agnes would not ask her to, and the fifty years of maintained fiction would go on being maintained because some things deserved that courtesy.

The kettle began to sound. Agnes poured it.

She set Douglas's mug on the staff table, where his place had been set since a Thursday in 1991 when she had done it by reflex and then stood very still for a long time and then gone to sit on the garden fence and look at the fog until she understood what she was going to do about the rest of her life.

The bread rose in its pan. The fire cracked. The fog thinned another small degree.

Agnes heard the deputy reach the bottom of the stairs.

She picked up her tea.
