# Chapter Four: Seven at Table

The kelp had been sitting in a bowl of cold water since noon, and Agnes had been pretending not to notice it.

She stood at the counter with her back to it, reducing the stock. The stock needed attention — needed the heat kept just so, needed the occasional pass of the skimmer — and this was a perfectly good reason to stand with her back to the bowl of kelp and not think about what she was doing.

She was making chowder. She made chowder every Friday. This was a Friday.

The kelp was because it was the good chowder. The kind she made when the chowder needed to be good for a reason.

Agnes skimmed the stock and did not examine her reasons.

The kitchen was warm, the kind of warmth that collected under the ceiling and pressed down gently on everything below it. The window over the sink showed the garden going dark, and below the bluff the harbor lights were coming on one by one, soft and diffuse through the fog. She could smell the sea from here, the cold salt of it, and the bread she'd left cooling on the rack, and the particular green-iron smell of the kelp in its bowl. She turned and added it to the pot.

She had cooked for a living nose six times. Each one had been a small, careful problem, and she had managed each one by the same method: feed them well, keep them warm, let the inn do what the inn did, which was to make people feel held. Harold Fitch, 1989, had eaten two bowls of chowder and slept ten hours and left her a review in the physical book that said *exceptional hospitality, highly recommend.* He had driven away in his rental car and she had watched his taillights disappear down the bluff road and felt the particular relief of a problem resolved without incident.

She had never cooked for one who arrived already asking about the 1743 survey.

Agnes added the potatoes. She added the onion she'd been sweating since four o'clock, translucent and soft, and the cream, and she stood over the pot and breathed it in. Salt and sweet and the deep mineral note of the kelp. She adjusted the heat by feel. She got out the good bowls, the ones she didn't use for herself, and began to think about the table.

---

She had set seven places. She stood in the doorway of the dining room and looked at them: seven bowls, seven spoons, seven folded napkins, the bread in the center of the table with the good bread knife beside it. The fire had been going since four, and the room held the heat of it, amber and close. It smelled of wood smoke and the faintest trace of the chowder drifting in from the kitchen.

Five of the seven chairs already had their occupants.

Agnes could see them clearly, had been able to see them clearly for so long it registered less as sight and more as the simple fact of the room: Vera in her usual chair, spine straight, hands folded in her lap with the patience of someone who has been waiting for propriety to be restored and has learned to wait indefinitely. Old Marsh two seats down, already reaching for the bread before Agnes had put the knife down, and Agnes moved the knife slightly out of reach without looking at him, a reflex. The Calloway sisters in the chairs nearest the window, who had arrived together in 1983 and conducted a continuous, inaudible argument Agnes had been watching for forty years. And at the far end of the table, in the chair with the straight back, Room 4.

He was watching the door.

Agnes noted this the way she noted most things about Room 4: filed it, set it aside, moved on. He was often watchful. It was not her business what he was watching for.

She went back to the kitchen for the pot.

Carla Venn came down the stairs at three minutes past seven, carrying a notebook. She paused in the doorway of the dining room the way people paused when they walked into a room that felt occupied and found it apparently empty, and Agnes, coming in behind her with the pot, watched the small recalibration: Carla's shoulders dropping a fraction, her chin lifting, her eyes moving around the table with the careful attention of someone who had decided to take notes in her head.

"Sit anywhere," Agnes said. "Not the end chair."

Carla sat in the middle of the table, which put her between Room 4 at the far end and the Calloway sisters at the near end, with Vera directly across. She put her notebook on the table beside her bowl, then appeared to reconsider and moved it to her lap.

Agnes ladled the chowder.

The temperature in the room dropped two degrees when she moved to serve the far end of the table. She knew this the way she knew the state of the fire, the way she knew whether the bread was done before the timer — not from instruments but from sixty years of this particular kitchen, this particular room, these particular guests. Carla felt it too. Agnes saw her set down her spoon and pick it up again without eating, her eyes moving to the window and back.

"It's a cold house," Agnes said. "Old houses run cold."

"Right," Carla said. She picked up her spoon.

Vera, across the table, watched Carla the way she watched everyone who sat at Agnes's table for the first time: with the expression of a woman who has already identified three social infractions and is deciding which one to address first. The notebook on Carla's lap had registered. Agnes could tell.

Marsh was eating with enthusiasm, which was normal. The Calloway sisters were ignoring the food and continuing their argument, which was also normal. Room 4 had not moved.

Agnes took her place at the end of the kitchen pass and watched.

Carla ate the chowder. She ate it carefully, the way a person eats when they're paying attention to what they're eating — not performatively, not suspiciously, but with the focused consideration of someone whose nose was telling them something and whose brain was trying to catch up. She ate half the bowl before she said anything.

"The other guests," she said. "Are they always this quiet?"

"Some of them," Agnes said.

"Long-term residents, you said."

"I did."

Carla looked at the chair across from her, the one Agnes had not served, and then at the one beside it. Her gaze went around the table slowly. The fire popped. From the direction of the Calloway sisters — from slightly the wrong direction, Agnes noted, the direction of the wall rather than the chairs — came a low murmur of conversation.

Carla's head turned toward the wall, then back to Agnes.

"The acoustics in here are interesting," she said.

"Old houses," Agnes said again.

A small silence. Carla ate another spoonful of chowder. Agnes watched her, and Douglas watched Agnes watching Carla from the doorway to the kitchen, where he stood with his mug and said nothing, which was its own kind of commentary.

Room 4 was still watching Carla.

Agnes refilled the bread plate. She did not look at Room 4. She was aware of him the way she was aware of a cold draft, something at the edge of the room that her attention kept sliding away from, but she noted his attention on Carla and she filed it and she moved on.

"Do they eat?" Carla asked.

Agnes set the bread plate down. "Now."

"I'm asking because I've served six bowls of chowder and none of them have—"

"They're not hungry," Agnes said. "They come for the company."

It was not entirely a lie. Agnes straightened the bread knife.

Carla looked at her for a moment, then down at her bowl. "The chowder is extraordinary," she said. "I don't say that to change the subject."

"I know," Agnes said. "Finish it."

---

After dinner, Agnes cleared the table and the dining room emptied in the way it always emptied: the guests dispersing by degrees, Vera moving toward the east parlor with the measured pace of a woman who has somewhere to be and considers haste beneath her, Marsh going wherever Marsh went after dinner, which Agnes had stopped tracking in 2004. The Calloway sisters went upstairs. Room 4 stayed at the table for three minutes after everyone else had gone, which he sometimes did, and then he was not there anymore, and Agnes was alone with the dishes.

She heard Carla in the east parlor. The particular sound of a notebook being opened.

Agnes washed the bowls. She dried them. She put them back in the cabinet and stood at the sink looking at the dark garden and the fog-lit harbor below, and she thought about the seven places she'd set and the one living person who'd sat at them, and she picked up the pot and washed that too.

She made tea. She put it on the small tray with the good cups, the ones that matched, and she carried it to the east parlor.

Carla was in the armchair by the window — not Vera's armchair, Agnes noted, the one on the other side of the hearth, which was fine — with a notebook open on her knee. The fire had burned down to coals and Agnes added two pieces of wood before she set down the tray. The room brightened, and in the brightening Agnes saw the notebook page.

It was a map.

Hand-drawn, careful, with the coastline rendered in the particular shorthand of someone who had looked at a lot of maps and had a good eye for scale. She had marked the harbor, the bluff road, the point. And along the edge of the harbor, following the contour she had apparently estimated from the topography, a line in red ink.

The fog line.

Agnes had been drawing that line in her own head for sixty years. She had never seen anyone else draw it.

She set down the teapot. "What are you looking for?"

Carla looked up. She had the expression of someone who had been waiting for this question and had prepared three different answers and was now deciding which one was true. She chose, Agnes thought, the true one.

"My brother came here in 2016," she said. "He never left." A pause. "Not died — I don't mean died. I mean he came here and he stopped leaving. He stopped calling, stopped responding, stopped — existing in any way I could reach. And everyone I talked to said he chose that, that he was troubled, that I was—" She stopped. "I've been looking at this place for two years from a distance. I needed to come see it."

Agnes poured the tea. She handed Carla a cup. She looked at the red line on the map, the 40-foot contour marked with the precision of someone who had done the math.

"Six hours," Agnes said.

Carla looked at her.

"You've been here six hours and you've mapped the fog line."

"I mapped it from the road before I checked in," Carla said, with a faint note of something approaching apology, or possibly pride, it was hard to tell. "I've been marking it in my notes for two years. Satellite imagery, mostly, but the resolution isn't—" She stopped herself. "I'm a researcher. It's a reflex."

"Your brother," Agnes said. "His name."

"Marcus Venn." Carla's hands were still around the tea cup. "He was thirty-one when he came here. He'd been — he'd had a difficult few years, which is why no one took me seriously. But he wasn't the kind of person who just disappears. He was the kind of person who called every Sunday. He called every Sunday for twenty-three years and then he stopped."

Agnes stood by the fire and let the warmth of it press against her back. The room smelled of tea and old wood and, faintly, the particular cold-salt draft that came from the direction of Vera's armchair, where Vera was now sitting and listening with the fixed attention she reserved for conversations she had decided were her business.

Agnes did not acknowledge Vera.

"I'll look into it," Agnes said. "In the morning."

Carla looked at her for a long moment. "You said that like you know something."

"I said it like I need to sleep on it," Agnes said. "Drink your tea. The bed in Room 3 is good."

She picked up the tray and left the east parlor before Carla could ask another question in the right direction.

---

The kitchen was quiet. Agnes stood at the counter and listened to the sound of the inn at night: the fire settling, the old wood of the walls contracting in the cold, the wind off the water pressing at the north-facing window over the sink. From upstairs, after a while, the sound of a door closing. Then nothing.

She stood there for a long time.

The chowder had been good. She knew it had been good because she'd tasted it and because Carla had eaten every drop and because the kelp did what the kelp did to living guests, which was to pull them down into sleep like a warm hand on the shoulder, and Carla would be asleep within the hour and would sleep until eight and would wake up feeling, as Harold Fitch had once written, *remarkably rested.* Agnes told herself she had made the good chowder because it was Friday and because she had a living guest and because she was a professional.

She straightened the wooden spoon on its rest.

"Ag."

Douglas was in the doorway. He had his mug and his paint-stained boots and the expression he wore when he'd been working up to something for several hours and had decided the time was now.

"I know," Agnes said.

"You don't, though." He came into the kitchen. He moved through the room the way he always had, with the unhurried certainty of a man who had built most of the shelves in it. "The thing is—"

"In the morning," Agnes said. "I'll look into the brother in the morning."

"Agnes."

She turned to the counter and began wiping it down. It was already clean.

"There are things I can look up," she said. "The guestbook goes back to '61. If he was here, he signed in. I'll find the entry and I'll—"

"Agnes." His voice had changed register. Not Ag. Agnes. "I need you to sit down."

"I'm not tired."

"That's not why I'm asking."

She kept wiping the counter. The cloth moved in the same small circle, the same patch of tile she had been cleaning for thirty years, the grout worn pale in the same places. She was aware of him watching her. She was aware of the weight of what he wasn't saying yet, how it filled the kitchen the way heat filled it, pressing down from the ceiling.

She reached out and straightened the fork at the edge of the counter. It had been straight already.

Douglas watched her do it.

The mug in his hand made a small, sharp sound, like something giving way, and Agnes did not look at it, but she heard it: the new crack, finding its line.
