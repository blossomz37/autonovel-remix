# Chapter Twelve

Agnes set the mug on the kitchen table and looked at it for a long time.

It was the fourth one. She knew it by the small asymmetry in the handle, a slight thickening on the left side where the Vermont potter had pressed a thumb and not pressed evenly. The glaze was honey-brown, and the cracks had started six months after she'd bought it, radiating from the base in the patient way of things that were being slowly used up. Douglas had not noticed. Douglas never looked at the mug the way Agnes looked at the mug.

Outside, the fog sat at the hedge line, thick and still in the gray November afternoon. The kitchen smelled of bread she'd baked at four in the morning, the loaves cooling now on the rack by the window, and of the coffee she'd made and not drunk. Her cup was on the counter, untouched and cold.

She wiped her hands on her apron. The apron was clean. She wiped them anyway.

"All right," she said to the room. "I'm going to do it."

Douglas did not answer. She'd sent him out of the kitchen an hour ago, which had required asking twice and then saying his full name in the particular register that meant she was not discussing it. He was somewhere in the east wing now, worrying at the problem the way he worried at loose doorknobs — persistent, circular, not quite effective. She could feel the quality of his absence the way you felt a draft from a door left open in another room.

She pulled out the chair and sat down.

The mug was warm, which meant Douglas had been holding it recently. She wrapped both hands around it the way he always did, thumbs up, and the ceramic settled against her palms and she felt the first edge of something — not a memory, not yet, but the shape of a door. She had done this four times in eleven years. She knew what came next.

"Douglas," she said. "I need you to let me in."

The kitchen was quiet. The bread cooled. A coal shifted in the firebox of the old range and the warmth pulsed once against her left side.

"I know it costs," she said. "I know what it costs me. I'm asking anyway."

The mug in her hands went very slightly heavier, and then the door opened, and Agnes went through it.

---

She was standing in the front hallway of the inn, but the light was wrong — thinner, colder, the particular light of three years after Douglas had died, when she'd still been repainting the parlor trim every autumn because Douglas had always repainted it and she didn't know how to stop. The wallpaper was the old pattern, the dark blue one she'd replaced in 1997. She could smell the oil paint and the salt air and Douglas's coffee, which meant she was inside his memory and not her own.

Douglas stood at the front desk. He was solid here, more solid than he'd been in years, and he was looking at the door.

A man had checked in.

Agnes watched from the edge of the memory the way you watched from the back of a theater — she could see but not touch, hear but not change. The man at the desk had his back to her. Medium height. Dark coat. He was signing the guestbook in the unhurried way of someone who had nowhere else to be, and Douglas was watching him with an expression Agnes had not seen on Douglas's face in thirty-three years of marriage and three years of haunting. Douglas's face, when he was troubled, went very still. This was the stillness.

The man finished signing. He took his key. He turned toward the east wing stairs, and Agnes saw his face, and the memory locked around her like a room with no windows.

She knew him.

Not from life. From a story — no, from the shape of a story she had told herself for sixty years without examining too closely, the way you told yourself the foundation was sound without wanting to go down and look at it. She knew the line of his jaw and the particular set of his shoulders, because she had seen a photograph once, in a Portland lawyer's office, a photograph attached to a file she had no business reading, a file she had read anyway on a cold morning in 1961 when she was twenty-four years old and desperate and certain she was doing the right thing.

The man from the file. The man from the Portland estate. The man whose money she had taken because he had taken it first, from her mother, from eleven other women, and she had told herself it was justice and she had bought an inn with it and she had never looked back.

He had not died naturally. She had known this, distantly, the way you knew things you had decided not to examine. A coronary, the lawyer had said when she'd called the number on the file. A coronary, alone, in the house on the hill above the harbor, six months after the estate had been quietly, illegally redistributed. She had said how terrible and she had hung up the telephone and she had not thought about it again.

She had thought about it every day for sixty years. She had thought about it in the specific way of not-thinking, which was the only kind she'd allowed herself.

Douglas had been watching him go up the stairs. And Douglas, who had been dead for three years at this point, who had sixty years of marriage behind him and a ghost's clarity about the things the living couldn't see clearly — Douglas had known. Not everything. Agnes understood this even inside the memory, felt the shape of what Douglas knew and didn't know. He'd recognized the face from a photograph she'd shown him once, early in their marriage, when she'd almost told him the whole story and had stopped at the photograph. He'd remembered. He'd put it together. And he had stood at the front desk of their inn and watched the man go up to Room 4 and he had not said a word.

He had been deciding ever since. Thirty years of deciding.

The memory released her.

Agnes was in the kitchen. Her hands were shaking — not badly, not dramatically, but the mug rattled once against the table when she set it down. The light through the north-facing window was the same gray afternoon light it had been. The bread was on the rack. The coffee was cold on the counter.

The migraine arrived like a door slamming in a room directly behind her eyes.

She pressed both palms flat against the table and breathed. The ozone smell was already displacing everything else — the bread, the coffee, the old wood smell of the kitchen — and she knew from experience she had perhaps ten minutes before the pain became something she'd need to lie down with. She stood up carefully, steadied herself on the counter, and stood at the north-facing window looking out at the fog.

Room 4.

She had known. She had known for eleven years, since the morning she'd found him in the east wing hallway reading the tide chart on the wall, and she'd looked at his face and the floor had gone soft under her feet and she'd said *good morning* and walked past him and gone into the kitchen and stood at this same counter and not moved for twenty minutes. She had known and she had not written his name in the guestbook and she had not asked him to leave and she had cooked for him every day since, trying different smells — vanilla, smoked fish, good coffee, fresh bread — because she did not know what else to do with what she had done, and he had never responded to any of it, and she had kept trying.

The migraine tightened. Agnes pressed two fingers to her right temple and thought: *He's been waiting for me to be ready.*

She had not been ready.

The question was whether she was ready now.

---

She was in the east wing hallway before she'd consciously decided to go there. This happened sometimes — her body making decisions while her mind was still arguing. The hallway was dim, the old wall sconces throwing a warm light that didn't reach the corners, and the floorboards here were the original ones, older than the 1743 settlement, and they made no sound under her feet because she had learned their particular language over sixty years.

Room 4's door was closed.

Agnes stood in front of it. The migraine pulsed behind her right eye. The ozone smell was complete now, blotting out everything else, and she registered its absence the way you registered the absence of a sound you'd been hearing so long you'd stopped hearing it.

She knocked.

"Come in," he said. One word. Like he'd been expecting her.

The room was tidy in the way his room was always tidy — not arranged, just undisturbed, as if objects came to rest naturally in their right places when he was present. The window faced east, toward the harbor, and the fog was at the glass, thick and white and still. He was sitting in the chair by the window. He was wearing the dark coat, the one from Douglas's memory, the one from the photograph in the Portland file. His face was the face she'd been not-looking-at for eleven years.

Agnes came in. She left the door open.

"Sit down," he said.

"I'll stand." She stood slightly sideways, the old habit. "I accessed Douglas's memory this morning."

Room 4 looked at her. His face was the particular kind of still she associated with people who had been waiting for something long enough to stop needing it urgently.

"I know what you are," Agnes said. "I know why you're here."

He didn't answer.

"I want you to understand," she said, and then she stopped, because she had been about to say *I want you to understand that I thought I was doing the right thing,* which was true and also the most useless sentence in the English language. She straightened the cuff of her cardigan instead. Her hands had mostly stopped shaking.

"You've been here eleven years," she said. "You signed the guestbook once. You've never asked for anything."

"No."

"Why not?"

He considered this. Outside, the fog moved against the glass, slow and purposeless. "You were going to have to arrive here on your own," he said. "I couldn't — it doesn't work if I push it."

Agnes looked at him. "Douglas knew."

"Douglas has known for thirty years."

The migraine pressed hard behind her eye. She breathed through it. "He was deciding how to tell me."

"Yes."

"He's not very quick about things," Agnes said, and something happened to her voice on the last word, a slight catch she hadn't planned, and she pressed her lips together and looked at the fog.

Room 4 watched her without expression, which was more bearable than sympathy would have been.

"The man you stole from," Agnes said, because she was going to say it plainly or she wasn't going to say it at all, and she was sixty years past the point of not saying it. "He was a bad man."

"Yes."

"He took money from women who had no way to get it back. My mother. Eleven others."

"Yes."

"I took it back." She paused. "I kept it."

Room 4 said nothing.

"I bought this inn with it," Agnes said. "I've run it for sixty years. I've —" she stopped. She had been about to list her justifications, and she recognized the list, she had maintained it for six decades, and she was so tired of the list. "I've been not looking at you for eleven years," she said instead. "And I owe you — I don't know what I owe you. I don't have a category for it."

"No," he said. "You don't." He said it without rancor, which was almost worse.

Agnes stood in the doorway of Room 4 with a two-day migraine assembling itself behind her right eye and the smell of ozone where the bread smell should have been, and she looked at the man she had displaced from his life and his death and his ordinary passage out of the world, and she said: "What do you want from me?"

He was quiet for long enough she thought he wasn't going to answer.

"I want to know my name is in the book," he said.

Agnes held very still.

"Not the story," he said. "Not the justification. Just the name. In the book, with the dates, like everyone else."

The fog shifted against the glass. Somewhere below the bluff, the harbor moved in its slow circles, and the herons stood in the shallows and did not startle, and the kelp beds came close to shore, and Agnes Murrow stood in the east wing hallway of the inn she had bought with stolen money and felt sixty years of careful maintenance crack straight down the middle like a mug that had been replaced four times and was going to need replacing again.

"I'll need your name," she said.

He told her.

She said it back to make sure she had it right. He nodded once.

Agnes turned and walked back down the hallway toward the kitchen, toward the guestbook on the front desk, toward Deputy Sheriff Nora Vale who was still sitting at the kitchen table with her notepad open to a clean page, waiting with the particular patience of someone who had learned that silence was a tool.

She had a name to write down. She had other things to say after that.

The migraine would last two days. She had two days' worth of things to say.
