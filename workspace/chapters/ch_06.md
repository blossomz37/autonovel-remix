# Chapter Six: The Third Morning

The coffee finished brewing at 6:14 and Agnes poured a cup and stood at the kitchen window and watched the garden go gray and then pale and then the specific washed-out green of early October morning.

Carla Venn had not come down.

Agnes drank her coffee. She refilled it. She started the bread because starting the bread was what she did at six-fifteen regardless of anything else, and the rhythm of it — flour, salt, the particular give of the dough under her palms — was something she could hold onto while she waited. Carla had been down at five-fifty the first morning and five forty-three the second. Agnes had not taken note of this consciously, but she knew it the way she knew when the tide was wrong, the way she knew when the bread was two minutes from ready without looking at the clock.

Six-thirty came.

Agnes shaped the loaf and set it to rise and washed her hands at the sink and looked at the garden again. The fog was thick this morning, pressing against the garden fence in a way she didn't look at directly. Something in her sternum had gone tight. She breathed around it.

She made a second cup of coffee and left it on the counter, still hot, and went upstairs.

---

The east wing in the early morning had its own particular silence — not peaceful, never peaceful, but the silence of a place holding its breath. Her footsteps on the runner were soft and she kept her hand near the wall without touching it, an old habit from decades of moving through the inn before the light came.

Room 1 was quiet. Room 2 had a faint sound through the door, a low murmur she recognized as Vera talking to herself, which Vera did every morning and would deny. Room 4's door was closed. It was always closed.

Room 3's door was open.

Not ajar. Open, fully, swung back against the interior wall, and from the hallway Agnes could see the gray rectangle of the window, and the window was open too, the curtains moving in the cold air coming off the water.

Agnes stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame.

The room smelled of sea salt and something older underneath it, something she had no name for because she had only smelled it once before, in 1991, in the garden, and she had not let herself name it then either. It was not rot. It was prior to rot. It was what the body knew before the mind caught up.

Carla Venn was in the bed.

The angle of her arm was wrong. Not fallen-asleep wrong — the wrongness went deeper than that, into the geometry of a body that had finished with the work of holding itself together. Her face was turned toward the window, toward the cold air and the moving curtain, and the color of her skin in the gray morning light was the color of the fog below the road. Agnes's hand tightened on the doorframe until she could feel the grain of the wood against her palm, each ridge of it, the small roughness where the paint had begun to lift.

There was no ambiguity here.

The two notebooks were on the desk. The first was closed. The second — the one Carla had described on her first night as "the other category," in a tone that suggested she found the category both embarrassing and necessary — was open. Agnes could see, from the doorway, the ragged edge where a page had been torn out. Not carefully. The paper still fringed with small fibers along the spiral.

She did not go in. The window curtain moved again. The cold coming through it carried kelp and iron and that faint sweetness that had no name, and Agnes stood with the doorframe under her palm and let herself understand what she was looking at, all the way down, without flinching from any part of it.

Carla Venn had come to the Cove looking for her brother. She had taken notes. She had eaten Agnes's chowder and slept the deep sleep of the kelp and asked careful questions and been, in some way Agnes had recognized without naming, very close to something.

And someone had torn a page from her notebook and opened the window and left.

Agnes released the doorframe. She turned and went downstairs.

---

The front porch was cold. She had not put on her coat and she did not go back for it. The air hit her face and she stood at the railing and looked out at the Cove, and the breath went out of her in a way she did not try to control.

The fog had risen above the bluff road.

She had watched the fog every morning for sixty-three years the way other people watched the news, looking for information, looking for what the day intended to be. It sat in the harbor basin, it pressed against the lower town, it swallowed the cannery and the market street and the slips. It did not climb the bluff.

It was here now, gray and thick and silent, against the garden fence, above the road, and it was responding to something the way a bruise responds — not the wound, but the record of it.

Agnes pressed her hands flat on the railing. The wood was damp under her palms. She stood there until she had finished looking, until she had let the fog be what it was and had filed what it meant in the place where she filed things she knew without wanting to.

Then she went inside to the front desk and opened the guestbook to the back pages. She wrote in the small careful handwriting she used for things she needed to remember exactly: *Oct. 11. Fog above bluff road. First time. 6:42 a.m. Receded by —* and left the time blank because she didn't know yet, and she would not write something she didn't know.

She did not write what she thought it meant.

She reached for the pen again, then set it down. Her fingers found the spine of the guestbook instead, running along it once, and she thought about Carla's notebook, the torn edge, the fibers of paper still caught in the spiral. Someone had wanted something removed. Someone had known exactly which page to take.

She closed the guestbook and went to the kitchen and picked up the telephone.

---

The county dispatcher had a voice Agnes recognized — a woman named Sandra who had been on the morning shift for fifteen years and who had taken Agnes's calls twice before, once for a chimney fire and once for a burst pipe, and who had both times been brisk and asked nothing unnecessary. Agnes was grateful for this.

"Bellweather Inn, this is Agnes Murrow. I need to report a death."

A pause. The particular pause of someone shifting from one kind of attention to another.

"Can you tell me the nature of the situation, Ms. Murrow?"

"I have a guest who has passed overnight. It isn't natural causes." Agnes stood at the kitchen counter with her free hand flat on the cold tile. "I need someone to come."

She answered the questions. Name of the deceased, as far as she knew it. Room number. Whether she had touched anything. Whether there was anyone else in the building. This last one she paused on for a fraction of a second — long enough to be honest with herself about what she was doing — and then said, "No other guests currently."

Which was true, depending on your definitions.

Her free hand moved to the notepad on the counter and straightened it, aligning it with the edge of the tile.

Sandra told her someone would be out within the hour, possibly longer given the road conditions. Agnes thanked her and hung up and stood with her hand on the receiver for a moment before she lifted it away.

The bread was still rising on the counter, covered with a cloth, doing what bread did regardless of everything else. Agnes looked at it. She thought about putting it in the oven and decided against it — she didn't want the smell of it in the house when the county arrived, the warmth of it, the domesticity. It felt like a lie of a different kind than the one she'd already told.

She stood at the counter, and the kitchen was quiet around her, and she let herself think about the torn page.

Carla had two notebooks. One for facts, one for the other category. The torn page was from the second one. Agnes had seen, from the doorway, the faint impression of writing on the page beneath — pressure from a pen, the ghost of words she hadn't been close enough to read. She had not gone in. She should not go in before the deputy arrived. She knew this. She was also aware, in the cold clear way she was aware of most things, that she needed to go back upstairs before the county arrived and read whatever remained of that impression, because once the county was in the room, the room would belong to the county, and the county would not know what it was looking at.

She was still deciding when Douglas came in.

He was wearing his paint-stained work clothes, which he always wore, and he was holding his mug. She could see a new crack along the handle from where she stood — a thin dark line that hadn't been there yesterday. He positioned himself near the counter and his hand came to rest perhaps three inches from hers on the tile, close as he could manage.

"Ag."

"I know."

"I heard you on the phone." He looked at her with his face doing the thing it did when he had already decided something and was waiting for her to catch up. "I've been trying to figure out how to —"

"Douglas."

He stopped.

"I need you to tell me," she said, "what you've been not telling me."

The fog pressed at the kitchen window. Not a sound — a pressure, the way the air changed when it was thick and close. The bread rose under its cloth. Douglas's mug had another hairline crack now, running parallel to the first, and she watched it spread another millimeter as she looked at it.

"I know," he said. "The thing is — I don't know where to start."

Agnes looked at her hand on the counter. She looked at the space between her hand and his.

"Start with Room 4," she said.
