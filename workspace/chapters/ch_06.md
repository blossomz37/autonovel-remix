# Chapter Six: The Third Morning

The coffee finished brewing at 6:14 and Agnes poured a cup and stood at the kitchen window and watched the garden go gray and then pale and then the specific washed-out green of early October morning.

Carla Venn had not come down.

Agnes drank her coffee. She refilled it. She started the bread because starting the bread was what she did at six-fifteen regardless of anything else, and the rhythm of it — flour, salt, the particular give of the dough under her palms — was something she could hold onto while she waited. Carla had been down at five-fifty the first morning and five forty-three the second. Agnes had not taken note of this consciously, but she knew it the way she knew when the tide was wrong, the way she knew when the bread was two minutes from ready without looking at the clock.

Six-thirty came.

Agnes shaped the loaf and set it to rise and washed her hands at the sink and looked at the garden again. The fog was thick this morning, thicker than usual, and it pressed against the garden fence in a way she didn't look at directly.

She made a second cup of coffee and left it on the counter, still hot, and went upstairs.

---

The east wing in the early morning had its own particular silence. Not peaceful — Agnes had never confused the two. It was the silence of a place holding its breath. Her footsteps on the runner were soft and she kept her hand near the wall without touching it, an old habit from decades of moving through the inn in the dark.

Room 1 was quiet. Room 2 had a faint sound through the door, a low murmur she recognized as Vera talking to herself, which Vera did every morning and would deny. Room 4's door was closed.

Room 3's door was open.

Not ajar. Open, fully, swung back against the interior wall, and from the hallway Agnes could see the gray rectangle of the window, and the window was open too, the curtains moving in the cold air coming off the water.

Agnes stood in the doorway with her hand on the frame.

The room smelled of sea salt and something older underneath it, something she had no name for because she had only smelled it once before, in 1991, in the garden, and she had not let herself name it then either.

Carla Venn was in the bed.

Agnes looked at her for a long moment. She took in the particular stillness, the angle of the arm, the color of the skin in the gray morning light. She had seen enough dead people in her life — had sat with enough of them, had watched the transition from one state to the other with the particular attention of someone for whom death was a professional matter — to know there was no ambiguity here. Carla Venn had been dead for some hours.

The two notebooks were on the desk. The first was closed. The second — the one Carla had described on her first night as "the other category," in a tone that suggested she found the category both embarrassing and necessary — was open. Agnes looked at it from the doorway. She did not go in. She could see, from where she stood, the ragged edge where a page had been torn out, not carefully, the paper still fringed with small fibers along the spiral.

The window curtain moved again. The cold coming through it smelled of the deep water, of kelp and iron and something faintly sweet, and Agnes's hand tightened on the doorframe until she could feel the grain of the wood under her palm.

She stood there for a long time.

Then she turned and went downstairs.

---

The front porch was cold. Agnes had not put on her coat and she did not go back for it. She stood at the porch railing with her hands wrapped around nothing and looked out at the Cove, and what she saw made her be still in a different way than she had been still upstairs.

The fog had risen above the bluff road.

She had lived on this bluff for sixty-three years. She had watched the fog every morning the way other people watched the news, looking for information, looking for what the day intended to be. In sixty-three years the fog had never come above the road. It sat in the harbor basin, it pressed against the lower town, it swallowed the cannery and the market street and the slips. It did not climb the bluff. This was not a rule anyone had written down because it had never needed writing.

The fog was here now, gray and thick and silent, and it had come up past the road and it was against the garden fence and it was, Agnes understood, responding to something.

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she went inside, to the front desk, and opened the guestbook to the back pages where she kept her own records. She wrote, in the small careful handwriting she used for things she needed to remember exactly: *Oct. 11. Fog above bluff road. First time. 6:42 a.m. Receded by —* and she left the time blank because she didn't know yet when it would recede, and she would not write something she didn't know.

She did not write what she thought it meant. She knew what it meant. Writing it would not make it more true.

She closed the guestbook and went to the kitchen and picked up the telephone.

---

The county dispatcher had a voice Agnes recognized — a woman named Sandra who had been on the morning shift for fifteen years and who had taken Agnes's calls twice before, once for a chimney fire in 2007 and once for a burst pipe in 2014, and who had both times been brisk and competent and had not asked unnecessary questions. Agnes was grateful for this.

"Bellweather Inn, this is Agnes Murrow. I need to report a death."

A pause. The particular pause of someone moving from one kind of attention to another.

"Can you tell me the nature of the situation, Ms. Murrow?"

"I have a guest who has passed overnight. It isn't natural causes." Agnes said this the way she would have said *the east-wing boiler is making a noise it shouldn't.* She stood at the kitchen counter with her free hand flat on the cold tile. "I need someone to come."

She answered the questions. Name of the deceased, as far as she knew it. Room number. Whether she had touched anything. Whether there was anyone else in the building. This last one she paused on for a fraction of a second, long enough to be honest with herself about what she was doing, and then she said, "No other guests currently."

Which was true, depending on your definitions.

Sandra told her someone would be out within the hour, possibly longer given the road conditions. Agnes thanked her and hung up and stood with her hand on the receiver for a moment before she lifted it away.

The kitchen smelled of bread and coffee and the ghost of yesterday's rosemary. The bread was still rising on the counter, covered with a cloth, doing what bread did regardless of everything else. Agnes looked at it. She thought about putting it in the oven and decided against it. She didn't know why. Something about the smell of baking bread while the county drove up the bluff road felt wrong in a way she couldn't articulate, and she had learned, over sixty years, to trust the instincts she couldn't articulate.

She stood with her hand flat on the counter.

She stood there for a long time.

"Ag."

Douglas was by the window. He was wearing his paint-stained work clothes, which he always wore, and he was holding his mug, which had a new crack along the handle — she could see it from here, a thin dark line she hadn't noticed yesterday. He was looking at her with his face doing the thing it did when he had already decided something and was waiting for her to catch up.

She had not heard him come in. She never did, anymore.

"I know," he said.

"Do you."

"I heard you on the phone." He moved slightly, positioning himself near her at the counter. His hand came to rest perhaps three inches from hers on the tile. He couldn't manage more than two pounds of physical contact, which meant the counter was beyond him, but the gesture was the gesture. "I've been — Ag, I've been trying to figure out how to —"

"Douglas."

He stopped.

"I need you to tell me," she said, "what you've been not telling me."

The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, she heard the fog — not a sound, exactly, but a pressure, the way the air changed when the fog was thick and close. The bread rose under its cloth. Douglas's mug had another hairline crack she could see now, running parallel to the first.

"I know," he said. "The thing is — I don't know where to start."

Agnes looked at her hand on the counter. She looked at the space between her hand and his.

"Start with Room 4," she said.
