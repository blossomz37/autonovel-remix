# Chapter Six: The Third Morning

The coffee finished brewing at 6:14 and Agnes poured a cup and stood at the kitchen window and watched the garden go gray and then pale and then the specific washed-out green of early October morning.

Carla Venn had not come down.

Agnes drank her coffee. She refilled it. She started the bread because starting the bread was what she did at six-fifteen regardless of anything else, and the rhythm of it — flour, salt, the particular give of the dough under her palms — was something she could hold onto while she waited. Carla had been down at five-fifty the first morning and five forty-three the second. Agnes had not taken note of this consciously, but she knew it the way she knew when the tide was wrong, the way she knew when the bread was two minutes from ready without looking at the clock.

Six-thirty came.

Agnes shaped the loaf and set it to rise and washed her hands at the sink and looked at the garden again. The fog was thick this morning, pressing against the garden fence. Something in her sternum had gone tight. She breathed around it, wiped her hands on her apron, and went upstairs.

---

The east wing in the early morning had its own particular silence — not peaceful, never peaceful, but the silence of a place holding its breath. Her footsteps on the runner were soft and she kept her hand near the wall without touching it, an old habit from decades of moving through the inn before the light came.

Room 1 was quiet. Room 2 had a faint sound through the door, a low murmur she recognized as Vera talking to herself, which Vera did every morning and would deny. Room 4's door was closed. It was always closed.

Room 3's door was open.

Not ajar. Open, fully, swung back against the interior wall, and from the hallway Agnes could see the gray rectangle of the window, and the window was open too, the curtains moving in the cold air coming off the water.

She put her hand on the doorframe.

The room smelled of sea salt and something older underneath it, something she had no name for because she had only smelled it once before, in 1991, in the garden, and she had not let herself name it then either. It was not rot. It was prior to rot. It was what the body knew before the mind caught up.

Carla Venn was in the bed.

The angle of her arm was wrong. Not fallen-asleep wrong — the wrongness went deeper than that, into the geometry of a body that had finished with the work of holding itself together. Her face was turned toward the window, toward the cold air and the moving curtain, and the color of her skin in the gray morning light was the color of the fog below the road. Agnes's hand tightened on the doorframe until she could feel the grain of the wood against her palm, each ridge of it, the small roughness where the paint had begun to lift.

There was no ambiguity here.

Agnes let herself look at the whole of it. The stillness of the chest. The particular way the bedclothes had settled. Thirty-four years old, she guessed — she had not asked, had not needed to, and now would not get to. The cold air moved the curtain again and Agnes felt it reach her across the room, that air, carrying iron and kelp and the faint sweetness that had no name.

The two notebooks were on the desk. The first was closed. The second — the one Carla had described on her first night as "the other category," in a tone that suggested she found the category both embarrassing and necessary — was open. Agnes could see, from the doorway, the ragged edge where a page had been torn out. Not carefully. The paper still fringed with small fibers along the spiral. Beneath the torn edge, the top of the remaining page held the faint impression of words — pressure from a pen, the ghost of what had been written on the page above. Agnes was not close enough to read it.

She was also aware, in the cold clear way she was aware of most things, that she needed to read it before the county arrived. Once the county was in the room, the room would belong to the county, and the county would not know what it was looking at.

She released the doorframe. She crossed to the desk in four steps, keeping her eyes from the bed — not from fear, but from respect, the same instinct that made her lower her voice in certain rooms. She bent over the notebook.

The impression was faint but legible in the gray window light. Four words, pressed hard into the page below, underlined twice.

*Room 4 knows everything.*

Agnes straightened. She looked at the window, at the moving curtain, at the cold morning beyond it. Her jaw had gone tight. She breathed through her nose until it loosened, then turned and left the room, pulling the door to behind her — not closed, not latched, but no longer open to the hall.

She went downstairs.

---

The front porch hit her with cold she hadn't prepared for. She hadn't put on her coat and she didn't go back for it. The air was sharp against her face and she walked to the railing and gripped it with both hands and looked out at the Cove.

The fog had risen above the bluff road.

She had watched the fog every morning for sixty-three years the way other people watched the news, looking for information, looking for what the day intended to be. It sat in the harbor basin, it pressed against the lower town, it swallowed the cannery and the market street and the slips. It did not climb the bluff.

It was here now, gray and thick and silent, pressing against the garden fence, above the road. The tops of the fence posts disappeared into it. Agnes looked at this for a long time — not stunned, not frozen, but the way you look at something you need to memorize because you will be asked about it later and the asking will matter.

Then she went inside.

At the front desk she opened the guestbook to the back pages and wrote in the small careful handwriting she used for things she needed to remember exactly: *Oct. 11. Fog above bluff road. First time. 6:42 a.m. Receded by —* and left the time blank.

She did not write what she thought it meant.

Her fingers found the spine of the guestbook instead, running along it once. She thought about the torn edge of Carla's notebook, the fibers of paper still caught in the spiral. Someone had wanted something removed. Someone had known exactly which page to take — and had not known, or not thought to check, what the pen had pressed through to the page below.

She closed the guestbook and went to the kitchen and picked up the telephone.

---

The county dispatcher had a voice Agnes recognized — a woman named Sandra who had been on the morning shift for fifteen years and who had taken Agnes's calls twice before, once for a chimney fire and once for a burst pipe, and who had both times been brisk and asked nothing unnecessary.

"Bellweather Inn, this is Agnes Murrow. I need to report a death."

A pause. The particular pause of someone shifting from one kind of attention to another.

"Can you tell me the nature of the situation, Ms. Murrow?"

"I have a guest who has passed overnight. It isn't natural causes." Agnes stood at the kitchen counter with her free hand flat on the cold tile. "I need someone to come."

She answered the questions. Name of the deceased, as far as she knew it. Room number. Whether she had touched anything. Whether there was anyone else in the building. This last one she paused on for a fraction of a second — long enough to be honest with herself about what she was doing — and then said, "No other guests currently."

Which was true, depending on your definitions.

Her free hand moved to the notepad on the counter and aligned it with the edge of the tile.

Sandra told her someone would be out within the hour, possibly longer given the road conditions. Agnes thanked her and hung up.

The bread was still rising on the counter, covered with a cloth, doing what bread did regardless of everything else. Agnes looked at it. She thought about putting it in the oven and decided against it, and set the thought down with the same deliberateness she'd set down the phone.

She was still standing there when Douglas came in.

He was wearing his paint-stained work clothes, which he always wore, and he was holding his mug. She could see a new crack along the handle from where she stood — a thin dark line that hadn't been there yesterday. He came to the counter and his hand came to rest perhaps three inches from hers on the tile, close as he could manage.

"Ag."

"I know."

"I heard you on the phone." He looked at her with his face doing the thing it did when he had already decided something and was waiting for her to catch up. "I've been trying to figure out how to —"

"Douglas."

He stopped.

"I need you to tell me," she said, "what you've been not telling me."

The fog pressed at the kitchen window. Not a sound — a pressure, the way the air changed when it was thick and close. The bread rose under its cloth. Douglas's mug had another hairline crack now, running parallel to the first, and she watched it spread another millimeter as she looked at it.

"I know," he said. "The thing is — I don't know where to start."

Agnes looked at her hand on the counter. She looked at the space between her hand and his.

"Start with Room 4," she said.
