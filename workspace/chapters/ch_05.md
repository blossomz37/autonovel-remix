# Chapter Five: What the Fog Does

The mug in Douglas's hand had cracked, and Agnes had gone to bed anyway.

She lay in the dark of her quarters with her eyes open and the ceiling above her doing nothing helpful, and at two she gave up and made tea, and at three she was sitting at the small table by the west window with Douglas's current mug — number four, the one from the Vermont potter, the one with the thumb-groove worn into the handle from thirty-three years of a dead man's grip — and she was trying to think clearly.

The mug was hers now, for the hour. Douglas was somewhere in the east wing, or in the dining room, or standing in the garden the way he sometimes did when he was troubled, looking at the fence he'd been painting when he died. He would not go far. He never did.

She wrapped both hands around the mug and tried to think about Carla Venn the way she thought about any management problem: what did she want, what did she need, how long would she stay, what was the risk. This was a framework Agnes had developed over sixty years of running a house full of people who had complicated relationships with their own desires, and it worked. It had always worked.

It was not working now.

Because Carla Venn was not a management problem. She was a woman of thirty-four who had driven to the end of a peninsula without a reservation and asked for a room and eaten the chowder and gone to bed, and what she wanted was her brother, and what she needed was her brother, and how long she would stay depended entirely on whether Agnes helped her find him, and the risk — Agnes set the mug down.

The risk.

She knew something about brothers who disappeared. Not her own — she had no brothers — but the shape of it, the specific texture of a person who has been looking for so long they've stopped being able to tell the difference between looking and grieving. She had seen it in Carla's face at dinner, in the way the woman had eaten the chowder with the focused attention of someone who had forgotten to be hungry and was now catching up. Two years, Agnes calculated. Two years of driving and asking and being told there was nothing to find.

The tea went cold. Agnes did not drink it.

She was still sitting there at four when the quality of the dark outside the window changed — not lighter, not yet, but different, the way a room changes when someone opens a door in the next house over. Agnes stood, her knees registering the hour, and went to the window.

The fog was wrong.

She could see it from here, below the bluff road, pooled in the harbor basin the way it always was, but it was moving against the wind. The wind off the headland came from the northwest at this hour, had come from the northwest at this hour for as long as Agnes had been watching, and the fog was moving southeast, thickening as it went, piling up against the eastern shore of the harbor in slow deliberate rolls. Agnes had learned to read the fog over sixty years of watching it from this window and from the porch and from the bluff road, had learned its moods and its patterns and its particular vocabulary of warning, and what she read now made her put on her coat.

The porch was cold. October cold, the kind with teeth. Agnes stood at the railing and watched the fog for a long time.

It was denser than it had any right to be, and the color was wrong — not the soft gray-white of a normal night but something darker at its center, the color of old pewter, the color of the harbor water when the kelp beds were disturbed. And it was moving with a kind of intention Agnes did not have a word for. Not toward the inn. Not toward anything she could identify. More like the movement of a person pacing a room: back and forth, back and forth, with the energy of something that could not be still.

*Distress,* Agnes thought, and the word landed in her chest with a weight she hadn't expected.

She was still watching when she heard footsteps on the bluff road.

She turned. Gareth Croft was walking east along the road, hands in the pockets of his canvas jacket, at four in the morning, which should have surprised her and did not. Gareth walked when something was wrong in the Cove. She had watched him do it for thirty years, had seen him out here in November storms and February freezes and on the night in 2009 when three of the harbor boats had broken their moorings for no reason and drifted in slow circles until dawn. He walked the way a man walks when he is checking the perimeter of something he considers his.

He stopped when he saw her on the porch. He did not wave.

Agnes came down the porch steps and stood at the edge of the road. They were perhaps ten feet apart, and neither of them said anything for a moment. They both looked at the fog.

"You have a living guest," Gareth said.

Not a question.

"Yes," Agnes said.

The fog moved in its slow wrong direction below them. Gareth's face in the pre-dawn dark was unreadable, which was usually the case. He had a face built for conveying authority and not much else.

"Historically speaking," he said, "that's not something the Cove accommodates well."

"I'm aware."

He nodded, once, as though she'd confirmed something he'd already filed. His eyes stayed on the fog. "Young woman?"

Agnes said nothing.

"She'll have come about something," Gareth said. "They always come about something, the ones who find their way here. They're not tourists, the ones who find this place. You know that." He paused. "She'll have come about someone."

Agnes kept her face still. Sixty years of not letting Gareth Croft read her face.

"Some debts transfer with the deed, Agnes." He said it conversationally, the way he said everything, as though he were discussing the weather or the state of the road. "You knew that when you bought it."

The cold off the fog had found its way up the bluff. Agnes felt it at her wrists, at the back of her neck.

"I knew it," she said.

Gareth looked at her then, directly, and she held his gaze because she had been holding his gaze since 1994 and she was not going to stop now. Something moved in his expression — not softness, nothing so simple as that, but an acknowledgment. He had said what he came to say. The visit was complete.

He turned and walked back the way he'd come, east along the bluff road, and Agnes watched him until the dark took him.

She stood on the road for another minute. The fog rolled against the eastern shore of the harbor and rolled back. The wind came from the northwest and the fog moved southeast and the two facts sat next to each other like a sum that wouldn't balance.

She went back inside.

---

The east wing was quiet. Agnes walked the hall with the particular care of a woman who has spent decades learning which floorboards announce themselves, stepping over the third board from the staircase and the one outside Vera's room, her hand trailing along the wall. The sconces were off. The hall had the compressed stillness of a place where several people are asleep and she moved through it like water finding the gaps.

She stopped outside Room 4.

The door was closed. It was always closed. Agnes stood before it and listened.

Nothing. No sound from inside, no movement, no sign of anything. The hallway smelled of old wood and beeswax and the faint salt that lived in the bones of the building, and from Room 4 there was the additional smell Agnes had learned to identify over eleven years: ozone, faint but constant, as though something in the room was always about to happen.

She had her hand raised. Not quite to the door. Close enough to feel the air between her knuckles and the wood.

She had a living guest in Room 3. A woman who was looking for her brother. And in Room 4, behind this door, was — Agnes lowered her hand.

She stood there for a full minute. She counted it, because she had learned that the things she didn't do were worth marking, worth knowing the exact length of.

Sixty seconds.

Then she went to bed.

---

She slept. This surprised her. She had expected to lie awake until dawn running the same calculations, but her body made its own decision and she was under before she'd finished the thought, and when she woke, the room was gray with early light and something was wrong.

Agnes sat up.

Not smoke. Not gas. She checked both of these in the first two seconds because sixty years of running a house had made the check automatic, and it was neither. She was out of bed and standing before she'd decided to stand, her feet on the cold floor, her nose working.

Ozone.

Strong, unmistakable, the particular metallic sharpness she associated with her own ability — the smell that came after she held an anchor and asked, the smell that meant she'd pushed through to something and paid for it. She knew this smell the way she knew her own hands. She had not used her ability. She had not touched any anchor last night, had sat with Douglas's mug but not in the way that counted, had not asked and had not pushed.

The smell was coming from the east wing.

Agnes put on her cardigan and her slippers and went into the hall. The smell was stronger here. Not from Vera's room, not from any of the middle rooms. From the far end of the hall.

From Room 4.

She stood in the hallway and the ozone smell sat in her nose and she could feel, at the back of her skull, the ghost of a migraine that wasn't hers. As though whatever had happened in that room had cost something, and the cost was bleeding through the walls.

Room 4's door was closed. It was always closed.

Agnes stood in the hallway for a long moment. The inn was quiet around her, the particular pre-dawn quiet before the building woke itself up, before Douglas came into the kitchen and the other guests began their slow emergence into another day. The light through the east-wing window was the pale nothing-color of six a.m., and the smell of ozone was as clear as a bell.

She turned and went downstairs to start the coffee.

Her hands were steady. She made note of this. Her hands were steady and she was moving with purpose toward the kitchen and the ordinary morning, and the ozone smell faded as she descended, replaced by the cold-wood smell of the staircase and then the bread smell of the kitchen, the ghost of yesterday's baking still in the walls.

She filled the kettle. She set it on the burner. She stood with her hands flat on the counter and looked at the window above the sink, which showed her the garden and the fence and the pale sky beginning, very slowly, to consider the possibility of light.

Room 4 smelled of ozone without her. It had always smelled of ozone without her. She had noticed this eleven years ago and had filed it and had not examined the filing, because the filing led somewhere she had decided, eleven years ago, not to go.

Some debts transfer with the deed.

The kettle began its low moan toward boiling, and Agnes kept her hands flat on the counter and her eyes on the garden, and from upstairs — from the east wing, from the direction of Room 3 — there was no sound at all.

Carla Venn, sleeping the deep sleep of the kelp, would not be down until eight. Agnes had until eight. She had two hours and she would use them to think, to plan, to find some approach to the problem of a living woman looking for a lost brother that did not require Agnes to open a door she had been keeping closed for eleven years.

She would use the two hours.

She lifted her hands from the counter and reached for the coffee.
