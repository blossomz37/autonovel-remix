# Chapter Ten: Below the Fog Line

The fog was moving wrong again.

Agnes stood at the edge of the bluff road with her coat buttoned to the chin and watched it. Below her, the harbor basin held its usual gray-white mass, thick enough to lose your hand in if you reached down far enough — she had tested this once, in 1974, purely out of curiosity, and had not repeated the experiment. But the fog this morning was not pooling the way it should. It was drifting uphill in slow, deliberate tongues, pulling back before it reached the road's edge, then pressing forward again. Patient. Like something testing a fence.

She had noted this in the margin of the guestbook the morning after Carla died, when it had risen above the bluff road entirely. Now it was behaving like something between its usual self and that. In-between, she thought, and did not like the word.

She shifted her bag to her other shoulder — two thermoses of coffee, one for her, one for Bertrand — and started down the hill.

The fog took her at the forty-foot mark, right where it always did. The change was immediate and particular: the light softened, arriving from no specific direction, and the sounds of the upper town — a gull, the distant clank of her own garden gate in the wind — came back to her half a beat late, as if the air here processed things on a slight delay. She had always noticed this below the fog line. She had never entirely stopped noticing it, which meant it still unsettled her, which was probably healthy.

The dead moved freely through the market street at this hour. She passed the fish seller — a woman named Hedda who had died in 1987 and still kept her usual Tuesday pitch near the covered stalls — and nodded, and Hedda nodded back and said something to the living man on her right side in the same breath, the easy bilingual fluency of someone who had been doing it for decades. Two children ran across the cobblestones ahead of Agnes. One of them was wearing clothes from the wrong century; Agnes registered this without breaking stride. The harbor market had always been this way, below the fog line. The softened time made it easier. The dead were less effortful here, less translucent, more themselves. She had always appreciated the market for that, even when she found it difficult to look at.

She bought bread from the inland stall without stopping to talk. The baker, who was living and knew her, handed it over with a look at the thermoses and said, "Bertrand?" and she said yes, and he said, "Tell him the mackerel's been running," and she said she would.

The cannery district was quieter. It was always quieter — the living had little business here, and the dead who anchored to the old buildings tended toward solitude. Agnes had not come down here often in the thirty years since Bertrand had stopped accepting visitors from most people. He accepted her. She had never taken this for granted.

The cannery itself sat at the end of the district road, a long low building of corrugated iron and old timber, its paint so weathered it had become a color with no name. The smell reached her before the building did: rust, old salt, and underneath both of those something she had never successfully identified in sixty years of trying. Not rot. Something older than rot. Something preserved.

The side door was unlocked, as it always was.

Inside, the light came through the high industrial windows in slanted bars, catching the dust that lived permanently in the air of the place. The floor was original — wide boards, dark with decades of fish oil and salt water — and Agnes's footsteps on it were the only sound. She walked past the old processing lines, the equipment cold and still and furred with rust, and into the back room where Bertrand sat.

He was in his chair. He was always in his chair. It was a plain wooden thing, a foreman's chair, positioned at the exact angle it had occupied for forty-six years, facing the window that looked out over the harbor slips. He did not look up when she entered. He rarely did.

Agnes pulled the second stool from against the wall, set it across from him, and sat. She opened the first thermos and poured two cups, even though Bertrand couldn't drink his, because the ritual mattered. She put his cup on the crate beside his chair. He looked at it.

"Good coffee," he said.

"Same as always."

"Mm." He looked back at the window. The fog pressed against the glass outside, pale and close. "You came a long way for a Tuesday."

"I wanted to ask you about 1923."

A pause. Not surprise — Bertrand didn't do surprise — but a settling, the way a building settles when the temperature drops. He picked up his coffee cup and held it without drinking from it, and Agnes waited, and the cannery held its particular quiet around them.

"The survey man," he said, finally. "Yes. He came back every summer." Another pause. "Some things you see once and can't stop seeing."

"What did he see?"

Bertrand turned the cup in his hands. Outside, something moved in the fog — a shadow, boat-shaped, gone before Agnes could fix on it.

"There was a woman," he said. "1923. She came looking for someone, same as your living woman."

Agnes kept her hands around her thermos and her face still. "What happened to her?"

"She found what she was looking for."

The cannery ticked around them. Agnes could hear the harbor, muffled by fog, the low knock of a hull against a piling somewhere out in the gray.

"And then?"

"She didn't leave."

"She's still in the Cove?"

Bertrand was quiet for a moment. "Not the way you mean."

Agnes looked at her coffee. The surface of it was perfectly still, which meant her hands were steady, which was good, because the rest of her was not. "Is she a drifter?"

Bertrand set his cup down on the crate. He looked at the window. He said nothing.

Which was yes.

Agnes thought about the four names on her list. She had maintained that list for thirty years. She had been wrong, then, about at least one of them — wrong about how long the list had been necessary, wrong about the shape of the problem. A woman from 1923, untethered for a hundred years. A hundred years of cold drafts and low-tide smells and silence.

She drank her coffee. It was good. She had always made good coffee, which was a small thing and not nothing.

"I want to ask you about the floor," she said.

Bertrand looked at her then. His face was the face of a man who had been 71 for 46 years, which meant it held the particular stillness of someone who had stopped being surprised by questions and started simply deciding which ones to answer.

"What's under it," Agnes said.

He looked back at the window. "Things that need a roof."

"Is it the Croft arrangement?"

The pause this time was longer. Agnes counted it by the harbor sounds — three knocks of hull against piling, a gull somewhere above the fog line, the distant tick of the cannery's iron walls contracting in the morning cold.

"The Crofts," Bertrand said, slowly, "think they made a contract." He stopped. Started again. "What they made was a record. The contract was already there."

Agnes set down her thermos. "Who made it?"

"The Quiet Town dead." He said it the way you say the name of weather, not the name of people. "Before the 1743 settlement. They agreed to stay below the fog line."

She waited. He had more. She could tell by the way he was holding the arm of his chair, the slight forward tilt of his weight.

"In exchange for—" He stopped.

Agnes waited.

The fog pressed against the window. The harbor knocked. Somewhere in the floor beneath them, something old held its shape in the dark.

"In exchange for the inn staying open," Bertrand said.

Agnes heard it. She heard it the way you hear a step on a stair you thought was empty — with your whole body, before your mind has assembled the thought. She sat with it for a moment. Then she picked up her thermos, screwed the cap back on, and stood.

"I'll tell the baker about the mackerel," she said.

Bertrand nodded. He had already returned to the window.

She walked back through the cannery the way she had come, past the rusted processing lines, past the wide dark floor with its decades of salt and fish oil. She did not look down. She had never particularly looked down in here, and she understood now, with the new weight of what Bertrand had said settling into her, that this had been its own kind of knowledge — her body knowing to look elsewhere while her mind kept the question at a comfortable distance.

She came out the side door into the fog.

The walk back up the bluff road was longer than the walk down. It always was — the grade was the same in both directions, but going up required something going down didn't. She stopped once, halfway, to shift her bag again. The bread from the market was still warm through the cloth.

She came out of the fog at the forty-foot mark, and the light changed: harder, clearer, the morning sun doing what it could against the November sky. The sounds of the upper town arrived on time again, present-tense, no delay.

Agnes stopped walking.

She stood on the bluff road with the fog line at her back and looked at the inn.

The Bellweather sat at the top of the road as it always had, white clapboard, the porch running the full width of the front face, the east wing windows catching the morning light. She had repainted it four years ago. She would repaint it again in two years, if her knees cooperated, or she would hire someone, which she had been saying for twenty years. The garden gate was open — the latch needed fixing, had needed fixing since September, she kept meaning to ask Douglas to look at it and then not asking because Douglas had been dead for thirty-three years and she could fix a latch herself and the real reason she hadn't was that she liked having something small left undone.

She looked at the inn.

The inn staying open.

Sixty years she had run it. Sixty years of bread every morning and chowder every Friday and the east wing guest rooms kept fresh and the dining room fire lit from October through April. Sixty years of knowing, without examining why she knew, that she could not stop. Could not leave for more than a day. Could not take the vacation Douglas had suggested in 2003 when she'd driven to the county line and turned back, telling herself it was choice.

She had been telling herself it was choice.

She had been running the Bellweather for sixty years and she had never — not once, not in any of the careful, deliberate, non-digging way she had built her life — understood what she was doing. She had thought she was the innkeeper. She had thought she was keeping people fed and comfortable because she was good at it and because they needed it and because, underneath all of that, because it kept her from having to look at anything else.

She was not the innkeeper.

She was the terms of the contract.

Agnes stood at the fog line with the cold at her back and the inn in front of her, and she did not move for a long time. The morning went on around her — a gull crossed the sky, the garden gate swung in the wind, the fog pressed its slow patient weight against the back of her legs. The bread in her bag had gone cool.

She thought about the woman from 1923, who had come looking for someone and stayed a hundred years, untethered, unable to speak, leaving cold drafts in empty rooms. A drifter. A name on no list Agnes had kept.

She thought about Carla Venn, who had also come looking for someone.

She started walking toward the inn. The gate swung open as she reached it, which was the wind, and she went through it and up the porch steps and in through the front door, and the smell of the place closed around her — old wood, good bread from yesterday still in the air, the fire Douglas had kept going in the parlor — and she stood in the front hall and took off her coat and hung it on the hook she had hung it on for sixty years.

Douglas appeared in the kitchen doorway. He was holding his mug. The crack along its handle had spread another quarter inch overnight; she could see it from here.

"Ag," he said. "How was Bertrand?"

"Informative," Agnes said. She set her bag down on the hall table. She looked at her husband — his paint-stained work clothes, his boots she had never convinced him to change, the mug with its spreading crack. She looked at him for a moment longer than she usually allowed herself.

"I need to know," she said, "how much you know."

Douglas was quiet. He looked at his mug. Then he looked at her, and his face had the particular quality it got when he had been deciding something for a long time and had finally decided.

"Agnes," he said. "I need you to come sit down. I need you to not straighten anything while I talk."

She followed him into the kitchen. She sat at the table. She put her hands flat on the surface of it and felt the grain of the old wood under her palms and did not straighten anything.

The morning light came through the north-facing window and lay across the floor in a pale bar, and the fire in the parlor crackled through the open door, and somewhere in the east wing above them a door opened and closed softly, and Agnes waited for her husband to tell her what he knew.
