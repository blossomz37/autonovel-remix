# Chapter Seven: Deputy Vale Arrives

The knock came at eight-fourteen.

Agnes knew because she had been watching the clock above the dining room mantel, had watched the minute hand crawl from the twelve toward the three in the particular way it crawled when she wanted it to move faster, which was the same way it moved when she wanted it to stop. She had made coffee — not for herself, out of institutional reflex, the same reflex that had been running her hands through tasks all morning while her mind sat somewhere else entirely. The coffee was hot and smelled right and she had not drunk any of it.

She went to the front door.

The woman on the porch was younger than Agnes expected, though Agnes had stopped being surprised by how young official people were getting. Thirty-eight, perhaps. Brown hair braided back with the kind of efficiency that suggested she'd done it in a car mirror. The uniform fit her like a decision. She was looking at the inn the way Agnes had seen surveyors look at property lines — not admiring it, not judging it, reading it. Her gaze went to the porch boards, the window trim, the fog-dampened wood of the eaves, and came back to Agnes with something filed away behind it.

"Ms. Murrow." Not a question. "Deputy Nora Vale, Kettle Falls. You called in a death this morning."

"I did." Agnes stepped back from the door. "Come in."

Deputy Vale came in. She paused inside the threshold in a way that was not quite hesitation — she was taking the room, the entry, the smell of old wood and coffee and the faint persistent salt of the sea through every wall. Her hand went briefly to the notepad at her hip. Not opening it. Touching it.

Agnes gave her the abbreviated version in the entry hall, standing, because sitting would have suggested they had time. Guest arrived two nights ago, no reservation, paid in cash. Found her this morning at six-forty. No sign of forced entry. No other living guests in the inn.

Nora Vale listened without writing anything down. When Agnes finished, she said: "Walk me through the morning you found her. Not the summary — the actual morning."

Agnes looked at her.

"The whole morning," Nora said. "Start where you started."

So Agnes walked her through it. She was precise — the time she rose, the order of the kitchen tasks, the bread she had not put in the oven, the reason she had gone upstairs at all. She was truthful about the facts. She was silent about Vera at the end of the hall, about the smell that had been wrong since before she reached the door, about the way the fog had been sitting above the bluff road for the first time in her living memory and what she thought it meant. She described the open window, the chill in the room, the position of the body, the two notebooks on the desk.

Nora took notes without looking at her notepad.

Agnes noticed this the way she noticed when a dough was wrong before she could say why — a small wrongness, a competence she hadn't accounted for.

"The window," Nora said. "Second floor, north-facing. You said it was open."

"It was."

"You said the latch was intact."

"It was."

"So it opened from inside."

"Or it was never latched." Agnes kept her voice even, the way she kept a flame even under something delicate. "I don't know how it opened. I noticed it was open."

Nora wrote something. "The torn page. You mentioned a torn page from the second notebook."

"I noticed a page had been removed. The edge was still in the binding."

"Did you touch either notebook?"

"No."

Nora looked at her. Not suspiciously — Agnes had been looked at suspiciously before, and this wasn't it. This was assessment, the same reading she'd given the porch boards, the same filing-away. Then she wrote something.

Agnes had cooked for sixty years and could read a room the way most people read a face. She understood, standing in her own entry hall, that she was doing less well at this than she had expected to do.

---

Carla's room smelled of cold air and the particular staleness of a space where something had happened and the air hadn't been exchanged since. Agnes stood in the doorway. Nora moved through the room with a systematic quiet, touching nothing she hadn't photographed first, her phone out and doing the work of a camera with the brisk efficiency of someone who had done this enough times to stop thinking about the doing.

The two notebooks were still on the desk. Agnes watched Nora find them.

The first notebook — the facts one, Carla's documented observations, the handwriting Agnes had seen over Carla's shoulder at dinner two nights ago — Nora paged through it with gloved hands, her face doing nothing, and then bagged it. She looked at the second notebook. She opened it.

Agnes watched her look for the torn page.

The page was gone. Agnes had known it would be gone — had known it since she stood in the kitchen this morning with her hand flat on the counter. The torn edge was still there, a ragged inch of paper in the binding, but the page itself had not been in this room when Agnes found Carla, and it was not in this room now.

"You said you saw the torn page," Nora said.

"I saw the edge. I saw a page had been removed."

"Before or after you found the body."

"I noticed the notebook after. The edge was visible from where I was standing." Agnes kept her hands at her sides. Her fingers wanted the doorframe. She didn't let them have it. "I didn't go through the notebooks."

Nora bagged the second notebook. She stood for a moment looking at the north wall, the wall shared with the room next door. Her eyes moved along it in a way that had nothing to do with the wallpaper.

"You looked at the wall when I asked about your other guests," she said. "Downstairs. When you said there were no other living guests. You looked at that wall."

Agnes had not known she had done this.

"What's on the other side of that wall?" Nora asked.

The room was very cold. The window was closed now — Agnes had closed it herself before calling the county, because she was not going to leave a dead woman in a cold room for the hours it took a deputy to drive up from Kettle Falls — but the cold had stayed. It had settled into the plaster and the floorboards and the particular pocket of air near the desk, and it was not going anywhere.

"Room 4," Agnes said.

Nora wrote it down.

The fog pressed at the window glass, gray-white and close, and Agnes stood in the doorway of a dead woman's room and understood with the clarity of something long-postponed that she was not, in fact, managing this.

Nora looked up from her notepad. "I'll need to speak with your other guests."

"They're not available at the moment."

"Ms. Murrow." Nora's voice was not unkind. It had the particular patience of someone who had said a version of this many times. "I understand this is your home. I understand you have your own ways of handling things here. I'm not interested in making this harder than it needs to be. But a woman is dead in your inn and someone killed her, and that person is still somewhere." A pause. "I'll need to speak with everyone in the inn."

"I understand," Agnes said.

She did. She understood completely. She also understood that everyone in the inn was a category with specific complications that Nora Vale's notepad did not have a column for, and she stood with that knowledge the way she stood with many things — squarely, without visible distress, while it pressed against the backs of her eyes.

She went downstairs to make more coffee.

---

Nora was at the dining room table when Agnes came back with the pot. She had spread her initial notes across the table's end — not messy, organized in the specific way of someone accustomed to working in temporary spaces, making order out of available surface. The fireplace was going; Agnes had lit it before the deputy arrived, and the oak log she'd put on had caught properly and was putting out a steady heat. The room smelled of coffee and wood smoke and, faintly, the rosemary that had burned off the pan she'd left on the stove yesterday and never quite cleared.

Agnes set the mug down at Nora's right. She moved to take the pot back to the kitchen.

"She came here looking for her brother." Nora said it almost to herself, the way people said things they were still deciding what to do with. Her eyes were on her notes, not on Agnes. "That's in the notebook."

Agnes went very still.

The fire cracked. The fog moved against the dining room windows, slow and purposeful, and the room was warm and smelled of good coffee and Agnes stood with the pot in her hand and did not move for the length of a long breath.

"What was his name?" she said.

Nora looked up.

There was a moment — brief, specific — where the deputy's face did the thing faces did when something landed differently than expected. She had not anticipated the question. Agnes noted this, filed it, kept her own face doing nothing.

"Venn," Nora said. "Marcus Venn. You know him?"

Agnes looked at her. The fire put orange light across the table, across the notepad, across Nora's grandmother's ring on her right hand — Agnes noticed the ring, had noticed it at the door, had not known what to make of it and had set it aside. She looked at the deputy's face, which was waiting, which was patient, which was the face of someone who had learned to use silence as a tool.

"That's a question," Agnes said.

She went back to the kitchen.

The bread was still on the counter under its cloth, still rising, still doing what bread did. Agnes set the coffeepot on the stove and stood with her back to the door and listened to the sounds of the inn around her — the fire in the dining room, the creak of the east wing settling against the morning wind, the faint persistent sound of the fog against the north windows, which was not a sound exactly but a pressure, the way the air changed.

Marcus Venn.

She knew the name. She had known it for eleven years, in the way she knew many things she had filed in the drawer she did not open — she knew it the way she knew the shape of a crack in a wall she passed every day without looking at it directly. She had heard it once, a long time ago, in a context she had not examined since. She had heard it and she had put it away and she had made breakfast.

Marcus Venn.

She put her hand flat on the counter.

In the dining room, Nora Vale was writing up her initial notes, and the coffee Agnes had brought her was steaming, and the fire was going well, and a woman named Carla Venn had come to this inn looking for her brother and was dead in an upstairs room, and the torn page from her second notebook was not in the notebook and not in the room, and Room 4 was on the other side of a wall, and Agnes stood in her kitchen with her hand on the cold tile and understood, with the specific clarity of something she had been not-understanding for a very long time, that she needed to find Vera.
