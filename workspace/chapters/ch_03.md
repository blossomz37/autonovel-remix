# Chapter Three: A Guest Without a Reservation

The doorbell was a brass thing, original to the house, and it didn't ring so much as it spoke — a low, declarative *bong* that Agnes felt in the back of her teeth more than she heard with her ears. She stood at the kitchen counter with her hands in a bowl of bread dough and listened to the silence after it, trying to remember the last time she'd heard it from this direction.

Deliveries came to the back. Had come to the back since 1989, when she'd moved the welcome mat and stopped replacing the porch light bulb on the front side and generally made the back door the kind of door you used and the front door the kind of door you looked at. The dead didn't ring the bell. They came through the wall or the window or they were simply there, in the hallway, looking at her with the patient expression of people who had all the time there was.

The bell spoke again.

Agnes pulled her hands from the dough, leaving two deep prints in the pale mass, and wiped them on her apron. She took her time. She composed, as she crossed the dining room, a polite refusal: *I'm sorry, we're not taking guests at the moment. I can direct you to Wick Harbor — there's a fine inn on the waterfront there, very well reviewed.* She adjusted this twice before she reached the door. She had it ready.

She opened the door.

---

The woman on the porch was dark-haired and thin in the way that comes from forgetting to eat rather than trying to. She had a backpack on one shoulder and two notebooks tucked under her arm — not in the backpack, under her arm, held against her side like something she needed to reach quickly. She wore a jacket with four visible pockets and probably more that Agnes couldn't see. Her face was the color of old paper, and there were circles under her eyes deep enough to have their own weather.

She was looking at the inn with an expression Agnes recognized, though she hadn't seen it in thirty years. It was the expression of someone who had been looking for a specific thing for a long time and had found it in the wrong place at the wrong time and was going to ask for it anyway.

"I'd like a room," the woman said.

Agnes kept her hand on the doorframe. "We're closed to guests."

"I know." The woman shifted the notebooks to her other arm. "Your sign is gone, but the hook's still there. And your porch light's been out since at least — I don't know, I can't tell from the bulb. But I know you're closed."

"Then you know my answer."

The woman looked at her. Not past her, not at the inn over her shoulder — at her, with the direct, slightly unfocused attention of someone running on insufficient sleep and too much coffee. "I've been in the Cove for six hours," she said. "I keep seeing things at the edge of my vision. Not — I know what peripheral hallucinations look like. I've been tested. This isn't that." She paused. "I'd rather be somewhere with walls I can trust."

Agnes's hand tightened on the doorframe.

She looked at the woman on the porch. Dark hair, too many pockets, the pallor of someone who had driven a long way without stopping. Six hours. Agnes did the arithmetic without wanting to. The seventy-two-hour rule was not a rule anyone had written down or explained to her — it was something she had observed, over thirty-one years, the way a farmer observes weather patterns: empirically, unhappily, with the knowledge that observation doesn't change the outcome.

Six hours in the Cove and she was already seeing things.

Agnes looked past her, briefly, at the fog below the bluff road. It was doing its usual evening work, thickening at the edges, the harbor lights going soft and diffuse behind it. The temperature on the porch was dropping in the way it dropped when the light failed, the cold coming up from the water rather than down from the sky.

If she turned this woman away now, she would be alone in the Cove at night, in a rented room somewhere below the fog line, watching something move at the edge of her vision with no framework for what it was.

Agnes stepped back from the door.

"Room 3," she said. "Dinner's at seven. Don't touch the right towel hook — it pulls from the wall."

The woman let out a breath. "Thank you."

"Mm," Agnes said, and led her inside.

---

Room 3 was at the end of the east wing hallway, the last door before the window that looked out over the back garden. It was the only room without a permanent occupant, and Agnes kept it made up from habit, the bed tight and the curtains straight and a small lamp on the nightstand. It smelled of lavender and the cedar lining of the wardrobe and, very faintly, of the sea — which everything in the inn smelled of, after enough years.

Agnes opened the curtains while the woman set her backpack on the bed.

"Bathroom's across the hall," Agnes said. "The hot water takes a moment. The window sticks in the lower sash — push it left before you push it up." She turned from the curtains. "Left hook for the towels."

The woman had put both notebooks on the nightstand. She was standing in the middle of the room looking at the walls with the focused attention of someone taking measurements by eye.

"Thank you," she said again. "I'm Carla Venn."

"Agnes Murrow."

Carla Venn nodded, like this confirmed something. She unzipped the outer pocket of her backpack and took out a pen, uncapped it, and wrote something in the top notebook without opening it to a fresh page — she already had one ready, Agnes saw. She'd had one ready before she knocked.

Agnes watched this and kept her expression the same as it always was.

Carla looked up from the notebook. "Does your basement predate the 1743 survey?" she said. "That's — sorry, I should — I know that sounds like a strange question. But that's the question."

Agnes stopped moving.

She was by the door, one hand on the frame, and she stopped. The lamp on the nightstand put a warm circle on the wall. Somewhere down the hall, she could hear the particular silence of a house with guests in it — not empty, not full, occupied in the way the Bellweather was always occupied.

"How do you know about the survey?" Agnes said.

Carla had the pen poised over the notebook. "I've been researching the Cove. For about two years." She said it evenly, without apology. "The survey is — the 1743 survey is where the documented record starts, but the foundations here are older. I've been trying to establish how much older."

Agnes looked at her for a moment. The notebook. The pen. The two years.

"Dinner is at seven," Agnes said. "Don't be late — the chowder doesn't hold."

She left before Carla Venn could ask anything else.

---

The kitchen received her the way it always did: warm, low-lit, smelling of the morning's bread and the stock she'd had going since noon. Agnes went to the counter and put both hands flat on the edge of it, feeling the cold solidity of the tile, the slight roughness where the grout had worn. She stood there.

The stock needed skimming. The bread from this morning needed to come out of its cloth. She had onions to dice and potatoes to cube and the kelp to prepare, which required its own attention — the harbor kelp was particular, and she'd learned over decades that you had to treat it with a certain respect, a certain deliberateness, or the chowder came out wrong in ways she couldn't entirely explain.

She did not start any of this immediately.

She went to the small desk in the corner of the kitchen, the one with the guestbook on it. She opened the drawer and took out the pen she used for entries — not the good pen, not a casual pen, but the specific pen she'd used for this purpose since 1984 when the previous one had run dry mid-signature and she'd taken it as a sign to be more deliberate. She opened the guestbook to the current page.

She wrote: *Carla Venn.* And the date.

She looked at it.

The ink was dark. The handwriting pressed into the page — she could feel the indentation on the back of the sheet if she ran her thumb across it. Living guests always pressed harder. They held things with more weight, as if they knew, somewhere below conscious thought, that the marks they made needed to last.

Agnes looked at the name. She set the pen down parallel to the spine of the book. She turned back, not deliberately counting, just looking — the way you look at a shelf of familiar objects and register their arrangement without naming each one. The signatures went back through the decades. The dead guests' signatures were lighter, more even, the handwriting of people who'd had time to practice patience.

She closed the book.

She put the pen back in the drawer.

She went to the counter and began on the onions, because the onions needed doing and the chowder needed time and Carla Venn had been in the Cove for six hours and was already seeing things at the edges, and Agnes had made her choice at the front door and there was no useful purpose in standing in her kitchen second-guessing it. She diced with the efficiency of someone who had diced ten thousand onions in this same kitchen under this same light, her eyes dry, her hands steady.

The stock needed skimming. She skimmed it.

She went to the cold box for the kelp, which she kept on the lowest shelf in its damp cloth, and she unwrapped it on the board and looked at it for a moment before she began. It was dark, almost purple at the fronds, and it smelled of iron and salt and something faintly sweet she had never been able to name. She had been using it in her chowder since the mid-seventies, when old Bertrand Luce had brought her the first bundle from the harbor shallows and told her, in his slow, incomplete way, that it was what the families used. She had asked what families. He had paused for a long time and then said, *all of them,* and that had been the end of the conversation.

She chopped the kelp fine. She added it to the stock.

From the east parlor, she heard the particular sound of Vera settling into her chair — a small, precise sound, the sound of a woman arranging herself correctly. From the direction of the dining room came the low, almost subsonic presence of Douglas, who was probably standing near the sideboard with his coffee mug, waiting to talk to her. She could tell his mood by whether she could feel him from this distance. Tonight she could feel him clearly.

She did not go to the dining room. She would deal with Douglas at dinner.

She diced the potatoes. She put them in cold water to wait. She measured the cream by eye, as she always had, and set it near the stove to take the chill off. She worked through the preparations with the methodical attention she brought to everything in this kitchen, and she did not think about Carla Venn's question, and she did not think about the two notebooks on the nightstand in Room 3, and she did not think about the word *survey* spoken in a stranger's voice in a room that had been empty for thirty years.

She thought about the chowder. She thought about what time it was and whether the bread needed to come out now or could wait another ten minutes.

It could wait.

She stood at the stove and stirred the stock in a slow circle and listened to the inn around her — the settling of old wood, the faint sound of movement from Room 3 above her, the particular quality of silence from the east parlor where Vera sat, and from Room 2 where Hollis Pratt was probably reading, and from Room 4 where the guest who would not give his name was doing whatever it was he did in the long hours of the evening.

She added the potatoes to the stock.

She added the onions.

She waited for the smell to change, the raw sharp smell of the raw vegetables giving way to something softer, something that would reach every room in the inn and pull everyone to the table the way it had been pulling them for thirty years. The dead couldn't eat. But they could smell. And the smell of a proper chowder on a cold evening was, Agnes had found, the closest thing she had to a language everyone in this house understood.

The cream went in last. She watched it bloom white through the darker stock and stirred it once, gently, and turned the heat down.

Seven o'clock. She would need to call Carla Venn down.

She stood at the stove with the wooden spoon in her hand and looked at the kitchen she had cooked in for sixty-one years. The same counter. The same tiles, the grout worn in the same places. The same window over the sink showing the same dark garden and the same fog-softened lights of the harbor below the bluff.

She had cooked for a living nose before. Six times before. Harold Fitch, 1989, who had complimented the chowder and left a five-star review in the physical book and driven away in a rental car and never come back. Five others before him, each one a small, careful problem she had managed and resolved. She had managed them by feeding them well and keeping them comfortable and making sure they understood, without being told directly, that the Bellweather was a particular kind of place that asked for a particular kind of guest.

She had never managed one who arrived already asking about the 1743 survey.

The chowder was ready. Agnes tasted it from the wooden spoon, considered, added a pinch of salt. She put the spoon down on its rest and went to the bottom of the stairs.

"Miss Venn," she called up. "Seven o'clock."

A pause. Then footsteps, quick and purposeful, from the direction of Room 3.

Agnes went back to the kitchen and began ladling the chowder into the good bowls.

She set seven places at the dining room table, as she did every night. Eight chairs, seven places — the eighth chair at the end, the one with the slightly uneven leg, was hers, and she ate in the kitchen or not at all, depending on the night. She set the bread in the center of the table. She put the salt cellar on the left side, where it belonged, because Vera would count the seconds until she could say something about it if it was on the right.

She put a bowl at every place.

She stood back and looked at the table, which was set for seven and would hold six dead and one living, and she thought: *well.* And then she went to get the bread knife, because the bread needed slicing and the chowder was getting cold and dinner did not wait, not even for this.
