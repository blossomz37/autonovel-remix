# 1. THE CARTOGRAPHERS OF FORGETTING

**HOOK:** The mapmakers of the Mnemonic Coast don't chart land — they chart memory, and the protagonist has been commissioned to erase her own city from the world's collective mind before the conquering army can find it.

**WORLD:** The Mnemonic Coast of a secondary-world Southeast Asian archipelago, where the tidal flats smell of copper and low tide reveals the calcified thoughts of the dead — literal crystalline formations where fishermen harvest old ideas like abalone. Cities exist in two states simultaneously: physical and *remembered*. A city that no one remembers begins to physically dissolve, its stones going soft as damp bread, its people becoming translucent. The maps here are not paper but stretched membrane, warm to the touch, that pulse when you trace routes that still exist in living minds.

**MAGIC/COST:** Cartomancy — the ability to inscribe or erase things from collective memory by mapping or unmapping them. The cost is precise and brutal: every act of unmapping costs the cartographer a proportional personal memory. Erase a street, lose a childhood afternoon. Erase a city district, lose a relationship. The protagonist, Sao, must unmap an entire city of 40,000 souls — which will require her to forget *why she's doing it* long before she finishes.

**TENSION:** Personal: Sao's mother is still inside the city, and every district she erases from the world's memory brings her closer to forgetting her mother exists. Cosmic: the conquering force isn't an army of soldiers but an army of *rememberers* — professional mnemonists who can re-anchor things into reality — and if they fully remember the continent's suppressed colonial history, the indigenous peoples' own self-erasure (their survival strategy for generations) will unravel catastrophically.

**THEME:** Is cultural survival worth the cost of cultural amnesia? Can you protect something by forgetting it, and what remains when the protectors can no longer remember what they saved?

**WHY IT'S NOT GENERIC:** The antagonist force wins not through violence but through *accurate historical memory*, making the protagonist's heroism indistinguishable from complicity in erasure — there is no clean side.

# 2. ALL THE NAMES OF THE FLOOD

**HOOK:** Told entirely from the perspective of a river that has watched seventeen civilizations rise and drown in its basin, this is the story of the first human in four thousand years the river has chosen to *warn*.

**WORLD:** A wide, slow river in a world where water has genuine geological memory — not metaphorically but chemically. Dissolved minerals carry experiential data; the river *tastes* everything that has ever entered it. The basin civilizations build in cycles, always making the same architectural error: they build their libraries on the eastern bank, which floods every 300 years without fail. The river knows this. It has watched fourteen libraries dissolve. The current civilization's library contains the only existing copy of a proof that the river itself is conscious — written by a monk who drowned in it 200 years ago and whose thoughts the river can still taste, faint as old tea. The world is sensory: the smell of silt and iron, the sound of water moving over the specific stones of a particular bend, the weight of sediment carrying the crushed bones of forgotten peoples.

**MAGIC/COST:** The river's consciousness is vast and slow — it thinks in decades, perceives individual humans as brief bright sparks, and communicates only through what it carries: objects, bodies, the specific patterns of current. To warn the human, the young archivist Tem, it must *narrow its attention* to human timescale, which causes it genuine pain analogous to staring at the sun — and risks the river losing its long memory, the very thing that makes it capable of warning anyone.

**TENSION:** Personal: Tem is the archivist tasked with moving the library to a new building — on the eastern bank, as always, because the eastern bank has always been traditional. She's fighting her institution's inertia with a deadline she doesn't know is real. Cosmic: if the library floods, the proof of the river's consciousness drowns with it — and without that proof, the river cannot petition the basin's legal council for protected status before the upstream dam is built, which will kill it within a century.

**THEME:** What do we owe to kinds of consciousness that don't communicate in ways we recognize as communication?

**WHY IT'S NOT GENERIC:** The narrator is the setting itself, the "villain" is bureaucratic tradition rather than malice, and the climax is an archivist successfully completing a records transfer — the most undramatic heroism imaginable.
